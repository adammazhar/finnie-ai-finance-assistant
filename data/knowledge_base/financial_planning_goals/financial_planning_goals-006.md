A financial plan is not a document you write once and file away. Incomes change, families grow, goals shift, and markets move. A plan that fit perfectly five years ago may no longer match your life today. Reviewing and adjusting it regularly helps keep your money working toward what matters to you now.

## When to review your plan

Two kinds of triggers are useful:

**Scheduled check-ins.** Many people review their finances once or twice a year, for example at the start of the year or around tax time. A regular review catches gradual drift before it becomes a problem.

**Life events.** Major changes are a natural time to revisit the plan, including:

- Starting a new job, getting a raise, or losing a job
- Marriage, divorce, or a new partner
- The birth or adoption of a child
- Buying a home or moving
- Receiving an inheritance or other windfall
- Health changes or caring for a family member
- Approaching retirement

## What to revisit

**Goals and priorities.** Is each goal still important? Has its cost or timing changed? A new child might add an education goal; a career change might push retirement earlier or later.

**Budget and savings rate.** A raise could let you save more. A job loss might mean pausing some contributions and relying on your emergency fund. One helpful habit after a raise is to direct part of it to savings before your spending adjusts.

**Emergency fund.** A larger household, a mortgage, or a less stable income often calls for a bigger cash cushion.

**Asset allocation.** As a goal's date gets closer, its money may need to become more conservative. Market moves can also shift your mix. For example, a hypothetical portfolio that started at 60% stocks might drift to 70% after a strong year. **Rebalancing** means bringing a portfolio back to its intended mix, by selling some of what grew or directing new money to what lagged. Rebalancing tends to work best when done relatively infrequently, such as on a schedule or when an asset class drifts past a set threshold.

**Accounts and beneficiaries.** Changing jobs raises the question of what to do with your old workplace retirement plan. Marriage, divorce, or a new child may mean updating beneficiary designations, which often take precedence over a will.

**Insurance and protection.** New dependents or a new home may call for reviewing life, disability, or property coverage.

## An example of adjusting

Here is a hypothetical example with round numbers. Jamie saves $500 a month: $300 toward retirement and $200 toward a car. Then two changes happen:

- Jamie gets a $6,000 annual raise, about $500 a month before taxes.
- Jamie and a partner decide to start saving for a home in five years.

After reviewing, Jamie raises total savings to $800 a month: $350 for retirement, $200 for the car until it's paid for, and $250 for the new down payment goal. Once the car goal is met, its $200 shifts to the home fund. Nothing about the original plan was wrong; it simply needed updating.

## Avoid overreacting

Adjusting a plan isn't the same as reacting to every headline. Market drops are a normal part of investing and are usually not, by themselves, a reason to change long-term goals. Changes are generally best driven by shifts in *your* life, goals, or time horizon rather than short-term market noise.

## Rerun your projections

If you use projections such as Monte Carlo simulations, rerun them after a review. Updated contributions, balances, and timelines can change the picture noticeably, and seeing the new probability of success can guide your next step.

## Key takeaways
- Review your plan on a schedule and after major life events.
- Revisit goals, savings rate, emergency fund, allocation, and beneficiaries.
- Rebalancing brings a drifting portfolio back to its intended mix.
- Base changes on your life and goals, not short-term market moves.
- Update any projections after making changes.
