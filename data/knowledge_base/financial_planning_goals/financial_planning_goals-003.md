Education costs can be large, and they arrive on a fairly predictable schedule. That makes education one of the more plannable financial goals. With a long runway, even modest monthly amounts can grow meaningfully. This article covers the main ideas and the most common savings vehicle, the **529 plan**.

## Start with the time horizon

A child's age sets the clock. Saving for a newborn's college gives about 18 years, which is a long horizon. Saving for a teenager gives only a few years. The horizon affects both how much you need to save each month and how much investment risk the money can reasonably carry.

Here is a hypothetical example with round numbers. A family saves $250 a month from birth to age 18, and the account earns an assumed 5% average annual return. They contribute $54,000, and the account grows to roughly $87,000. Starting at age 10 instead, the same $250 a month would have far less time to compound. Actual returns will vary, and costs differ widely by school.

## What is a 529 plan?

A **529 plan**, legally called a qualified tuition plan, is a tax-advantaged savings plan for education, named after a section of the federal tax code. Plans are sponsored by states, state agencies, or educational institutions. The person who opens the account is the **account holder**, and the student is the **beneficiary**.

The main tax benefit: earnings grow without federal income tax, and withdrawals used for **qualified education expenses** are free of federal income tax and often state income tax too. Some states also offer a state tax deduction or credit for contributions.

There are two types:

- **Education savings plans:** investment accounts, often holding mutual funds or ETFs. Money can generally be used at colleges and universities, including some abroad, and at recognized credential programs. Within limits set by law, these plans can also cover some K-12 tuition and related expenses, registered apprenticeship costs, and a limited amount of student loan repayment. Check current IRS rules and your plan's documents for the details.
- **Prepaid tuition plans:** let you buy tuition credits at today's prices for use later at participating, usually in-state public schools. They are less flexible and often have residency requirements.

## Investment choices inside a 529

Education savings plans typically offer:

- **Age-based portfolios** (sometimes called target-date or enrollment portfolios) that start more growth-oriented and automatically shift toward bonds and cash as the student approaches college age.
- **Static portfolios** that keep the same mix of investments throughout.

Investments in education savings plans are not guaranteed by the state or federal government, and you can lose money. Fees also vary widely by plan and by whether you buy directly or through a broker, so comparing costs is worthwhile.

## What if the money isn't used for education?

If a withdrawal isn't used for qualified expenses, the earnings portion is generally subject to income tax plus an additional 10% federal tax. Options such as changing the beneficiary to another eligible family member can help avoid that. Rules have changed in recent years, so it helps to verify current details on IRS.gov or with the plan.

## Balancing education with other goals

Many families weigh education savings against retirement savings. A common consideration is that students have more ways to pay for education, such as scholarships, grants, work, and loans, than retirees have to pay for retirement. There's no single right balance, but it's worth thinking through before committing large amounts to one goal.

## Key takeaways
- A child's age sets the time horizon, which drives monthly savings and risk level.
- 529 plans offer tax-free growth and tax-free withdrawals for qualified education expenses.
- Education savings plans are invested and can lose value; prepaid plans lock in tuition credits.
- Non-qualified withdrawals can trigger income tax plus an extra 10% federal tax on earnings.
- Education goals are often balanced against retirement savings.
