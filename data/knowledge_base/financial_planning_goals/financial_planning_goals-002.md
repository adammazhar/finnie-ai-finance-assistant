For many people, buying a home is the largest purchase they will ever make, and the **down payment** is often the biggest hurdle. The down payment is the portion of the home price you pay upfront in cash; a mortgage loan covers the rest. Planning ahead makes the target less intimidating and helps avoid draining savings you may need later.

## How much down payment is typical?

According to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), in most cases you need at least **3%** of the home price, and many loan types and lenders require **5% or more**. You can often save money by putting down at least **10%**, and you save the most at **20% or more**. In general, a larger down payment means a smaller loan, which can lower your interest costs and monthly payment. Putting down less than 20% on a conventional loan typically means paying for private mortgage insurance.

Lenders often price loans in 5% steps, so there may be little benefit to putting down "almost" the next threshold, such as 8% instead of 10%.

Special programs for veterans, rural buyers, some first-time buyers, and others may offer low or no down payment options, though they usually come with other costs.

## It's more than the down payment

Home buyers also need cash for:

- **Closing costs:** fees to finalize the loan and purchase, which the CFPB notes typically range from **2% to 5%** of the purchase price, on top of the down payment.
- **Moving and setup costs:** movers, utility deposits, furniture, and immediate repairs.
- **An emergency cushion:** a common rule of thumb is three to six months of expenses, which you still want after buying the home.

## Building your target

Here is a hypothetical example with round numbers. Alex is targeting a $300,000 home in four years.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Down payment (10%) | $30,000 |
| Closing costs (about 3%) | $9,000 |
| Moving and setup | $3,000 |
| **Total savings target** | **$42,000** |

Alex already has an emergency fund set aside separately. Spread over 48 months, $42,000 works out to $875 a month. If that is too much, Alex could extend the timeline, aim for a less expensive home, or consider a smaller down payment and accept higher borrowing costs. Each is a trade-off, not a right or wrong answer.

## Where to keep down payment savings

Time horizon matters here. A down payment goal is usually a short- to medium-term goal, and the purchase date matters. If the money is invested in stocks and the market drops 25% the year before you plan to buy, you may have to delay the purchase or buy with less.

For that reason, down payment money is often kept in stable options such as:

- High-yield savings accounts
- Certificates of deposit (CDs) timed to mature near the purchase date
- Money market funds
- Short-term Treasury securities

Someone with a longer runway, such as eight to ten years, might accept more investment risk early and shift toward stability as the date approaches. The closer the goal, the less time there is to recover from a loss.

## Ways to build the fund

- **Automate** a monthly transfer into a dedicated account labeled for the goal.
- **Direct windfalls,** such as tax refunds or bonuses, toward the goal.
- **Review your budget** for spending you could redirect.
- **Talk to a HUD-certified housing counselor,** who may know of local down payment assistance programs.

## Keep the whole picture in mind

Money put into a home is hard to get back out quickly. Draining every account for a bigger down payment can leave you exposed to emergencies or behind on retirement savings. Balancing the down payment against other goals is part of a healthy plan.

## Key takeaways
- Down payments often range from 3% to 20% or more of the home price.
- Closing costs typically add 2% to 5%, plus moving and setup costs.
- Dividing the total target by months remaining gives a monthly savings goal.
- Short timelines usually call for stable, accessible savings rather than stocks.
- Keep an emergency fund intact after buying the home.
