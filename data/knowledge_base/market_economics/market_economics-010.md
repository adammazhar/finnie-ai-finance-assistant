The stock market includes thousands of companies doing very different things, from drilling for oil to selling toothpaste to writing software. To make sense of it all, investors group companies into **sectors** based on their main business. Sectors help people see where a portfolio's money is concentrated, compare similar companies, and understand why different parts of the market can move in different directions.

## The GICS framework

The most widely used system is the **Global Industry Classification Standard (GICS)**, developed by MSCI and S&P Dow Jones Indices. GICS has four levels, from broad to narrow: **sectors**, **industry groups**, **industries**, and **sub-industries**. Each company is assigned to one sub-industry based mainly on where it earns its revenue, which places it in exactly one sector.

The structure is reviewed and updated from time to time as the economy changes. For example, Real Estate became its own sector in 2016 after being part of Financials, and Communication Services was created in 2018 from the former Telecommunication Services sector plus some media and internet companies.

## The 11 sectors

1. **Energy:** oil, gas, and coal producers, and companies providing equipment and services to them.
2. **Materials:** chemicals, metals and mining, construction materials, and packaging.
3. **Industrials:** aerospace and defense, machinery, construction and engineering, airlines, railroads, and business services.
4. **Consumer Discretionary:** goods and services people can put off buying, such as cars, homebuilding, restaurants, hotels, apparel, and many retailers.
5. **Consumer Staples:** everyday necessities such as food, beverages, household products, and grocery stores.
6. **Health Care:** pharmaceuticals, biotechnology, medical devices, and health care providers and services.
7. **Financials:** banks, insurance companies, investment firms, and payment and lending businesses.
8. **Information Technology:** software, IT services, computers and hardware, and semiconductors.
9. **Communication Services:** telecom providers, media and entertainment, and interactive online platforms.
10. **Utilities:** electric, gas, and water utilities, and independent power producers.
11. **Real Estate:** real estate investment trusts (REITs) and real estate management and development companies.

A company's popular image does not always match its sector. Some large internet companies are classified in Communication Services or Consumer Discretionary rather than Information Technology.

## Cyclical vs. defensive sectors

Sectors respond differently to the business cycle:

- **Cyclical sectors** tend to rise and fall with the economy. Consumer Discretionary, Industrials, Materials, and Financials often fall into this group. When people feel confident, they buy new cars and travel; when times are tight, they cut back.
- **Defensive sectors** tend to be steadier, because demand for their products holds up even in downturns. Consumer Staples, Utilities, and Health Care are commonly described this way. People keep buying groceries, paying electric bills, and filling prescriptions.
- **Rate-sensitive sectors,** such as Utilities and Real Estate, are often affected by interest rate changes because they borrow heavily and are valued partly for their income.

These are tendencies, not rules, and any sector can surprise.

## Why sectors matter to investors

**Understanding diversification.** A broad index like the S&P 500 holds all 11 sectors, but not in equal amounts. Its weights follow company size, so a few sectors can make up a large share. Here is a hypothetical example: if one sector made up 30% of an index and fell 20%, that sector alone would pull the index down about 6%.

**Spotting concentration.** Someone who owns several funds or individual stocks may discover much of their money is in one or two sectors, which can increase risk.

**Reading the news.** Headlines often explain market moves by sector, such as energy stocks rising with oil prices or bank stocks reacting to interest rates.

Sector leadership rotates over time. The best-performing sector in one year has often lagged in another, and predicting which will lead next is very difficult. Sector funds let investors focus on one area, but that concentration adds risk compared with owning the broad market.

## Key takeaways
- Sectors group companies by their main business, helping investors understand and compare parts of the market.
- GICS, from MSCI and S&P Dow Jones Indices, sorts companies into 11 sectors with more detailed levels beneath.
- The 11 sectors range from Energy and Materials to Information Technology, Utilities, and Real Estate.
- Cyclical sectors tend to move with the economy, while defensive sectors are usually steadier.
- Sector weights in broad indexes are uneven, so checking sector exposure helps reveal concentration risk.
