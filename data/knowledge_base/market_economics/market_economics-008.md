The economy does not grow at a steady pace. It speeds up, slows down, occasionally shrinks, and then recovers. This recurring pattern of ups and downs is called the **business cycle**. Understanding it helps explain why jobs, profits, interest rates, and investment returns tend to shift over time, and why recessions, though painful, are a regular part of economic life.

## The four phases

The business cycle is usually described in four phases:

1. **Expansion:** The economy grows. Employment rises, consumers spend more, businesses invest, and company profits tend to increase.
2. **Peak:** The high point of activity before a downturn begins. Near a peak, inflation pressures may build and the Federal Reserve may be raising interest rates.
3. **Contraction (recession):** Economic activity declines. Spending falls, businesses cut back, and unemployment rises.
4. **Trough:** The low point. After the trough, a new expansion begins.

Cycles are not regular like seasons. Expansions and contractions vary widely in length and severity, and no one can reliably predict when the next turn will come.

## What counts as a recession?

A popular rule of thumb says a recession is two consecutive quarters of falling real (inflation-adjusted) GDP. That is not the official U.S. definition.

In the United States, recession dates are determined by the **Business Cycle Dating Committee** of the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), a private, nonprofit research organization. The NBER describes a recession as a significant decline in economic activity that is spread across the economy and lasts more than a few months. It looks at three criteria, often summarized as **depth, diffusion, and duration**, and examines a range of monthly data such as employment, real personal income, consumer spending, and industrial production.

Because the committee waits for enough data to be confident, it usually announces the start or end of a recession many months after the fact. By the time a recession is officially declared, it may already be over.

## How long do cycles last?

According to NBER data, U.S. recessions since World War II have averaged around 10 months, while expansions have averaged a little over five years. The range is wide:

- The 2007-2009 recession, often called the Great Recession, lasted about 18 months.
- The 2020 recession, triggered by the pandemic, lasted only about two months, the shortest on record, but the drop in activity was extremely steep.
- Some expansions have lasted around a decade.

## What causes recessions?

There is no single cause. Common triggers include:

- **Tighter monetary policy:** Higher interest rates meant to fight inflation can slow borrowing and spending.
- **Financial crises:** Problems in banks or credit markets can cut off lending, as in 2008.
- **Shocks:** Sudden events such as oil price spikes or a pandemic can disrupt activity.
- **Burst bubbles:** When asset prices fall after a speculative boom, households and businesses may cut back.

## The business cycle and investing

Financial markets and the economy are connected, but they do not move in lockstep:

- **Stocks often look ahead.** Stock prices have frequently started falling before a recession begins and started recovering before it ends, because investors trade on expectations about future profits.
- **Bonds often respond to rate expectations.** High-quality bonds have often gained value during recessions as interest rates fell, though this pattern has not held every time.
- **Sectors behave differently.** **Cyclical** businesses, such as automakers, airlines, and luxury goods, tend to be more sensitive to the cycle. **Defensive** businesses, such as utilities, groceries, and health care, tend to hold up relatively better.

Here is a hypothetical example. An investor who sold all stocks as soon as a recession was officially announced might find that the market had already fallen and begun to recover by then. Because turning points are hard to identify in real time, many investors focus on diversification and their long-term plan rather than on predicting the cycle.

## Key takeaways
- The business cycle moves through expansion, peak, contraction, and trough, with irregular timing.
- In the U.S., the NBER dates recessions, defining them as significant, widespread declines lasting more than a few months.
- The "two negative quarters of GDP" rule is a popular shortcut, not the official definition.
- Since World War II, recessions have been much shorter on average than expansions.
- Stock markets often move ahead of the economy, making recession timing a difficult guide for investors.
