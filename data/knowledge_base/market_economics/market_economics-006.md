**Inflation** is the general rise in prices across the economy over time. When inflation is running, each dollar buys a little less than it did before. That loss of **purchasing power** is one of the most important, and most overlooked, forces affecting savings and investments.

## How inflation is measured

In the U.S., the best-known gauge is the **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**, published monthly by the Bureau of Labor Statistics. It tracks the average change in prices paid by urban consumers for a broad basket of goods and services, such as food, housing, transportation, and medical care. Another measure, the **Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index**, is the one the Federal Reserve uses for its 2% longer-run inflation goal.

You will often hear about **core inflation**, which excludes food and energy prices. Those prices swing a lot, so leaving them out can make the underlying trend easier to see.

## What causes inflation

Economists usually describe a few main sources:

- **Demand-pull inflation:** Demand for goods and services grows faster than the economy can produce them. Too much money chasing too few goods pushes prices up. Strong consumer spending, government stimulus, or easy credit can contribute.
- **Cost-push inflation:** Costs of production rise, such as for energy, raw materials, or wages, and businesses pass those costs along. Supply disruptions can have this effect.
- **Built-in inflation (expectations):** If people expect prices to keep rising, workers ask for higher wages and businesses raise prices in anticipation, helping keep inflation going.

Growth in the money supply also plays a role over long periods. The Federal Reserve tries to keep inflation low and stable mainly by adjusting interest rates.

## Why a little inflation is considered normal

Mild, predictable inflation is generally seen as consistent with a healthy economy. The Fed aims for about 2% over time. **Deflation**, a general fall in prices, can be harmful, because people may delay purchases and debts become harder to repay. High or unpredictable inflation is also damaging, because it makes planning difficult and erodes savings quickly.

## The math of purchasing power

Here is a hypothetical example. If prices rise 3% a year, something that costs $100 today would cost about $134 in 10 years and about $181 in 20 years. Put another way, $100 kept in cash would buy only about three-quarters as much after 10 years.

This leads to an important idea: the **real return**. A real return is your return after subtracting inflation. If a savings account pays 2% and inflation is 3%, your real return is roughly -1%, even though your balance is growing.

## How inflation affects different investments

- **Cash and savings accounts:** Safe from market swings, but interest rates may not keep up with inflation, especially over long periods.
- **Traditional bonds:** Fixed interest payments lose purchasing power as prices rise. Unexpected inflation also tends to push interest rates up, which lowers the market value of existing bonds. Longer-term bonds are usually hit harder.
- **Inflation-protected bonds:** Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) and I bonds adjust with the CPI and are designed to help preserve purchasing power.
- **Stocks:** Over long periods, stocks have historically outpaced inflation, partly because companies can raise prices and grow earnings. In the short run, however, high or rising inflation has often been rough for stocks, especially when it leads to higher interest rates and squeezes profit margins.
- **Real assets:** Real estate and commodities have sometimes held up during inflationary periods, though results vary widely and these assets carry their own risks.

No single investment protects perfectly against inflation in every period, which is one reason many investors diversify across several types of assets.

## Key takeaways
- Inflation is a general rise in prices that reduces the purchasing power of money over time.
- The CPI, published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics, is the most widely quoted U.S. inflation measure.
- Inflation can come from strong demand, rising costs, and expectations of future price increases.
- Real return, meaning return minus inflation, shows whether savings are actually gaining purchasing power.
- Cash and fixed-rate bonds are especially vulnerable to inflation; stocks have historically outpaced it over long periods.
