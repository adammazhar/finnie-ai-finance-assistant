Financial news is full of animal talk. A **bull market** is a period when prices are rising broadly and investors are generally optimistic. A **bear market** is a period of significant, sustained price declines and widespread pessimism. One common way to remember the terms: a bull thrusts its horns up, while a bear swipes its paws down.

## The 20% rule of thumb

There is no official referee who declares bull or bear markets, but a widely used convention is:

- **Bear market:** a decline of 20% or more from a recent peak.
- **Bull market:** a rise of 20% or more from a recent low, usually lasting a sustained period.
- **Correction:** a smaller drop of at least 10% but less than 20% from a recent peak.

These labels are usually applied to a broad index such as the S&P 500, though they can describe individual stocks, sectors, or other asset classes too. They are also usually applied in hindsight. In the moment, no one knows whether a 12% drop will stop there or become a 30% decline.

Here is a hypothetical example. An index peaks at 1,000. If it falls to 900, it is down 10%, which would be called a correction. If it keeps falling to 800 or below, it has entered bear market territory. If it later climbs from 800 back to 960, that 20% gain from the low would often be described as a new bull market, even though the index is still below its old peak.

## What tends to happen in each

**Bull markets** often accompany a growing economy, rising company profits, low or stable unemployment, and investor confidence. As prices climb, optimism can feed on itself, and sometimes prices run ahead of what the underlying businesses can justify.

**Bear markets** often coincide with, or come before, economic slowdowns or recessions, falling profits, or financial shocks. Fear can also feed on itself, pushing prices below what the fundamentals might suggest. Not every bear market comes with a recession, and not every recession comes with a bear market, but the two are often linked.

## How long do they last?

Historically, U.S. bull markets have tended to last considerably longer than bear markets, and their gains have more than made up for the losses of bear markets over long periods. Bear markets, while painful, have generally been shorter. That said, averages hide wide variation: some bear markets have ended within months, while others have lasted years, and the time needed to recover to a previous peak has ranged from a few months to many years.

Past patterns are no promise about the future, and nobody can reliably predict when one phase will end and the next begin.

## Why these cycles matter for investors

Understanding bull and bear markets helps set realistic expectations:

- **Declines are a normal part of investing.** Over a lifetime of investing, a person is likely to experience several bear markets.
- **Timing is hard.** Some of the strongest single days in market history have happened during or shortly after bear markets. Investors who sell after large drops risk missing the rebound.
- **Time horizon matters.** Money needed within a few years is more exposed to a badly timed bear market than money meant for goals decades away.
- **Emotions run high.** Excitement in bull markets and fear in bear markets can both lead to decisions that look poor in hindsight. Having a plan in advance, including an asset mix that fits one's comfort with risk, can make volatility easier to live with.

## Other market labels

You may also hear about a **bear market rally**, a temporary rise within a longer decline, and a **secular** bull or bear market, meaning a long-term trend lasting many years that may contain shorter moves in the other direction.

## Key takeaways
- A bull market is a sustained period of rising prices; a bear market is a decline of 20% or more from a peak.
- A correction is a drop of 10% to less than 20%, and these labels are usually applied in hindsight.
- Historically, bull markets have tended to last longer than bear markets, though individual cycles vary widely.
- Bear markets are a normal part of investing, and predicting their start or end is very difficult.
- Time horizon and a plan made in advance help investors handle both phases.
