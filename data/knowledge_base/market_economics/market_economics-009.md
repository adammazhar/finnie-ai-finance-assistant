Interest rates are sometimes described as the "price of money." They determine how much it costs to borrow and how much savers earn by lending. Because nearly every investment competes with the option of simply earning interest, changes in rates ripple through both the bond and stock markets. The effects on bonds are fairly mechanical; the effects on stocks are real but less predictable.

## Where rates come from

Short-term rates are heavily influenced by the Federal Reserve's target for the federal funds rate. Longer-term rates, such as the 10-year Treasury yield, are set in the market and reflect expectations for inflation, economic growth, future Fed policy, and the extra return investors demand for tying up money longer. Short- and long-term rates usually move in the same direction, but not always by the same amount.

## The effect on bonds

For bonds, the relationship is direct: when market interest rates rise, the prices of existing fixed-rate bonds fall, and when rates fall, their prices rise. An existing bond with a lower coupon becomes less attractive than new bonds paying more, so its price drops until its yield is competitive.

How much a bond's price moves depends mostly on its **duration**, a measure of rate sensitivity. Here is a hypothetical example. If rates rise by 1 percentage point, a short-term bond fund with a duration of 2 years might fall about 2%, while a long-term fund with a duration of 15 years might fall about 15%.

There is an upside to rising rates for bond investors: new bonds and reinvested interest earn more. Over time, that higher income can offset earlier price losses, especially for those with longer time horizons.

## The effect on stocks

Rates influence stocks through several channels:

- **Borrowing costs:** Higher rates make it more expensive for companies to borrow for expansion and for consumers to finance homes, cars, and purchases. That can slow sales and reduce profits.
- **Valuation:** A stock's value reflects the profits a company is expected to earn in the future. Investors translate those future profits into today's dollars using a **discount rate** that tends to rise with interest rates. A higher discount rate makes future profits worth less today, which can lower stock prices even if the profits themselves do not change.
- **Competition from safer assets:** When bonds and savings accounts pay more, some investors are less willing to accept the risks of stocks without a lower price. When rates are very low, stocks can look more attractive by comparison.

Here is a hypothetical illustration of the competition effect. If a safe Treasury pays 1%, a stock with an expected 4% earnings yield (profits divided by price) may look appealing. If the Treasury pays 5%, investors may demand a lower stock price, and therefore a higher earnings yield, before taking on the extra risk.

## Not all stocks react the same way

- **Growth stocks,** whose profits are expected mostly far in the future, have often been more sensitive to rising rates, because distant profits are discounted more heavily.
- **Rate-sensitive sectors** such as real estate, utilities, and homebuilders are affected by borrowing costs and by competition with bond yields for income-seeking investors.
- **Banks** can sometimes benefit from higher rates if they earn more on loans than they pay on deposits, though the picture is complicated.

## Why the relationship is not simple

Stocks do not automatically fall when rates rise. The reason rates are moving matters. Rates sometimes rise because the economy is strong, which can support profits and offset the drag. Rates may fall because the economy is weakening, which can hurt stocks despite cheaper borrowing. Markets also react to rate changes relative to expectations; a widely expected Fed move may already be reflected in prices.

Stocks and high-quality bonds have often moved in different directions, which is one reason they are combined in portfolios. But there have been periods, such as 2022, when rapidly rising rates and high inflation led to losses in both at the same time.

## Key takeaways
- When interest rates rise, existing bond prices fall; longer-duration bonds fall more.
- Higher rates can weigh on stocks by raising borrowing costs, lowering the present value of future profits, and making safer assets more attractive.
- Growth stocks and rate-sensitive sectors have often reacted more strongly to rate changes.
- Why rates are changing, and whether it was expected, matters as much as the change itself.
- Stocks and bonds often move differently, but both can fall together when rates rise quickly.
