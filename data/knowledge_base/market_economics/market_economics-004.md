Stock prices do not rise in a straight line. Every so often they fall sharply, sometimes over months and sometimes in a single day. Looking back at major declines helps put today's headlines in context. It shows that large drops have happened many times, that their causes vary, and that markets have historically recovered, though sometimes only after a long wait.

## Corrections vs. crashes

- A **correction** is commonly defined as a decline of at least 10% but less than 20% from a recent peak. Corrections have been fairly frequent in U.S. market history, often occurring every year or two, and many recover within months.
- A **bear market** is a decline of 20% or more.
- A **crash** has no formal definition. The word usually describes a sudden, severe drop over a day or a few days, often driven by panic.

## Notable episodes

The figures below are approximate and describe well-documented events.

**The 1929 crash and the Great Depression.** After a speculative boom in the 1920s, stocks plunged in October 1929. The decline continued until 1932, and the Dow lost close to 90% of its value from its 1929 peak. It took until the 1950s for the Dow to regain that peak, not counting dividends. The crash and depression led to major reforms, including the creation of the Securities and Exchange Commission.

**Black Monday, 1987.** On October 19, 1987, the Dow fell about 22.6% in a single day, still its largest one-day percentage drop. Automated selling strategies and panic contributed. Unlike 1929, the economy did not fall into a depression, and the market recovered its losses within about two years. The event led to **circuit breakers**, rules that pause trading when the market falls very quickly. Today, market-wide halts can be triggered by S&P 500 declines of 7%, 13%, and 20% in a single day.

**The dot-com bust, 2000-2002.** Excitement about the internet pushed technology stocks to extreme valuations in the late 1990s, with many companies having little or no profit. When the bubble burst in 2000, the tech-heavy Nasdaq Composite lost roughly three-quarters of its value over the next two-plus years, and it took about 15 years to regain its 2000 peak.

**The 2008 financial crisis.** Losses on risky mortgages spread through the global banking system, leading to the failure or rescue of major financial firms. From its 2007 peak to its early 2009 low, the S&P 500 fell by more than half. The crisis came with a deep recession and prompted new financial regulations.

**The 2020 COVID-19 crash.** As the pandemic spread, the S&P 500 fell about a third in roughly a month in early 2020, one of the fastest declines on record. Circuit breakers were triggered several times. Supported by aggressive government and central bank responses, the market regained its prior peak within about six months, a remarkably quick recovery by historical standards.

## Common threads

Although each episode had its own trigger, some patterns repeat:

- **Speculation and excess:** Prices that run far ahead of fundamentals, as in 1929 and 2000, have often been followed by sharp declines.
- **Leverage and financial stress:** Heavy borrowing can magnify losses, as in 1929 and 2008.
- **Sudden shocks:** Unexpected events, such as a pandemic, can cause rapid repricing.
- **Panic:** Fear-driven selling can push prices below what the fundamentals suggest.

## Lessons investors often draw

- **Declines are normal.** Anyone investing for decades will likely live through several corrections and bear markets.
- **Recovery times vary widely.** Some drops reversed within months; others took more than a decade to recover in price terms. Diversification across asset types and regions has helped some investors avoid being tied to a single market's worst stretch.
- **Timing is difficult.** Some of the market's best days have come close to its worst days, so investors who sold in a panic often missed part of the rebound.
- **Time horizon matters.** Money needed soon is more exposed to a badly timed decline than money meant for goals many years away.

History does not repeat exactly, and past recoveries are no promise about future ones.

## Key takeaways
- A correction is a 10% to under 20% drop; a bear market is 20% or more; a crash is a sudden, severe fall.
- Major episodes include 1929, Black Monday in 1987, the dot-com bust, the 2008 crisis, and the 2020 pandemic crash.
- Causes often include speculation, excessive borrowing, sudden shocks, and panic selling.
- Circuit breakers now pause trading after very large single-day drops in the S&P 500.
- Markets have historically recovered from major declines, but recovery times have ranged from months to many years.
