Every week brings a stream of economic reports: jobs numbers, inflation figures, retail sales, and more. Markets sometimes move sharply when these numbers come out. **Economic indicators** are statistics that describe how the economy is doing, and investors watch them for clues about growth, inflation, interest rates, and corporate profits. Knowing what the main indicators measure makes financial news much easier to interpret.

## Leading, coincident, and lagging

Indicators are often grouped by timing:

- **Leading indicators** tend to change before the broader economy does, such as building permits, new orders for manufactured goods, and the stock market itself.
- **Coincident indicators** move along with the economy, such as employment, personal income, and industrial production.
- **Lagging indicators** change after the economy has already shifted, such as the unemployment rate, which often keeps rising for a while after a recession ends.

No indicator is perfect. Each can give false signals, and first releases are often revised later.

## Gross domestic product (GDP)

**GDP** measures the total value of goods and services produced in the U.S. It is the broadest gauge of economic activity. The Bureau of Economic Analysis releases it quarterly, with several estimates that are refined as more data arrives. Headlines usually cite **real GDP**, which is adjusted for inflation, expressed as an annualized growth rate. Rising real GDP means the economy is expanding; falling real GDP means it is contracting.

## Jobs reports

The Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes the **Employment Situation** report, usually on the first Friday of each month. It includes:

- **Nonfarm payrolls:** the number of jobs added or lost, based on a survey of employers.
- **Unemployment rate:** the share of people in the labor force who are without work and looking for it, based on a survey of households.
- **Average hourly earnings:** a measure of wage growth.

Weekly **initial jobless claims**, from the Department of Labor, offer a more frequent read on layoffs.

## Inflation measures

- **Consumer Price Index (CPI):** monthly changes in prices paid by consumers, from the Bureau of Labor Statistics.
- **Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index:** from the Bureau of Economic Analysis, the measure the Federal Reserve uses for its inflation goal.
- **Producer Price Index (PPI):** prices received by producers, which can hint at future consumer price changes.

Inflation data matters to investors largely because of how it may influence Federal Reserve interest rate decisions.

## Spending, business, and sentiment

- **Retail sales:** monthly spending at stores and online, a window into consumer demand, which drives a large share of the U.S. economy.
- **Purchasing managers' surveys:** monthly business surveys where readings above 50 generally signal expansion and below 50 signal contraction.
- **Consumer confidence and sentiment surveys:** how households feel about their finances and the economy.
- **Housing data:** housing starts, building permits, and home sales, which are sensitive to interest rates.

## The yield curve

The **yield curve** compares interest rates on Treasuries of different maturities. Normally, longer-term bonds yield more than short-term ones. When short-term yields rise above long-term yields, the curve is said to be **inverted**. Historically, inversions have come before many U.S. recessions, but the timing has varied and the signal has not been perfect.

## How markets react

Markets respond less to whether a number is "good" or "bad" than to how it compares with expectations. Here is a hypothetical example. If economists expect 150,000 new jobs and the report shows 300,000, stocks might rise on signs of strength, or they might fall if investors worry the strong economy will keep the Fed from cutting rates. The same data can be read in different ways depending on what markets are most concerned about at the time.

For long-term investors, individual reports are usually less important than broad trends over many months.

## Key takeaways
- Economic indicators are statistics that describe the economy's health and help investors gauge growth and inflation.
- Indicators can be leading, coincident, or lagging, and early estimates are often revised.
- Key reports include GDP, the monthly jobs report, CPI and PCE inflation, retail sales, and business surveys.
- An inverted yield curve has historically preceded many recessions, though not with reliable timing.
- Markets react mainly to surprises relative to expectations, not to the numbers alone.
