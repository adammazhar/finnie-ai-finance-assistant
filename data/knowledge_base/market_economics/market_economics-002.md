When a news anchor says "the market was up today," they are almost always talking about a **stock market index**. An index tracks the combined performance of a group of stocks, giving a quick snapshot of how that slice of the market is doing. The three U.S. indexes you will hear about most are the S&P 500, the Dow Jones Industrial Average, and the Nasdaq Composite. They often move in the same direction, but they measure different things in different ways.

## Why indexes exist

Thousands of stocks trade every day. An index summarizes many of them into one number so investors can see broad trends, compare the performance of their own investments against a **benchmark**, and build **index funds** that aim to match the index's return at low cost. You cannot invest directly in an index itself, but many mutual funds and ETFs are designed to track them.

## How weighting works

The biggest difference between indexes is how they **weight** each stock, meaning how much influence each company has.

- **Market-capitalization weighting:** Larger companies (by total market value) count more. A company worth ten times as much as another has about ten times the influence.
- **Price weighting:** Stocks with higher share prices count more, regardless of the company's overall size.

## The S&P 500

The S&P 500 includes 500 leading U.S. companies and covers roughly 80% of the available value of the U.S. stock market, according to its provider. It is widely regarded as the best single gauge of large U.S. companies and is the benchmark many professionals use.

It is weighted by market capitalization, adjusted to count only shares available to the public (called **float adjustment**). A committee chooses members based on criteria such as size, profitability, and trading activity. Because of its weighting, the largest companies have a big effect on the index's daily moves.

## The Dow Jones Industrial Average

The Dow, created in the late 1800s, is one of the oldest and most famous indexes. It tracks just 30 large, well-known U.S. companies, often called **blue-chip** stocks, selected by a committee.

The Dow is **price-weighted**, which is unusual. Here is a hypothetical example: if Stock A trades at $400 and Stock B at $40, a 10% rise in Stock A ($40 per share) moves the Dow about ten times as much as a 10% rise in Stock B ($4 per share), even if Stock B is a much larger company. Because of this quirk and its small size, many analysts view the Dow as a less complete picture of the market than the S&P 500, though its long history keeps it popular.

When the news reports that the Dow "rose 300 points," that point change depends on the index's level. Percentage changes are usually more meaningful for comparing days or indexes.

## The Nasdaq Composite

The Nasdaq Composite includes thousands of companies listed on the Nasdaq stock exchange, from giants to small firms. It is weighted by market capitalization. Because many large technology companies list on Nasdaq, the index leans heavily toward the technology sector, and it has historically tended to swing more than the S&P 500.

Don't confuse it with the **Nasdaq-100**, a separate index of roughly 100 of the largest non-financial companies on Nasdaq.

## Other indexes worth knowing

- **Russell 2000:** tracks smaller U.S. companies (small caps).
- **Total market indexes:** aim to include nearly every publicly traded U.S. company.
- **International indexes:** track stocks in developed and emerging markets outside the U.S.
- **Bond indexes:** track broad groups of bonds.

## Limits of indexes

An index shows how a group of stocks performed, not how any individual investor did. Headline indexes also usually report **price returns**, which leave out dividends; **total return** versions include reinvested dividends and give a fuller picture over time.

## Key takeaways
- A stock market index tracks a group of stocks to summarize market performance and serve as a benchmark.
- The S&P 500 covers 500 leading U.S. companies and is weighted by float-adjusted market value.
- The Dow tracks 30 blue-chip companies and is price-weighted, so higher-priced shares have more influence.
- The Nasdaq Composite includes thousands of Nasdaq-listed stocks and leans toward technology.
- Percentage changes and total returns are more informative than point moves and price returns alone.
