"The stock market" sounds like a single place, but it is really a network of exchanges, trading venues, brokers, and investors that together let people buy and sell shares of publicly traded companies. Understanding the basic plumbing makes news headlines and your own brokerage account much easier to follow.

## What a share of stock represents

A share of stock is a small piece of ownership in a company. Shareholders may benefit if the company grows and its value rises, and some companies pay part of their profits to shareholders as **dividends**. Shareholders can also lose money if the company struggles or the stock price falls.

## The primary market: where stock is born

A company first sells shares to the public through an **initial public offering (IPO)**. In an IPO, the company, usually working with investment banks, sells newly created shares to investors and receives the money to fund its business. This first sale happens in what is called the **primary market**. Companies can also sell additional shares later.

## The secondary market: where most trading happens

After the IPO, shares trade between investors in the **secondary market**. When you buy a stock through a brokerage app, you are almost always buying from another investor, not from the company. The company does not receive money from these trades.

Secondary-market trading happens on:

- **Stock exchanges,** such as the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) and Nasdaq. Companies apply to be "listed" and must meet the exchange's standards.
- **Other venues,** including electronic trading systems that match buy and sell orders automatically, and firms called **market makers** that stand ready to buy or sell a stock at publicly quoted prices.

## How your order gets filled

As an individual, you trade through a **broker**, a firm registered to buy and sell securities for customers. When you place an order, your broker decides where to send it. For a listed stock, it might go to an exchange, to a market maker, or to an electronic network. Brokers have a legal duty to seek the best execution reasonably available for customer orders.

Two common order types are:

- **Market order:** buy or sell right away at the best available price. It prioritizes speed, and the final price can differ from the last quote, especially in fast-moving markets.
- **Limit order:** buy or sell only at a set price or better. It controls price but may not be filled.

Every stock has a **bid** (the highest price buyers are currently offering) and an **ask** (the lowest price sellers will accept). The gap between them is the **spread**.

## What makes prices move

Stock prices move with **supply and demand**. If more investors want to buy a stock than sell it at the current price, the price tends to rise until buyers and sellers balance. If more want to sell, it tends to fall.

What drives that demand? Company earnings and growth prospects, interest rates, economic news, industry trends, and investor sentiment all play a role. Over long periods, stock prices have tended to follow company earnings; over short periods, they can swing for reasons that are hard to predict.

## Who keeps it fair

The U.S. **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** oversees the securities markets, requires public companies to disclose financial information, and enforces laws against fraud. The **Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA)** oversees brokerage firms. Exchanges also have rules and use **circuit breakers**, temporary trading halts that kick in after very large market-wide drops, to give investors time to absorb information.

## A simple example

Here is a hypothetical example. A company's IPO sells shares at $20, and the company receives that money. A year later, strong sales lead more investors to want the stock, and it trades at $30 between investors. The company does not get the extra $10 per share, but its higher stock price can make it easier to raise money in the future and increases the value held by its shareholders.

## Key takeaways
- A stock is a share of ownership in a company, which may pay dividends and can rise or fall in value.
- Companies raise money in the primary market through IPOs; investors trade with each other in the secondary market.
- Brokers route orders to exchanges, market makers, or electronic networks and must seek best execution.
- Prices move with supply and demand, driven by earnings, interest rates, news, and sentiment.
- The SEC and FINRA regulate the markets, and circuit breakers pause trading during extreme drops.
