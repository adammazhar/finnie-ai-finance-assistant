Few organizations get as much attention from investors as the **Federal Reserve**, often just called "the Fed." It is the central bank of the United States, and its decisions about interest rates ripple through mortgages, credit cards, savings accounts, bond yields, and stock prices. Knowing what the Fed does, and what it does not do, helps make sense of a lot of financial news.

## What the Fed is trying to achieve

Congress has given the Fed two main economic goals for monetary policy, often called the **dual mandate**:

1. **Maximum employment:** the highest level of employment the economy can sustain without fueling excessive inflation.
2. **Stable prices:** keeping inflation low and steady. The Fed has said that inflation of 2% per year over the longer run, measured by the price index for personal consumption expenditures (PCE), is most consistent with its goals.

These goals can pull in different directions. Policies that support hiring can push prices up, while policies that cool inflation can slow hiring. Much of the Fed's work involves balancing the two.

## Who makes the decisions

Interest rate decisions are made by the **Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC)**. It has twelve voting members: the seven members of the Fed's Board of Governors, the president of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, and four of the other eleven regional Reserve Bank presidents, who rotate. The committee holds regularly scheduled meetings several times a year and releases a statement after each one, which markets watch closely.

## The federal funds rate

The Fed's main tool is its target for the **federal funds rate**, the interest rate at which banks lend money to each other overnight. The Fed sets a target range and uses tools such as the interest it pays banks on their reserves to keep the actual rate within that range.

Although ordinary people never pay the federal funds rate, it influences many other rates:

- **Short-term rates** such as those on savings accounts, money market funds, Treasury bills, and credit cards tend to follow it fairly closely.
- **Longer-term rates** such as mortgage rates and 10-year Treasury yields are influenced by it too, but also by expectations for inflation, growth, and future Fed policy.

## Raising and cutting rates

**When the Fed raises rates (tightening):** Borrowing becomes more expensive, which tends to slow spending and investment by households and businesses. That can help bring down inflation but may also slow economic growth and hiring.

**When the Fed cuts rates (easing):** Borrowing becomes cheaper, which tends to encourage spending and investment. That can support the economy and employment, but if overdone it can add to inflation.

Here is a hypothetical example. Suppose the Fed raises its target by 1 percentage point. A savings account that paid 2% might gradually rise toward 3%, while a variable-rate credit card charging 20% could rise to about 21%. A new car loan or mortgage would likely cost more than it did before.

Monetary policy works with a delay. Changes can take many months, even a year or more, to fully affect the economy.

## Other tools

Beyond the federal funds rate, the Fed can:

- **Buy or sell securities** such as Treasuries and mortgage-backed securities. Large-scale purchases, sometimes called quantitative easing, aim to push longer-term rates down; shrinking those holdings works in the opposite direction.
- **Lend to banks** through its discount window, especially during periods of financial stress.
- **Communicate** its outlook. Signals about future policy, sometimes called forward guidance, can move markets before any rate actually changes.

## Why investors pay attention

Interest rates affect what investors earn on cash and bonds, how much companies pay to borrow, and how stocks are valued. Markets often react not only to what the Fed does but to how it compares with what was expected. Trying to predict Fed moves is difficult, and even professionals are frequently surprised.

The Fed also has other responsibilities, including supervising banks and keeping the payment system running, but its interest rate decisions are what most investors watch.

## Key takeaways
- The Federal Reserve is the U.S. central bank, with a dual mandate of maximum employment and stable prices.
- The Fed aims for 2% inflation over the longer run, measured by the PCE price index.
- The FOMC sets a target range for the federal funds rate, which influences many other interest rates.
- Raising rates tends to cool the economy and inflation; cutting rates tends to stimulate it.
- Policy works with a lag, and markets react to how Fed actions compare with expectations.
