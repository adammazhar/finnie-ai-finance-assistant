If one investment offered high returns with no chance of loss, everyone would buy it and nobody would buy anything else. In real markets, higher potential returns almost always come with more uncertainty. This link is called the **risk-return tradeoff**, and it sits at the center of every investing decision.

## What "risk" means in investing

In everyday life, risk means danger. In investing, **risk** means uncertainty about what you will earn, including the chance that you lose money. It shows up in a few common forms:

- **Volatility risk:** prices move up and down, sometimes sharply. Large company stocks as a group have historically had a losing year roughly one year out of three.
- **Business risk:** a company can struggle or fail, which can wipe out its stock.
- **Inflation risk:** your money grows more slowly than prices rise, so it buys less over time.
- **Interest rate risk:** when rates rise, the market value of existing bonds usually falls.
- **Liquidity risk:** you may not be able to sell quickly at a fair price.

Even "safe" choices carry some risk. A savings account protects your dollars but may lose ground to inflation.

## Why higher return requires more risk

Investors are generally unwilling to accept extra uncertainty unless they expect to be paid for it. That expected extra payment is often called a **risk premium**. Because stocks can fall sharply, buyers demand a higher expected long-term return from them than from a government bond or a bank deposit.

This creates a rough ladder, from lower risk and lower expected return to higher risk and higher expected return:

1. Cash and insured deposits
2. Short-term government bonds
3. Longer-term and corporate bonds
4. Broad stock funds
5. Individual stocks and more speculative investments

The word "expected" matters. Higher risk means a *wider range* of outcomes, not a promise of higher results. A risky investment can underperform for years, or permanently.

## A hypothetical illustration

Imagine two investments, each starting at $1,000, held for one year:

- **Investment A** (lower risk) ends somewhere between $1,010 and $1,040.
- **Investment B** (higher risk) ends somewhere between $800 and $1,300.

B's best case is much better than A's, but B's worst case includes a real loss. Over long periods, investments like B have historically tended to finish ahead more often, which is why many long-term investors hold them. Over short periods, the range of outcomes is wide and anything can happen.

## Risk you are paid for, and risk you are not

Not all risk is rewarded. Putting all your money into a single company adds risk that could be reduced by spreading money across many companies, a practice called **diversification**. Markets do not reliably pay extra for risk you could have easily diversified away. So "take more risk" is not the same as "earn more."

## Finding your own balance

Two ideas help people decide how much risk fits them:

- **Risk capacity:** how much loss your finances could absorb, based on things like your time horizon, income stability, and emergency savings.
- **Risk tolerance:** how much volatility you can live with emotionally without making rushed decisions, such as selling in a panic.

Someone saving for a house in two years may have little capacity for loss, even if they feel comfortable with risk. Someone decades from retirement may have plenty of capacity but still sleep poorly during downturns. A workable plan respects both.

## Watch out for promises of "high return, no risk"

Because risk and return travel together, any pitch promising high returns with little or no risk is a classic warning sign of fraud. Treat it with deep suspicion.

## Key takeaways
- In investing, risk means uncertainty, including the possibility of loss.
- Higher expected returns generally come with a wider range of outcomes.
- Diversifiable risk, such as owning one stock, is not reliably rewarded.
- Consider both your capacity for loss and your comfort with swings.
- Offers of high returns with no risk are a red flag.
