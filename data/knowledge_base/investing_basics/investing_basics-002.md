Compounding is one of the most important ideas in personal finance. It explains why small amounts saved early can grow into large sums, and why time is often a beginner investor's biggest advantage.

## Simple interest vs. compound interest

**Simple interest** is paid only on your original deposit, called the **principal**. If you deposit $1,000 at 5% simple interest, you earn $50 every year, no more and no less.

**Compound interest** is paid on your principal *and* on the interest you have already earned. In the same example, you earn $50 in year one. In year two, you earn 5% on $1,050, which is $52.50. In year three, you earn 5% on $1,102.50. Each year's growth is a little bigger than the last, because your earnings start earning too.

With investments such as stocks or funds, returns are not a fixed "interest rate," but the same snowball effect applies when gains and dividends stay invested. People often call this **compound growth**.

## Watching the snowball grow

Here is a hypothetical example using round numbers. Suppose $10,000 grows at an average of 7% per year and nothing is withdrawn:

| Years | Approximate value |
|---|---|
| 10 | $19,700 |
| 20 | $38,700 |
| 30 | $76,100 |

Notice that the balance roughly doubles every decade or so, and the dollar growth in the last ten years (about $37,000) is far larger than in the first ten (about $9,700). With simple interest at 7%, the same $10,000 would reach only $31,000 after 30 years.

Real investments do not grow at a steady rate. Some years are up and some are down, so actual results will differ. The example only shows the math of compounding.

## The Rule of 72

A handy shortcut for estimating doubling time is the **Rule of 72**: divide 72 by the annual growth rate. At 6%, money doubles in about 12 years (72 / 6). At 9%, about 8 years. It is an approximation, but it makes the effect of different rates easy to picture.

## Why starting early matters

Time is the engine of compounding. Consider two hypothetical savers who each invest $200 a month, with an assumed 6% average annual return:

- **Early Emma** starts at 25 and contributes for 40 years. She puts in $96,000 and ends with roughly $398,000.
- **Later Leo** starts at 35 and contributes for 30 years. He puts in $72,000 and ends with roughly $201,000.

Emma contributes only $24,000 more, yet her ending balance is nearly twice as large, because her early dollars had an extra decade to compound. These are illustrations, not predictions, but the pattern holds across many assumptions.

## Compounding can work against you

The same math applies to debt. Credit card balances that carry high interest can compound quickly, so unpaid interest adds to what you owe and starts accruing interest of its own. Investment fees also compound, because every dollar paid in fees is a dollar that no longer grows. This is why many educators suggest paying off high-interest debt and paying attention to fees.

## Try it yourself

The SEC's Investor.gov site offers a free compound interest calculator. You can enter a starting amount, a monthly contribution, a time period, and an estimated rate, then see how changing each one affects the result. Try a range of rates rather than a single number, since future returns are uncertain.

## Key takeaways
- Compound interest earns returns on both your principal and past earnings.
- Growth accelerates over time, so later years often add the most dollars.
- Starting earlier can matter more than contributing larger amounts later.
- Compounding also magnifies the cost of high-interest debt and fees.
