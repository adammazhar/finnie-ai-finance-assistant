When you buy a mutual fund or exchange-traded fund (ETF), someone has to decide what goes inside it. Broadly, there are two philosophies. **Active investing** tries to *beat* the market by picking investments. **Passive investing** tries to *match* the market by owning everything in a chosen index. Understanding the difference helps you read fund descriptions and compare costs.

## What active investing means

An **actively managed fund** has a manager or team that researches companies and decides what to buy, hold, and sell. Their goal is to outperform a **benchmark**, such as a broad stock index, or to reduce risk compared with it.

**Possible advantages:**
- A skilled manager might outperform, at least for a time.
- Managers can avoid companies they consider overpriced or risky.
- Some strategies aim to limit losses in downturns or target specific goals.

**Drawbacks:**
- **Higher costs.** Research analysts, managers, and frequent trading cost money, which usually shows up as higher expense ratios and trading costs.
- **Uncertain results.** Outperformance is hard to sustain. Many studies over the years have found that most actively managed funds trail their benchmarks over long periods after fees, and past winners often do not keep winning.
- **Potential tax drag.** Frequent trading can generate taxable capital gains distributions in taxable accounts.

## What passive investing means

A **passive fund**, often called an **index fund**, aims to deliver approximately the same return as a particular market index, before fees. An **index** is a list of securities meant to represent a market or part of it, such as large U.S. companies or U.S. investment-grade bonds. You cannot invest in an index directly, but an index fund gives you an indirect way to do it by holding the securities in the index.

**Advantages:**
- **Lower costs.** No need to pay analysts to pick securities, and less trading generally means lower expenses, although not every index fund is cheap.
- **Broad diversification.** A single fund can hold hundreds or thousands of securities.
- **Transparency and simplicity.** You generally know what the fund holds and what it is trying to do.

**Drawbacks:**
- **No chance to beat the market.** By design, you get market returns minus fees, including during steep declines.
- **You own the good with the bad.** An index fund holds every company in its index, including struggling ones.
- **Not all "index" funds are simple.** Some track custom-built indexes with complex rules, so reading the fund's description still matters.

## A hypothetical cost comparison

Here are round numbers for illustration. Suppose two stock funds both earn 7% a year before costs over 25 years on a $10,000 investment. An index fund charging 0.05% would grow to about $53,600. An active fund charging 0.80% would grow to about $45,000. For the active fund to finish even, its manager would need to beat the index by roughly the fee difference every year, which is difficult to do consistently.

## It is not all or nothing

Many investors combine the two, for example using low-cost index funds as the core of a portfolio and adding a few active funds for specific purposes. Others choose one approach for its simplicity. The key is to understand what each fund is trying to do and what you are paying for it.

## Key takeaways
- Active funds try to beat a benchmark; passive funds try to match an index.
- Index funds usually cost less and offer broad diversification.
- Many active funds have trailed their benchmarks over long periods after fees.
- Passive investing means accepting market returns, including downturns.
- Compare expense ratios and strategies rather than labels alone.
