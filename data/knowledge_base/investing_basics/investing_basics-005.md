**Dollar-cost averaging (DCA)** means investing a fixed amount of money at regular intervals, such as every month, no matter what the market is doing. Many people already do this without calling it by name, for example through automatic contributions to a workplace retirement plan every payday.

## How it works

Because you invest the same dollar amount each time, the number of shares you buy changes with the price:

- When prices are **high**, your fixed amount buys **fewer** shares.
- When prices are **low**, your fixed amount buys **more** shares.

Here is a hypothetical three-month example with round numbers. You invest $300 each month in a fund:

| Month | Price per share | Shares bought |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $30 | 10 |
| 2 | $20 | 15 |
| 3 | $25 | 12 |

You invested $900 and own 37 shares. Your average cost is about $24.32 per share ($900 divided by 37), which is below the simple average of the three prices ($25). That happens because you automatically bought more shares when the price was lowest.

## Benefits of dollar-cost averaging

- **It removes the need to time the market.** Nobody reliably knows the "right" day to invest. DCA replaces guessing with a schedule.
- **It builds a habit.** Automatic, regular investing makes saving part of your routine rather than a decision you revisit every month.
- **It can soften regret.** If prices fall right after you start, only part of your money was invested at the higher price.
- **It fits how most people earn money.** For those investing from each paycheck, DCA is simply the natural way to invest.

## Limitations to understand

DCA is helpful, but it is not magic:

- **It does not prevent losses.** If an investment keeps falling, you will still lose money. DCA controls *when* you buy, not *what* you buy.
- **Lump-sum investing has often come out ahead.** When someone already has a large amount available, such as an inheritance, research has generally found that investing it all at once has tended to beat spreading it out more often than not, because markets have historically risen more often than they have fallen. The trade-off is the emotional pain if the market drops soon after. Some people choose DCA anyway for peace of mind, accepting that it may cost some expected return.
- **Transaction costs can add up.** Many brokers now charge no commission on stock and ETF trades, but check whether frequent small purchases trigger any fees.

## Staying the course

The biggest challenge with DCA is continuing during downturns. When prices fall, it can feel wrong to keep investing, yet those are the months when your fixed amount buys the most shares. Automating contributions can help you stick with the plan instead of reacting to headlines.

## Putting DCA to work

Common ways people practice dollar-cost averaging include:

1. Payroll contributions to a 401(k) or 403(b) plan.
2. Automatic monthly transfers into an IRA or brokerage account.
3. Recurring purchases of a mutual fund or ETF set up with a broker.

Whatever method you use, it helps to pick an amount that fits comfortably in your budget so you can keep going through both good and bad markets.

## Key takeaways
- Dollar-cost averaging means investing a fixed amount on a regular schedule.
- Your fixed amount buys more shares when prices are low and fewer when high.
- It builds discipline and avoids market timing, but it cannot prevent losses.
- With a lump sum already in hand, investing all at once has historically come out ahead more often, though DCA may feel easier emotionally.
