A **brokerage account** is an investment account at a registered brokerage firm that lets you buy and sell investments such as stocks, bonds, mutual funds, and exchange-traded funds (ETFs). Opening one is usually quick and can often be done online in under an hour. Knowing what to expect makes the process smoother and helps you avoid a few common surprises.

## Step 1: Choose a firm

Brokerage firms differ in fees, investment choices, tools, and customer service. Before choosing, it helps to:

- **Check the firm's background.** Tools on Investor.gov and FINRA's BrokerCheck show registration and any disciplinary history.
- **Read the relationship summary (Form CRS).** This short document explains services, fees, and conflicts of interest.
- **Compare costs.** Look at trading commissions, account maintenance fees, transfer fees, and fees for paper statements.
- **Confirm SIPC membership.** If a member firm fails financially, the Securities Investor Protection Corporation (SIPC) works to restore missing customer cash and securities up to set limits. SIPC does **not** protect against investments losing value.

## Step 2: Choose the account type

Firms offer several account types, including individual taxable accounts, joint accounts, and retirement accounts such as IRAs. Two important choices come up on the application:

- **Cash account vs. margin account.** In a **cash account**, you pay the full price of anything you buy. In a **margin account**, the firm can lend you money to buy securities, using your holdings as collateral. Margin increases buying power but also magnifies losses, charges interest, and can force sales if prices fall. Some applications make margin the default, so read carefully and confirm which type you are opening.
- **Taxable vs. tax-advantaged.** A regular brokerage account has no contribution limit but is taxed on dividends and realized gains each year. An IRA has annual contribution limits (for 2026, $7,500, plus a $1,100 catch-up for ages 50 and older; limits change each year) but offers tax benefits.

## Step 3: Provide your information

Brokers are required by law to collect certain details, so expect to provide:

- Name, address, date of birth, and contact information
- Social Security number or taxpayer identification number
- A government-issued ID
- Employment status, annual income, and net worth
- Investment experience, objectives, risk tolerance, and time horizon
- Optionally, a **trusted contact** the firm can reach if it cannot reach you or suspects fraud

## Step 4: Fund the account

Common funding methods include:

1. **Electronic bank transfer (ACH):** link a checking or savings account. Usually free, and often takes a few business days to settle.
2. **Wire transfer:** faster, but banks may charge a fee.
3. **Account transfer:** move investments from another brokerage without selling them.
4. **Check deposit:** available at many firms.

Once money arrives, it usually sits as uninvested cash. Your firm may "sweep" it into a bank deposit or money market fund. Sweep options differ in interest rates and protections, so check what yours does.

Here is a hypothetical example. Maria opens a cash account, links her checking account, and sets up an automatic $100 transfer on the first of each month. Once each transfer settles, she can place orders to buy investments.

## Step 5: Watch your statements

Brokers send trade confirmations and account statements. Reviewing them helps you spot errors or unauthorized activity early.

## Key takeaways
- Research a firm's background and fees before opening an account.
- Confirm whether you are opening a cash or margin account.
- Expect to share personal, financial, and tax information by law.
- Funding by bank transfer is common; deposits may take days to settle.
- SIPC protects against firm failure, not against investment losses.
