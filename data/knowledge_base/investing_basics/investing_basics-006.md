An **asset class** is a group of investments that share similar traits and tend to behave in similar ways. The three core asset classes most beginners encounter are **stocks**, **bonds**, and **cash**. Understanding what each one does, and how they differ, is the foundation for building any portfolio.

## Stocks (equities)

When you buy a **stock**, you own a small piece of a company. As an owner, you may benefit in two ways:

- **Price growth:** if the company becomes more valuable, its share price may rise.
- **Dividends:** some companies pay part of their profits to shareholders.

**Strengths:** Stocks have historically offered the highest long-term growth of the three classes, which helps savings outpace inflation over long periods.

**Weaknesses:** Stocks are the most volatile. Prices can fall sharply, sometimes by a third or more in a bad market, and it can take years to recover. If a company fails, its stockholders are last in line and may get nothing back.

## Bonds (fixed income)

A **bond** is a loan. You lend money to a government, city, or company, and in return it typically pays you interest on a schedule and repays the original amount, called **face value**, when the bond **matures**.

**Strengths:** Bonds generally provide steadier income and smaller price swings than stocks. High-quality bonds have often held up better than stocks during stock market downturns, which can cushion a portfolio.

**Weaknesses:** Long-term returns have historically been lower than stocks. Bond prices fall when interest rates rise (interest rate risk), and a borrower can fail to pay (credit or default risk). Inflation can also erode the value of fixed interest payments.

## Cash and cash equivalents

**Cash** in an investing sense includes savings accounts, money market funds, CDs, and short-term Treasury bills. These are designed to keep your principal stable and easy to access.

**Strengths:** Stability and **liquidity**, meaning you can reach the money quickly. Bank deposits are generally protected by FDIC insurance up to set limits.

**Weaknesses:** Low returns. Over long periods, cash has often struggled to keep up with inflation, so its purchasing power can slowly shrink.

## Comparing the three

| Asset class | Main role | Typical volatility | Long-term growth potential |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stocks | Growth | High | Highest |
| Bonds | Income and stability | Moderate | Moderate |
| Cash | Safety and access | Very low | Lowest |

These are general tendencies, not rules. Some bonds are riskier than some stocks, and any asset class can have an unusual year.

## Why combine them?

Different asset classes often react differently to the same events. When stocks fall, high-quality bonds or cash may hold steady. Combining them, a practice called **asset allocation**, lets you balance growth against stability.

Here is a hypothetical example with round numbers. Imagine a $10,000 portfolio split 60% stocks ($6,000), 30% bonds ($3,000), and 10% cash ($1,000). If stocks drop 20% and bonds and cash stay flat, the portfolio falls $1,200, or 12%, a smaller decline than stocks alone. In a strong stock year, the same mix would rise less than stocks alone. That trade-off is the point.

## Beyond the big three

Other asset categories include real estate, commodities such as gold, and private investments. Most beginners get exposure to stocks and bonds through **mutual funds** and **exchange-traded funds (ETFs)**, which hold many securities in one purchase.

## Key takeaways
- Stocks offer the most growth potential but the biggest swings.
- Bonds are loans that usually provide income and more stability than stocks.
- Cash offers safety and easy access but low long-term growth.
- Mixing asset classes balances growth against stability.
- Funds and ETFs make it easy to own many stocks or bonds at once.
