Before choosing any investment, it helps to answer a simple question: *when will I need this money?* The answer is your **time horizon**, the number of months, years, or decades you expect to keep money invested before you spend it. Time horizon shapes how much risk a goal can reasonably carry.

## Short, medium, and long horizons

Educators often group goals into rough buckets. The exact cutoffs vary, but a common framing is:

- **Short term (under about 3 years):** an emergency fund, a car purchase next year, a wedding in 18 months.
- **Medium term (about 3 to 10 years):** a house down payment, a child's education that is several years away, starting a business.
- **Long term (more than about 10 years):** retirement, or education for a newborn.

One person usually has goals in all three buckets at once, and each can have its own horizon and its own mix of investments.

## Why time changes the picture

Investments like stocks can fall sharply in a single year. Historically, though, broad stock markets have tended to recover and grow over long stretches, and the range of average annual outcomes has tended to narrow as the holding period gets longer. That pattern is historical, not guaranteed, but it explains why time horizon matters so much.

Here is a hypothetical example. Suppose a stock fund drops 25% in a bad year.

- If you need the money **next month**, you may have to sell at the low point and lock in the loss.
- If you do not need the money for **20 years**, you have time to wait and give the investment a chance to recover, while continuing to add money at lower prices.

The drop is the same in both cases. What differs is whether you are forced to sell during it. This is why short-horizon money is often kept in steadier places such as savings accounts, CDs, or short-term bonds, while long-horizon money may include more stocks.

## Time horizon and asset allocation

**Asset allocation** means dividing your money among different types of investments, such as stocks, bonds, and cash. Investors with a longer horizon may feel comfortable taking on more volatile investments in exchange for higher potential growth. Those with a shorter horizon often prefer steadier holdings that protect what they have.

A common pattern is to become more conservative as a goal approaches. For example, a hypothetical college fund for a newborn might hold mostly stocks at first, then shift gradually toward bonds and cash as the student nears age 18. **Target-date funds** and age-based 529 portfolios automate this kind of gradual shift.

## Horizon is not the same as age

It is tempting to think only about your age, but each goal has its own clock. A 30-year-old saving for a home purchase in two years has a short horizon for that money, even though their retirement money has a very long one. Also, retirement itself is not a single date. Someone retiring at 65 may spend from their savings for 25 years or more, so part of their money still has a long horizon.

## When your horizon changes

Horizons shift as life happens. A move, a new child, or a change in career plans can bring a goal closer or push it further out. When that happens, it can make sense to revisit how the money for that goal is invested.

## Key takeaways
- Your time horizon is how long money can stay invested before you need it.
- Longer horizons can usually absorb more short-term ups and downs.
- Money needed soon is often kept in steadier, easy-to-reach places.
- Each goal has its own horizon, separate from your age.
- Many plans become more conservative as a goal's date approaches.
