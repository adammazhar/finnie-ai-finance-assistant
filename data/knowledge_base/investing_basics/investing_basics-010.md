A dollar today will not buy as much in twenty years as it buys now. That gradual loss is caused by **inflation**, a general rise in the prices of goods and services over time. For savers and investors, inflation is a quiet but powerful force: it means that simply keeping your dollars safe is not the same as keeping your wealth safe.

## What purchasing power means

**Purchasing power** is the amount of goods and services your money can buy. When prices rise, each dollar buys less, so purchasing power falls.

Here is a hypothetical example with round numbers. Suppose prices rise 3% a year on average. After 20 years:

- A basket of groceries that costs $100 today would cost about $181.
- Put another way, $100 kept in a drawer would buy only about $55 worth of today's goods.

Using the **Rule of 72** (72 divided by the rate), 3% inflation roughly halves purchasing power in about 24 years. Inflation rates vary a lot from year to year, and these numbers are only for illustration.

In the United States, inflation is commonly measured by the **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**, which tracks the prices of a broad basket of consumer goods and services.

## Nominal vs. real returns

A **nominal return** is the percentage your money grew on paper. A **real return** is what is left after accounting for inflation, and it is the better measure of whether you are actually getting ahead.

A rough way to estimate it: real return ≈ nominal return minus inflation.

- If a savings account pays 2% and inflation runs 3%, the real return is about **negative 1%**. The balance grows, but its buying power shrinks.
- If an investment earns 7% and inflation is 3%, the real return is about **4%**.

This is why inflation is sometimes called a hidden tax on cash.

## How inflation affects different assets

- **Cash and savings:** very stable in dollar terms, but often lose ground to inflation over long periods. Inflation risk is a main concern for money held in cash equivalents.
- **Bonds:** most pay fixed interest, so rising inflation reduces the real value of those payments. Unexpected inflation can also push interest rates up, which lowers existing bond prices.
- **Stocks:** companies can often raise prices over time, so stocks have historically outpaced inflation over long periods, though not in every year or even every decade.
- **Inflation-protected securities:** Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (**TIPS**) have a principal value that adjusts with the CPI. At maturity you receive the adjusted principal or the original amount, whichever is greater. Series I savings bonds are another government option tied to inflation.

## Why inflation matters for long-term goals

Inflation matters most for goals that are far away. If you estimate you'll need $50,000 a year to live on in retirement in today's dollars, and retirement is 20 years away, 3% inflation means you would need about $90,000 a year in future dollars to buy the same lifestyle. Good planning tools let you work in "today's dollars" or adjust targets for inflation so the numbers stay meaningful.

## Balancing safety and growth

Inflation creates a tension. Money you need soon belongs in stable places, even if it earns less than inflation for a while. Money for goals decades away faces a bigger threat from inflation than from short-term market swings, which is one reason long-term portfolios often include growth-oriented assets.

## Key takeaways
- Inflation raises prices over time and reduces what each dollar can buy.
- Real return, meaning return after inflation, shows whether you are really getting ahead.
- Cash is stable in dollars but can lose purchasing power over long periods.
- TIPS and I bonds are government securities designed to adjust for inflation.
- Long-term goals should account for inflation when setting targets.
