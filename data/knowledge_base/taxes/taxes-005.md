Nobody enjoys watching an investment fall in value. But in a regular taxable brokerage account, a loss can have one silver lining: it may reduce your tax bill. **Tax-loss harvesting** is the practice of selling an investment that is worth less than you paid, so the loss is "realized" and can be used on your tax return. This article explains how it works under U.S. federal rules. It is general education, not tax advice, and the rules can change.

## How capital losses help

When you sell an investment below your cost basis, you have a capital loss. The tax rules let you use those losses in a specific order:

1. **Against capital gains.** Losses first offset gains. Short-term and long-term amounts are grouped separately and then netted together.
2. **Against ordinary income.** If losses exceed gains for the year, you can deduct up to **$3,000** of the net loss against other income, such as wages ($1,500 if married filing separately).
3. **Carried forward.** Any loss beyond that carries over to future years, with no expiration for individuals, until it is used up.

## A hypothetical example

Suppose an investor has these results in one year (hypothetical numbers):

- A $6,000 long-term gain from selling fund shares.
- A stock bought for $10,000 that is now worth $4,000, a $6,000 paper loss.

If they do nothing, the $6,000 gain is taxable. If they sell the losing stock, the $6,000 loss offsets the $6,000 gain, and no net capital gain remains for the year.

Now imagine the losing position had fallen by $10,000 instead. The loss would wipe out the $6,000 gain, $3,000 more could offset ordinary income, and the remaining $1,000 would carry forward to next year.

## The catch: the wash sale rule

Many investors want to stay invested after harvesting a loss. The wash sale rule limits how they can do that. If you buy the same or a "substantially identical" investment within 30 days before or after the sale, the loss is disallowed for now. It isn't lost forever in most cases; it gets added to the cost basis of the new shares. Purchases in your IRA or by your spouse can also trigger the rule. (See the separate article on the wash sale rule for details.)

Some investors instead buy a different investment that is not substantially identical, to keep a similar overall mix. Where that line falls isn't always clear: the IRS says to consider all the facts and circumstances, and it doesn't offer a simple test for comparing similar funds.

## Harvesting defers tax, it rarely erases it

A key point that is easy to miss: harvesting usually **postpones** tax rather than eliminating it. When you sell at a loss and buy a replacement at the lower price, your new cost basis is lower. If the replacement later rises, your future taxable gain is larger.

The benefit comes from things like:

- Using losses to offset gains that would be taxed at higher short-term rates.
- Deducting up to $3,000 a year against ordinary income.
- Deferring tax, which keeps more money invested in the meantime.
- Potentially never paying the deferred tax, in some circumstances such as assets held until death, which have special basis rules.

## Limits and cautions

- **Only taxable accounts.** Losses inside IRAs and 401(k)s generally can't be deducted.
- **Transaction costs and spreads** can eat into small benefits.
- **Don't let taxes drive everything.** Selling solely for a tax benefit can change your portfolio's risk, and a small tax saving may not outweigh investment considerations.
- **Record-keeping matters.** Track your lots, sale dates, and any wash sales.
- **Low-income years.** If your long-term gains would be taxed at 0% anyway (for 2026, taxable income up to $49,450 single or $98,900 married filing jointly), harvesting losses may offer little benefit, and some investors instead consider realizing gains.

Because details depend on your situation, consider IRS Publication 550 or a qualified tax professional.

## Key takeaways
- Tax-loss harvesting means selling investments at a loss in a taxable account to use that loss on your tax return.
- Losses offset gains first, then up to $3,000 of ordinary income per year, with the rest carried forward.
- The wash sale rule disallows the loss if you buy substantially identical securities within 30 days before or after the sale.
- Harvesting generally defers tax rather than eliminating it, because the replacement has a lower cost basis.
- Figures and rules change, so verify current details on IRS.gov.
