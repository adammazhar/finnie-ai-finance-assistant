When your investments make money, the IRS usually wants a share. But not all investment income is treated the same way. The type of income, how long you held the investment, and the kind of account it sits in can all change how much tax is owed. This article gives a beginner's map of the main categories under U.S. federal tax rules. It is general education, not tax advice, and tax rules change over time.

## The three main types of investment income

**Interest.** Money earned from savings accounts, CDs, and most bonds is interest. Most interest is taxed as ordinary income, meaning the same rates that apply to wages. Some interest is treated differently. For example, interest on many municipal bonds is exempt from federal income tax, and interest on U.S. Treasury securities is generally exempt from state and local income tax (though still federally taxable).

**Dividends.** Dividends are payments a company or fund makes to its shareholders. The IRS splits them into two groups. *Ordinary dividends* are taxed at your regular income rates. *Qualified dividends* meet certain requirements and are taxed at the lower long-term capital gains rates (0%, 15%, or 20%). Your broker's Form 1099-DIV shows which is which.

**Capital gains.** A capital gain happens when you sell an investment for more than you paid for it (more precisely, more than your *cost basis*). If you sell for less, you have a capital loss. A key point for beginners: an investment that goes up in value is usually not taxed until you sell it. Until then, the gain is "unrealized."

## Short-term vs. long-term gains

The holding period matters a lot. If you held an investment for **more than one year** before selling, the gain is long-term and qualifies for the lower capital gains rates. If you held it one year or less, the gain is short-term and is taxed as ordinary income.

For 2026, the long-term capital gains rate is 0% for taxable income up to $49,450 (single) or $98,900 (married filing jointly), and 15% up to $545,500 (single) or $613,700 (married filing jointly). Above those levels, the rate is 20%. These thresholds are adjusted each year for inflation.

Losses can offset gains. If your losses exceed your gains for the year, you can deduct up to $3,000 of net capital loss against other income, and carry any remainder forward to future years.

## A simple hypothetical example

Imagine a single investor in one year earns (all numbers are hypothetical):

- $200 of interest from a savings account: taxed as ordinary income.
- $300 of qualified dividends from a stock fund: taxed at the long-term capital gains rate that applies to their income.
- A $1,000 gain on shares held for three years: a long-term gain, also taxed at capital gains rates.
- A $500 gain on shares held for six months: a short-term gain, taxed as ordinary income.

Same investor, same year, but four pieces of income that may be taxed at different rates.

## Other things that affect the bill

- **Mutual fund distributions.** Funds pass along dividends and capital gains to shareholders, often near year-end. In a regular taxable account, these are taxable even if you reinvest them and never sell a share.
- **Account type.** Income earned inside tax-advantaged accounts such as 401(k)s, IRAs, and HSAs generally isn't taxed year to year. Taxes, if any, depend on the account's rules for withdrawals.
- **Additional taxes.** Higher-income investors may also owe the 3.8% net investment income tax. The income thresholds are set by law; see IRS.gov for current details.
- **State taxes.** Most states tax investment income too, and their rules can differ from federal rules.

Because the rules have many exceptions, consider checking IRS publications or a qualified tax professional for your own situation.

## Key takeaways
- Interest, dividends, and capital gains are the three main types of investment income, and each can be taxed differently.
- Long-term gains (held more than one year) and qualified dividends get lower rates; short-term gains and ordinary dividends are taxed like wages.
- Gains are generally taxed when you sell, not while an investment is simply rising in value.
- Account type matters: tax-advantaged accounts shield income from yearly taxes.
- The 2026 figures here change each year, so verify current numbers on IRS.gov.
