In a regular brokerage account, you generally owe taxes each year on interest, dividends, and any gains you realize. **Tax-advantaged accounts** change that. Congress created them to encourage saving for specific goals such as retirement, education, and health care. In return, you accept some rules about how much you can put in and when you can take money out. This article is a general overview of U.S. accounts. It is education, not tax advice, and the limits change every year.

## Three kinds of tax breaks

Tax-advantaged accounts offer some combination of these benefits:

- **Tax-deductible (pre-tax) contributions.** Money goes in before income tax, lowering your taxable income today.
- **Tax-deferred growth.** Investments grow without yearly taxes on interest, dividends, or gains.
- **Tax-free withdrawals.** Qualifying withdrawals are not taxed at all.

A useful way to think about the two most common retirement styles:

- **Traditional (pre-tax):** tax break now, and withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income.
- **Roth (after-tax):** no tax break now, but qualified withdrawals, including all the growth, are tax-free.

Which is more valuable depends partly on whether your tax rate is higher now or in retirement, which nobody can know for certain.

## The main account types

**Workplace retirement plans (401(k), 403(b), and similar).** Offered through employers, often with matching contributions. Many plans offer both traditional and Roth options. For 2026, the employee contribution limit is **$24,500**, plus an extra **$8,000** catch-up for those 50 and older, or a higher **$11,250** catch-up for those aged 60 to 63.

**Individual Retirement Accounts (IRAs).** Opened on your own at a brokerage or other provider. For 2026, you can contribute up to **$7,500** combined across traditional and Roth IRAs, plus **$1,100** catch-up if you're 50 or older. Traditional IRA deductions may be limited if you or your spouse have a workplace plan. Roth IRA eligibility phases out at higher incomes: for 2026, between **$153,000 and $168,000** of modified adjusted gross income for single filers and **$242,000 and $252,000** for married couples filing jointly.

**Health Savings Accounts (HSAs).** Available to people covered by a qualifying high-deductible health plan. For 2026, the limits are **$4,400** for self-only coverage and **$8,750** for family coverage, plus a **$1,000** catch-up at age 55 or older. HSAs can offer tax benefits on the way in, while growing, and on the way out for qualified medical expenses.

**529 education savings plans.** State-sponsored accounts for education costs. Contributions aren't deductible federally, but earnings can be withdrawn tax-free for qualified education expenses, and many states offer their own tax incentives.

Other options exist, such as ABLE accounts for qualified disability expenses. Their rules and limits are described on IRS.gov and Investor.gov.

## The trade-offs

Tax benefits come with strings attached:

- **Contribution limits** cap how much you can add each year.
- **Withdrawal restrictions.** Taking money out early or for the wrong purpose can mean income tax plus additional penalty taxes, depending on the account.
- **Required withdrawals.** Traditional retirement accounts generally require minimum distributions starting at a certain age.
- **Limited investment menus**, especially in workplace plans and 529s.

## A hypothetical illustration

Imagine $5,000 invested that grows to $10,000 over many years (hypothetical numbers). In a taxable account, dividends are taxed along the way and the $5,000 gain is taxed when sold. In a traditional IRA, nothing is taxed until withdrawal, when the whole amount is taxed as income. In a Roth IRA, a qualified withdrawal of the full $10,000 is tax-free. The best fit depends on your income, goals, and timeline.

## Key takeaways
- Tax-advantaged accounts can offer tax-deductible contributions, tax-deferred growth, tax-free withdrawals, or a mix.
- Traditional accounts give a tax break now; Roth accounts give tax-free qualified withdrawals later.
- 2026 limits include $24,500 for 401(k)s, $7,500 for IRAs, and $4,400 / $8,750 for HSAs.
- Benefits come with contribution limits and withdrawal rules, and early or non-qualified withdrawals can trigger taxes and penalties.
- Limits change every year, so check IRS.gov for current figures.
