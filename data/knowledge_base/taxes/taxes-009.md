Education is expensive, and saving for it years in advance can make a big difference. A **529 plan** is a tax-advantaged account designed for exactly that. Its name comes from Section 529 of the Internal Revenue Code, and the plans are officially called "qualified tuition programs." This article explains how they work under U.S. federal rules. It is general education, not tax or financial advice, and both federal and state rules can change.

## Two kinds of 529 plans

**Education savings plans** are investment accounts. You choose from a menu of options, usually mutual funds or ETFs, often including age-based portfolios that automatically become more conservative as the student nears college age. The account's value rises and falls with the markets, so you can lose money.

**Prepaid tuition plans** let you buy tuition credits at today's prices for use later, typically at participating public in-state colleges. They are less flexible and often require state residency.

Most people who say "529" mean the education savings plan, which is the focus of the rest of this article.

## How the tax benefits work

- **Contributions** are made with after-tax money. There's no federal deduction, but many states offer a state income tax deduction or credit, sometimes only if you use your own state's plan.
- **Growth** is not taxed while it stays in the account.
- **Withdrawals** used for qualified education expenses are free of federal income tax, and often free of state income tax too.

Qualified expenses include college tuition and fees and certain other costs such as books, supplies, and, in many cases, room and board. Current law also allows limited use for elementary and secondary school tuition and certain other expenses, including registered apprenticeship costs and, up to a lifetime cap, student loan repayments. These categories and their dollar limits have changed several times, so check the current rules on IRS.gov or Investor.gov.

## What if the money isn't used for education?

If you withdraw money for a non-qualified purpose, the **earnings** portion is subject to federal (and possibly state) income tax **plus an additional 10% federal tax**. Your original contributions come back without federal tax because they were made with after-tax money. Exceptions to the additional tax can apply, for example if the beneficiary receives a scholarship.

Other options if plans change:

- **Change the beneficiary** to another qualifying family member, such as a sibling.
- **Roll over to a Roth IRA** for the same beneficiary, subject to restrictions: the 529 account must have been open for many years, recent contributions don't qualify, annual Roth IRA contribution limits apply, and there's a lifetime cap. Details are on Investor.gov and IRS.gov.

## A hypothetical example

Suppose a family contributes $200 a month to a 529 plan from a child's birth until age 18, a total of $43,200, and the account grows to $70,000 (hypothetical numbers). If the whole amount pays for qualified college costs, the $26,800 of earnings is never subject to federal income tax. If instead $10,000 of that account were withdrawn for a non-education purpose, only the earnings portion of that withdrawal would be taxed and face the additional 10% tax.

## Things to compare before choosing a plan

- **Fees.** Plans can charge enrollment, maintenance, program management, and underlying fund fees. Broker-sold plans may add sales charges. Fees reduce your returns over time.
- **State tax benefits vs. costs.** You can generally use any state's plan. A lower-cost out-of-state plan might outweigh your home state's tax benefit, or it might not.
- **Investment options.** You can typically change investments only twice a year or when you change beneficiaries.
- **Financial aid.** 529 assets can affect need-based aid eligibility, with treatment varying by institution.

## Key takeaways
- A 529 plan is a state-sponsored, tax-advantaged account for education expenses.
- Earnings grow tax-deferred and are federally tax-free when used for qualified education expenses; many states add their own tax incentives.
- Non-qualified withdrawals trigger income tax plus an additional 10% federal tax on the earnings portion.
- Unused funds can move to another family member or, with restrictions, to the beneficiary's Roth IRA.
- Rules and limits change, so compare plan fees and check current details on Investor.gov and IRS.gov.
