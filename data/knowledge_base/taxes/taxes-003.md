When you sell an investment, taxes aren't based on the sale price alone. They're based on your profit, and to figure your profit you need to know your **cost basis**. Cost basis sounds technical, but the idea is simple: it's what you've invested in the asset for tax purposes. Getting it right can keep you from paying tax on the same money twice. This article is general education about U.S. federal rules, not tax advice.

## What cost basis is

For stocks, bonds, and funds you buy, your basis is generally the **purchase price plus related costs**, such as commissions or transfer fees. When you sell, the calculation is:

**Sale proceeds - cost basis = capital gain (or loss)**

Hypothetical example: you buy 10 shares at $50 each and pay a $5 commission. Your basis is $505. Later you sell all 10 shares for $70 each ($700) with no fee. Your gain is $700 - $505 = $195.

Basis can also change over time. The IRS calls the updated figure your **adjusted basis**. For example, a fund's "return of capital" distribution reduces your basis, and a wash sale can increase the basis of replacement shares.

## Why reinvested dividends matter

Many investors automatically reinvest dividends and fund distributions. Each reinvestment is a new purchase that adds to your basis. Because you already paid tax on those distributions in the year you received them (in a taxable account), forgetting to include them would mean paying tax on that money again when you sell.

Hypothetical example: you invest $1,000 in a fund, and over several years $200 of taxable distributions are reinvested. Your basis is $1,200, not $1,000. If you sell for $1,500, your gain is $300, not $500.

## Which shares did you sell?

If you bought the same investment at different times and prices, each purchase is a separate "lot" with its own basis. When you sell only part of your holding, the method used to identify the shares affects your gain:

- **First in, first out (FIFO):** if you don't identify specific shares, the rules generally treat the oldest shares as sold first.
- **Specific share identification:** you tell your broker exactly which lots to sell at the time of the sale and get written confirmation. This gives you the most control.
- **Average basis:** available for mutual fund shares and certain dividend reinvestment plans. You add up the cost of all your shares and divide by the number of shares to get an average cost per share. You must elect this method.

Hypothetical example: you bought 10 shares at $40 and later 10 more at $60. You now sell 10 shares at $70. Using FIFO, your gain is $300 (from the $40 lot). Identifying the $60 lot instead gives a $100 gain. Averaging ($50 per share) gives $200.

## Special cases

Basis works differently for assets you didn't buy. Inherited assets and gifts have their own basis rules, described in IRS Publication 551. If you receive stock this way, the date and value at transfer, or the previous owner's basis, may matter.

## Keeping good records

Brokers now report basis for most securities bought in recent years on Form 1099-B, which helps a lot. Still, it's wise to keep your own records of purchases, reinvestments, and any transferred or older holdings. The IRS expects taxpayers to be able to support their basis, and without records you could end up reporting a larger gain than you really had.

## Key takeaways
- Cost basis is generally what you paid for an investment, including fees, and it determines your taxable gain or loss.
- Reinvested dividends add to your basis, which helps avoid being taxed twice on the same money.
- When you sell part of a position, the method (FIFO, specific identification, or average basis) changes the gain you report.
- Inherited and gifted assets have special basis rules.
- Good records protect you; confirm details in IRS publications or with a tax professional.
