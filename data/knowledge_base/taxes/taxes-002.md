When you sell an investment for more than you paid, the profit is a capital gain. The IRS sorts every gain into one of two buckets, short-term or long-term, and the bucket can make a big difference in how much tax is owed. The deciding factor is simply how long you owned the investment. This article explains the rule under U.S. federal law. It is general education rather than tax advice, and the figures below change each year.

## The dividing line: more than one year

- **Long-term:** you held the investment for **more than one year** before selling.
- **Short-term:** you held it for one year or less.

"More than one year" has a precise meaning. The IRS says to start counting on the day *after* you acquired the investment, and to include the day you sold it. In practice, that means a sale qualifies as long-term only if it happens **after the one-year anniversary** of the purchase date. Selling on the anniversary itself is still short-term.

## A calendar example

Suppose you bought shares on **March 10, 2025** (hypothetical dates).

- Sold on **February 20, 2026**: short-term (less than a year).
- Sold on **March 10, 2026**, the exact anniversary: still short-term, because you held it exactly one year, not more than one year.
- Sold on **March 11, 2026** or later: long-term.

That single day can matter. Because leap years change the number of days in a year, it is clearer to think in terms of the calendar anniversary rather than counting days.

## How each type is taxed

**Short-term gains** are taxed as ordinary income, at the same graduated rates that apply to wages and salary.

**Long-term gains** get preferential rates of 0%, 15%, or 20%, depending on your taxable income. For tax year 2026:

| Rate | Single: taxable income up to | Married filing jointly: taxable income up to |
|---|---|---|
| 0% | $49,450 | $98,900 |
| 15% | $545,500 | $613,700 |
| 20% | above those amounts | above those amounts |

These thresholds are based on your total taxable income, not just the gain, and they are adjusted for inflation each year. Some assets, such as collectibles, follow different rules, and higher-income investors may also owe the net investment income tax. Check IRS.gov for current details.

## A hypothetical comparison

Imagine an investor whose ordinary income falls in a 22% bracket and whose long-term gains would be taxed at 15%. They have a $2,000 gain (hypothetical numbers).

- Sold as a **short-term** gain: roughly $2,000 x 22% = **$440** in federal tax.
- Sold as a **long-term** gain: roughly $2,000 x 15% = **$300** in federal tax.

The investment and the profit are identical. Only the holding period changed, and the tax bill differs by about $140. For some lower-income investors, the long-term rate could even be 0%.

## Gains and losses net against each other

At tax time, short-term gains and losses are combined, and long-term gains and losses are combined. Then the two results are netted together. If you end up with a net loss, you can deduct up to $3,000 of it against other income per year and carry the rest forward to future years.

## A word of balance

The holding period is worth understanding, but taxes are only one part of an investment decision. Holding an investment longer just to reach long-term status means staying exposed to its price swings in the meantime, and the tax savings could be outweighed by a drop in value. Rules also have exceptions (for example, inherited property), so consider IRS publications or a tax professional for your specific situation.

## Key takeaways
- Long-term means held more than one year, so the sale must come after the one-year anniversary of the purchase date.
- Short-term gains are taxed as ordinary income; long-term gains get 0%, 15%, or 20% rates.
- For 2026, the 0% long-term rate applies to taxable income up to $49,450 (single) or $98,900 (married filing jointly).
- Up to $3,000 of net capital loss per year can offset other income, with the rest carried forward.
- Figures change annually, so confirm current rules on IRS.gov.
