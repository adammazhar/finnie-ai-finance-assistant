Dividends are cash payments that companies, and the funds that own them, pass along to shareholders. In the U.S., the IRS treats dividends in two different ways: **ordinary dividends** and **qualified dividends**. The difference can change the tax rate on the same dollar of income by a meaningful amount. This article explains the distinction in general terms. It is education, not tax advice, and tax rules can change.

## Two labels, two tax treatments

**Ordinary dividends** are the default category. They are taxed as ordinary income, at the same graduated rates that apply to wages.

**Qualified dividends** are a subset of ordinary dividends that meet certain requirements. They are taxed at the lower long-term capital gains rates of 0%, 15%, or 20%, depending on your taxable income. For 2026, the 0% rate applies to taxable income up to $49,450 for single filers and $98,900 for married couples filing jointly, and the 15% rate applies up to $545,500 (single) and $613,700 (married filing jointly). These thresholds change each year.

One confusing detail: on Form 1099-DIV, box 1a shows *total* ordinary dividends, and box 1b shows the portion that is qualified. Qualified dividends are included within the ordinary total, not added on top.

## What makes a dividend "qualified"

In general, three conditions must be met:

1. **The payer.** The dividend must come from a U.S. corporation or a qualified foreign corporation.
2. **Not an excluded type.** Some payments are never qualified, even though they may be called dividends. Examples include capital gain distributions from funds, and "dividends" paid on deposits at credit unions and savings institutions, which are really treated as interest.
3. **The holding period.** You must have held the stock for **more than 60 days during the 121-day period that begins 60 days before the ex-dividend date**. (The ex-dividend date is the first day a new buyer of the stock is no longer entitled to the upcoming dividend.) Preferred stock can have a longer requirement.

The holding period rule is aimed at people who buy a stock just before a dividend and sell right after, hoping to collect a low-taxed payment without really being a long-term owner.

## A hypothetical example

Two investors each receive $1,000 in dividends in a year, and both have ordinary income in a 22% bracket, with long-term gains taxed at 15% (hypothetical numbers).

- **Investor A** has held a broad U.S. stock fund for years. Most of the dividends are qualified, so the tax is roughly $1,000 x 15% = **$150**.
- **Investor B** owns investments whose payouts don't qualify, or bought shares just before the ex-dividend date and sold shortly after. Those dividends are taxed as ordinary income: roughly $1,000 x 22% = **$220**.

Same amount of income, different tax bill.

## Funds and dividends

Mutual funds and ETFs collect dividends from the stocks and bonds they hold and pass them through to shareholders. The fund reports which part of its payouts are qualified. A fund that mostly holds U.S. stocks often passes along a high share of qualified dividends. A bond fund's payouts come mostly from interest, so they are generally taxed as ordinary income even though they're called "dividends." You also need to meet the holding period for the fund shares themselves.

## Where this doesn't matter

Inside tax-advantaged accounts like 401(k)s and IRAs, the qualified vs. ordinary distinction generally doesn't affect your yearly taxes, because dividends aren't taxed as they're earned. What matters there is how withdrawals are taxed under that account's rules.

## Key takeaways
- Ordinary dividends are taxed like wages; qualified dividends get the lower long-term capital gains rates.
- To be qualified, a dividend generally must come from an eligible corporation, not be an excluded type, and meet a holding period of more than 60 days in a 121-day window around the ex-dividend date.
- Form 1099-DIV box 1b shows the qualified portion, which is part of the box 1a total.
- Bond fund payouts are mostly ordinary income despite the "dividend" label.
- Rates and thresholds change, so confirm current figures on IRS.gov or with a tax professional.
