"What's your tax rate?" sounds like a simple question, but there are two very different correct answers. Your **marginal tax rate** is the rate on your *next* dollar of income. Your **effective tax rate** is the *average* rate you pay across all your income. Confusing the two is one of the most common money misunderstandings, and it can lead to poor decisions about raises, retirement contributions, and investments. This article explains both under U.S. federal income tax. It is general education, not tax advice, and tax figures change every year.

## Marginal tax rate: the rate on the next dollar

The U.S. uses a progressive system. Income is split into layers, called brackets, and each layer is taxed at its own rate. Federal ordinary income tax rates currently range from 10% to 37%. Your marginal rate is the rate on the highest layer your taxable income reaches.

Marginal rates matter for "what if" questions:

- How much of a $1,000 raise or bonus will I keep?
- How much tax would a $1,000 deductible retirement contribution save me?
- How much tax will extra interest income add?

In each case, the marginal rate is the relevant number, because the change happens at the top of your income.

## Effective tax rate: the average across all income

Your effective rate is your total tax divided by your income. Because the lower layers of income are taxed at lower rates, your effective rate is almost always lower than your marginal rate, often much lower.

People calculate it in two ways, so it helps to say which:

- **Tax divided by taxable income** (after deductions).
- **Tax divided by total income** (before deductions), which shows the share of everything you earned that went to federal income tax.

## A worked example with hypothetical brackets

To keep the math simple, this example uses **made-up round-number brackets**, not the real IRS brackets:

- 10% on the first $10,000 of taxable income
- 20% on taxable income from $10,000 to $40,000
- 30% on taxable income above $40,000

It does use the real **2026 standard deduction of $16,100** for a single filer.

Suppose a single person earns $66,100 and takes the standard deduction. Their taxable income is $66,100 - $16,100 = **$50,000**.

- First $10,000 x 10% = $1,000
- Next $30,000 x 20% = $6,000
- Last $10,000 x 30% = $3,000
- **Total tax: $10,000**

Now the two rates:

- **Marginal rate:** 30%, because their last dollar falls in the 30% layer.
- **Effective rate on taxable income:** $10,000 / $50,000 = **20%**.
- **Effective rate on total income:** $10,000 / $66,100 = about **15%**.

Same person, three different percentages, each answering a different question.

## Why the difference matters

- **Raises don't backfire.** If this person earned $1,000 more, only that $1,000 would be taxed at 30%, adding $300 in tax. Moving into a higher bracket never raises the tax on income already in lower brackets.
- **Deductions are worth your marginal rate.** A $1,000 deductible contribution would reduce their tax by about $300 in this example, not $200 or $150.
- **Comparing traditional and Roth accounts** often involves comparing your marginal rate today with the rate you expect on withdrawals later.
- **Budgeting uses the effective rate.** To estimate what share of your income goes to federal income tax overall, the average rate is more useful.

Real situations add layers: capital gains and qualified dividends have their own rates, tax credits reduce tax directly, and some benefits phase out as income rises, which can make the true marginal cost of extra income differ from the bracket rate. State and payroll taxes are separate too.

## Key takeaways
- Your marginal rate is the rate on your next dollar of taxable income; your effective rate is your total tax divided by your income.
- Because income is taxed in layers, the effective rate is usually well below the marginal rate.
- Use the marginal rate for decisions about extra income or deductions, and the effective rate for the big picture.
- Entering a higher bracket only affects income in that bracket, not all your income.
- The 2026 standard deduction is $16,100 for single filers; figures change yearly, so check IRS.gov.
