Selling an investment at a loss can lower your taxes, because capital losses offset gains and, within limits, other income. But what if someone sold a stock at a loss on Monday and bought it right back on Tuesday? They'd have a tax deduction while their investment position barely changed. The **wash sale rule** exists to stop exactly that. This article explains the rule in plain language. It is general education about U.S. federal tax rules, not tax advice, and the rules can change.

## What counts as a wash sale

A wash sale happens when you sell stock or securities **at a loss** and, within **30 days before or after** the sale, you:

- buy substantially identical stock or securities,
- acquire them in a fully taxable trade,
- acquire a contract or option to buy them, or
- acquire substantially identical stock for your IRA or Roth IRA.

The window covers 30 days on each side of the sale date, so it's roughly a two-month period centered on the sale. It also applies if your **spouse**, or a corporation you control, buys substantially identical stock during that window.

The rule only applies to losses. Selling at a gain and buying back immediately doesn't create a wash sale (the gain is simply taxable).

## What happens to the loss

In most cases the loss is **postponed, not erased**. Here's how:

1. The loss is disallowed for the current year.
2. The disallowed loss is **added to the cost basis** of the replacement shares.
3. The holding period of the original shares carries over to the new shares.

When you eventually sell the replacement shares, the higher basis reduces your gain (or increases your loss), so the tax benefit shows up later.

## A hypothetical example

- You buy 100 shares for $1,000.
- Later you sell all 100 for $700, a $300 loss.
- Twenty days later you buy 100 shares of the same stock for $750.

Because you bought back within 30 days, the $300 loss is disallowed now. Your basis in the new shares becomes $750 + $300 = **$1,050**. If you later sell those shares for $1,100, your gain is $50, instead of $350. The $300 loss was effectively deferred into the new position.

## The IRA trap

There's an important exception to the "postponed, not erased" idea. If the replacement shares are bought inside an **IRA or Roth IRA**, the loss is disallowed and the basis adjustment does not apply to the IRA shares. In that situation the loss may be permanently lost. This can happen by accident, for example if an IRA has automatic purchases of the same fund you just sold at a loss in a taxable account.

## What is "substantially identical"?

The IRS says to look at all the facts and circumstances. Shares of one company generally aren't substantially identical to shares of a different company. Buying back the exact same stock or fund clearly is. The gray area is investments that are very similar, such as two funds tracking the same index from different providers. There isn't a simple published test for those, which is one reason many investors keep careful records or consult a tax professional.

## Common ways people trip over the rule

- **Automatic dividend reinvestment** in the same fund within 30 days of a loss sale can create a small wash sale on the reinvested shares.
- **Buying before selling.** The 30 days *before* the sale count too, so adding shares and then selling older, higher-cost shares at a loss can trigger it.
- **Multiple accounts.** The rule looks across your accounts and your spouse's, not just a single brokerage account.

Brokers report some wash sales on Form 1099-B, but generally only within the same account, so investors with several accounts may need to track this themselves.

## Key takeaways
- A wash sale occurs when you sell at a loss and buy substantially identical securities within 30 days before or after the sale.
- The disallowed loss is usually added to the new shares' basis, so it is deferred rather than lost.
- Purchases by your spouse, or in your IRA or Roth IRA, can trigger the rule, and an IRA purchase can make the loss permanently unusable.
- Dividend reinvestment and multiple accounts are common sources of accidental wash sales.
- Rules change and have nuances, so check IRS Publication 550 or a tax professional.
