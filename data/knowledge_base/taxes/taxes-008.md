A Health Savings Account (HSA) is usually described as a way to pay medical bills. That's true, but many people don't realize an HSA can also hold investments like mutual funds and grow for decades. Because of how it's taxed, some investors view it as one of the most tax-efficient accounts available. This article explains how HSAs work under U.S. federal rules. It is general education, not tax or medical-planning advice, and limits change every year.

## Who can open one

To contribute to an HSA, you generally must:

- be covered by a **high-deductible health plan (HDHP)**, a health plan that meets IRS requirements for minimum deductibles and maximum out-of-pocket costs,
- have no other disqualifying health coverage,
- not be enrolled in Medicare, and
- not be claimed as a dependent on someone else's tax return.

Once you enroll in Medicare, you can no longer contribute, although you can keep using the money already in the account.

## The tax treatment

HSAs are often described as having a "triple tax advantage" when used for medical costs:

1. **Contributions are tax-deductible** (or excluded from income if made by your employer, including through payroll), even if you don't itemize.
2. **Growth is tax-free.** Interest, dividends, and gains inside the account aren't taxed.
3. **Withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free.**

For 2026, the contribution limit is **$4,400** for self-only coverage and **$8,750** for family coverage. People 55 or older can add a **$1,000** catch-up contribution. These limits are adjusted annually.

## Why investors pay attention to HSAs

Unlike a flexible spending account (FSA), an HSA has **no "use it or lose it" rule**. Unused money carries over year after year, and the account stays with you if you change jobs.

Many HSA providers let you move money beyond a cash balance into investment options such as mutual funds. Over a long period, invested money can grow, and if it's eventually used for qualified medical expenses, the growth is never taxed.

Some people choose to pay current medical bills from other savings and leave their HSA invested, keeping receipts in case they want to reimburse themselves later. Whether that makes sense depends on cash flow, emergency savings, and record-keeping discipline. It's also worth checking how the rules on later reimbursement apply to you.

## What happens with non-medical withdrawals

- **Before age 65:** a withdrawal not used for qualified medical expenses is included in taxable income **and** hit with an additional 20% tax.
- **At 65 or older** (or if disabled): the 20% additional tax no longer applies. Non-medical withdrawals are still taxed as ordinary income, which makes the account behave much like a traditional IRA for those dollars. Medical withdrawals remain tax-free.

## A hypothetical example

Suppose someone contributes $3,000 a year to an HSA for 20 years and invests it, and the account grows to $100,000 (hypothetical numbers). If that money is later used for qualified medical expenses, none of it is taxed: not the contributions, and not the growth. If they instead withdraw it for non-medical purposes after 65, they'd owe ordinary income tax but no penalty. Before 65, they'd owe income tax plus the 20% additional tax on non-medical withdrawals.

## Things to weigh

- **Fees and investment choices** vary widely between HSA providers.
- **Investments can lose value**, so money needed for near-term medical bills may not suit risky investments.
- **An HDHP means higher out-of-pocket costs** before insurance pays, which isn't a good fit for everyone's health or budget.
- **State taxes:** state income tax treatment of HSAs can differ from the federal rules, so it's worth checking your state.

## Key takeaways
- HSAs require coverage by a qualifying high-deductible health plan and no Medicare enrollment.
- Used for qualified medical expenses, HSA money can be tax-deductible going in, grow tax-free, and come out tax-free.
- For 2026, limits are $4,400 (self-only) and $8,750 (family), plus a $1,000 catch-up at 55 or older.
- Unused balances roll over and can be invested, which is why some treat the HSA as a long-term investment account.
- Non-medical withdrawals before 65 face income tax plus a 20% additional tax, and rules change, so check IRS Publication 969.
