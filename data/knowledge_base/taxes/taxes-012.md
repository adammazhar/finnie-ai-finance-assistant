Many people believe that earning a little more money can push them into a higher tax bracket and leave them with less take-home pay. Under the U.S. federal income tax, that's a myth. The reason is how **progressive taxation** works: income is taxed in layers, and a higher rate applies only to the income inside the higher layer. This article walks through the mechanics. It is general education, not tax advice, and brackets and deductions change every year.

## From income to taxable income

Brackets don't apply to every dollar you earn. First, your income is reduced by deductions to arrive at **taxable income**.

Most people take the **standard deduction**, a flat amount set by law. For tax year 2026, it is **$16,100** for single filers and **$32,200** for married couples filing jointly. Others itemize deductions instead, if their itemized total is larger. Some adjustments, such as deductible contributions to a traditional IRA or pre-tax 401(k) contributions, reduce income before this step as well.

## Brackets are layers, not labels

Think of your taxable income as water filling a series of buckets. The first bucket fills and is taxed at the lowest rate. Only income that overflows into the next bucket is taxed at the next rate, and so on.

Federal ordinary income tax currently has seven rates, from 10% to 37%. The dollar ranges for each bracket depend on your filing status (single, married filing jointly, and so on) and are adjusted for inflation each year. The IRS publishes the current tables on IRS.gov.

## A worked example with hypothetical brackets

To make the arithmetic easy to follow, here are **made-up, round-number brackets** (not the actual IRS brackets):

| Hypothetical bracket | Rate |
|---|---|
| First $10,000 of taxable income | 10% |
| $10,000 to $40,000 | 20% |
| Above $40,000 | 30% |

**Person A** is single, earns $56,100, and takes the 2026 standard deduction of $16,100. Taxable income is **$40,000**.

- $10,000 x 10% = $1,000
- $30,000 x 20% = $6,000
- **Total tax: $7,000**

**Person A gets a $2,000 raise** to $58,100. Taxable income becomes $42,000, which crosses into the 30% bracket.

- $10,000 x 10% = $1,000
- $30,000 x 20% = $6,000
- $2,000 x 30% = $600
- **Total tax: $7,600**

The raise added $2,000 of income and $600 of tax. Person A keeps $1,400 of the raise. The 30% rate touched only the $2,000 above $40,000; the first $40,000 is taxed exactly as before. Their overall (effective) rate on taxable income went from 17.5% to about 18.1%, not to 30%.

## What progressive taxation does and doesn't mean

- **Everyone gets the lower rates on their first dollars.** A high earner pays the same low rates on their first layers of income as everyone else.
- **Your "bracket" describes your top layer only.** Saying "I'm in the 22% bracket" means your last dollars are taxed at 22%, not your whole income.
- **Other rules can interact.** Long-term capital gains and qualified dividends use their own rate schedule, stacked on top of ordinary income. Tax credits reduce the tax bill directly. Some credits and deductions phase out as income rises, which can make the real cost of extra income somewhat higher than the bracket rate in certain ranges. States have their own systems too.

## Why it's worth understanding

Knowing how brackets work helps you estimate the tax effect of a raise, a side job, a bonus, or a deductible retirement contribution, all of which are measured at your top (marginal) rate. It also helps you avoid turning down income because of a misunderstanding.

## Key takeaways
- Federal income tax is progressive: taxable income is split into layers, each taxed at its own rate.
- Taxable income is income minus deductions; the 2026 standard deduction is $16,100 (single) or $32,200 (married filing jointly).
- Moving into a higher bracket only raises the tax on income inside that bracket, so a raise doesn't reduce take-home pay from ordinary income tax.
- Bracket ranges depend on filing status and change yearly; current tables are on IRS.gov.
- Credits, phase-outs, capital gains rates, and state taxes can add complexity, so consider a tax professional for your situation.
