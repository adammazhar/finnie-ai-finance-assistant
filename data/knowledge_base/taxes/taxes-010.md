Most investors hear a lot about **asset allocation**: how much to hold in stocks, bonds, and cash. A related but separate idea is **asset location**: deciding *which account* holds each investment. If you have more than one type of account, such as a taxable brokerage account, a traditional 401(k) or IRA, and a Roth IRA, the same investments can produce different after-tax results depending on where they sit. This article explains the general principles. It is education, not tax or investment advice, and the right approach depends on individual circumstances.

## Why location matters

Different investments generate different kinds of taxable income:

- **Taxable bond interest** is taxed as ordinary income each year.
- **Qualified dividends and long-term capital gains** get lower tax rates.
- **Broad stock index funds**, especially ETFs, tend to distribute relatively little in capital gains, so much of their growth stays untaxed until you sell.

And different accounts tax income differently:

- **Taxable accounts** tax interest, dividends, and fund distributions every year, and realized gains when you sell.
- **Traditional (tax-deferred) accounts** don't tax yearly income, but all withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income.
- **Roth accounts** don't tax yearly income, and qualified withdrawals are tax-free.

Asset location tries to match the two lists so that less of your return is lost to taxes over time.

## Common general guidelines

These are widely discussed rules of thumb, not universal answers:

1. **Tax-inefficient investments often go in tax-advantaged accounts.** Taxable bonds and bond funds, and funds with high turnover or large ordinary-income distributions, would otherwise produce yearly income taxed at ordinary rates.
2. **Tax-efficient investments often go in taxable accounts.** Broad stock index funds and ETFs generate mostly qualified dividends and few capital gain distributions. In a taxable account you also get the ability to harvest losses and to control when gains are realized.
3. **Roth space is valuable for high expected growth.** Since qualified Roth withdrawals are tax-free, some investors prefer to hold the assets they expect to grow the most there.
4. **Municipal bonds belong in taxable accounts, if anywhere.** Their interest is generally exempt from federal income tax already, so holding them in a tax-advantaged account wastes that benefit.

## A hypothetical example

Imagine an investor wants $50,000 in a bond fund and $50,000 in a stock index fund, with $50,000 of room in an IRA and $50,000 in a taxable account (hypothetical numbers). Suppose the bond fund pays $2,000 a year in interest and the stock fund pays $800 in qualified dividends.

- **Bonds in the IRA, stocks in taxable:** the $2,000 of interest isn't taxed yearly. Only the $800 of dividends is taxed, at the lower qualified rate.
- **Stocks in the IRA, bonds in taxable:** the $2,000 of interest is taxed yearly at ordinary rates, while the lower-taxed dividends are sheltered.

In this simple case, the first arrangement typically results in lower yearly taxes. The actual difference depends on tax brackets, returns, and how long the money is invested.

## Why it isn't always that simple

- **Your overall allocation comes first.** Asset location only rearranges holdings; it shouldn't change your target mix of stocks and bonds.
- **Limited choices.** A 401(k) may not offer the funds you want, which constrains placement.
- **Differing growth rates.** If bonds grow slowly inside a traditional account, and stocks grow quickly in taxable, the balances drift apart and rebalancing can get complicated.
- **Accessibility.** Money you may need before retirement generally has to be in a taxable account.
- **Uncertainty.** Future tax rates and laws are unknown, and your own bracket may change.
- **Small portfolios.** If nearly all your money is in one type of account, there may be little to optimize.

## Key takeaways
- Asset location means choosing which account holds each investment, separate from choosing your overall mix.
- Investments that produce ordinary income, like taxable bonds, are often placed in tax-advantaged accounts.
- Tax-efficient investments, like broad stock index funds, are often placed in taxable accounts.
- Municipal bonds generally make little sense inside tax-advantaged accounts because their interest is already federally tax-exempt.
- Benefits depend on your brackets, accounts, and time horizon, so treat these as general principles.
