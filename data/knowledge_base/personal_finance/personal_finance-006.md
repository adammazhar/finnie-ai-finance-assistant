Interest is the price of borrowing money. When you take out a loan or carry a credit card balance, the lender charges interest as a percentage of what you owe. Understanding how that percentage is quoted and applied makes it much easier to compare offers and see the true cost of debt.

## APR: the yearly price tag

The **annual percentage rate (APR)** expresses the yearly cost of borrowing as a percentage. For many loans, the APR includes not just the interest rate but also certain fees, such as some loan origination fees, spread over the year. That's why APR is useful for comparing offers "apples to apples": two loans with the same interest rate can have different APRs if one charges higher fees.

For credit cards, the APR and interest rate are usually described as the same thing. Many cards actually have several APRs:

- A **purchase APR** for regular purchases
- A **cash advance APR**, often higher, usually with no grace period
- A **balance transfer APR**, sometimes a low promotional rate for a limited time
- A **penalty APR** that may apply after late payments

APRs can also be **fixed** or **variable**. A variable APR is tied to an index, such as the prime rate, and moves up or down when that index changes.

## How credit card interest is calculated

Credit card interest is usually charged daily. The card issuer converts the APR into a **daily periodic rate** by dividing it by 365 (some use 360). That daily rate is applied to your balance each day.

**Hypothetical example:** With an 18% APR, the daily rate is about 0.049% (18% / 365). On a $2,000 balance, that's roughly $0.99 of interest per day, or about $30 over a 30-day month.

Many cards calculate interest on your **average daily balance** and add it to what you owe. Next month, interest is charged on the new, larger balance, which means you pay interest on past interest. That's **compounding**, and it's why balances can grow even if you stop using the card.

## The grace period: how to pay no interest

Most credit cards offer a **grace period**, the time between the end of a billing cycle and the payment due date. If you pay the full statement balance by the due date, you generally owe no interest on new purchases. Carry any balance past the due date and you usually lose the grace period, so interest may start on new purchases right away until the balance is paid in full.

## The minimum-payment trap

Minimum payments are often a small percentage of the balance. Paying only the minimum keeps an account in good standing, but much of each payment goes to interest, so the balance shrinks slowly.

**Hypothetical example:** On a $3,000 balance at 22% APR, a $60 monthly payment would take more than 11 years to repay, with total interest of over $5,000, more than the original balance. Raising the payment to $150 would clear the debt in a little over two years, with total interest under $800. Credit card statements include a minimum payment warning showing how long repayment would take at the minimum.

## Installment loans work differently

Car loans, mortgages, and many personal and student loans are **installment loans**: you borrow a set amount and repay it in fixed monthly payments over a set term. This is called **amortization**. Early payments go mostly toward interest; later payments go mostly toward principal (the amount borrowed).

A longer term lowers the monthly payment but usually increases total interest paid. For instance, a hypothetical $20,000 car loan at 7% costs about $2,200 in interest over three years but about $3,800 over five years.

## Key takeaways
- APR states the yearly cost of borrowing and, for many loans, includes certain fees, making it useful for comparing offers.
- Credit card interest is typically calculated daily and compounds, so unpaid interest itself starts earning interest.
- Paying the full statement balance by the due date generally avoids interest on purchases thanks to the grace period.
- Paying only the minimum can stretch repayment out for years; longer loan terms lower payments but raise total interest.
