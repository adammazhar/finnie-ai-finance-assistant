Your **net worth** is a single number that summarizes your financial position at a moment in time: everything you own minus everything you owe. Income tells you how much money flows in, but net worth tells you how much you've actually kept and built. Tracking it over time is one of the simplest ways to see whether your overall finances are moving in the right direction.

## The basic formula

**Net worth = assets - liabilities**

- **Assets** are things you own that have value: cash in checking and savings accounts, investment and retirement accounts, the market value of a home, a car's resale value, and other valuable property.
- **Liabilities** are debts you owe: mortgages, car loans, student loans, credit card balances, personal loans, medical debt, and unpaid taxes.

Think of it as a personal version of the **balance sheet** a company publishes. Economists use the same idea to study households; the Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances, for example, measures families' assets, debts, and net worth.

## A hypothetical example

Here's how Morgan's net worth might look:

| Assets | Value | Liabilities | Owed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Checking and savings | $6,000 | Student loans | $22,000 |
| 401(k) | $15,000 | Car loan | $9,000 |
| Car (resale value) | $12,000 | Credit card | $2,000 |
| **Total assets** | **$33,000** | **Total liabilities** | **$33,000** |

Morgan's net worth is $33,000 - $33,000 = **$0**. That may sound discouraging, but it's a very common starting point, especially for young adults with student loans. What matters most is the direction over time. If Morgan pays down $6,000 of debt and adds $4,000 to savings over the next year, net worth rises to roughly $10,000 (before any drop in the car's value), even with the same income.

## Tips for an honest calculation

- **Use current, realistic values.** A car is worth what it would sell for today, not what you paid. For a home, a conservative estimate of market value, minus selling costs if you like, is more useful than an optimistic one.
- **Include every debt,** even small ones and money owed to family.
- **Be consistent.** Some people exclude their home or retirement accounts to see a "liquid" net worth, the part more easily turned into cash. Either choice is fine as long as you use the same method each time.
- **Leave out everyday belongings** such as clothes and furniture unless they're genuinely valuable; they rarely resell for much.

## Negative net worth is not a verdict

Many people, particularly recent graduates, have a negative net worth because their debts exceed their assets. That's a snapshot, not a judgment of character or a prediction of the future. Some debts, such as education loans or a mortgage, are often taken on to build long-term earning power or a valuable asset. The goal is for the number to trend upward.

## How net worth grows

Net worth changes in three main ways:

1. **Positive cash flow.** Spending less than you earn and saving or investing the difference.
2. **Paying down debt.** Every dollar of principal repaid reduces liabilities.
3. **Asset growth.** Investments and home values can rise over time, though they can also fall.

Buying something with debt doesn't change net worth at the moment of purchase if the item holds its value, but items that lose value quickly, such as cars, tend to pull net worth down over time.

## Using net worth alongside other measures

Net worth is a powerful big-picture metric, but it isn't everything. Someone with a high net worth tied up entirely in a house could still struggle to pay an unexpected bill. That's why net worth works best alongside other checks, such as having an emergency fund, a budget with positive cash flow, and manageable debt payments. Tools like the CFPB's financial well-being questionnaire also look at how secure and in control people feel, not just the numbers.

Many people calculate net worth once or twice a year, or quarterly, using a simple spreadsheet.

## Key takeaways
- Net worth equals total assets minus total liabilities and gives a snapshot of your financial position.
- Use realistic current values and a consistent method each time you calculate it.
- A negative or zero net worth is common early in adult life; the trend over time matters more than any single reading.
- Net worth grows through positive cash flow, debt repayment, and asset growth, and is best used alongside other measures such as cash reserves and budgeting.
