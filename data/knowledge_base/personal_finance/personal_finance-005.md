A **credit score** is a three-digit number that estimates how likely you are to repay borrowed money on time. Lenders use it to decide whether to approve a credit card, car loan, or mortgage and what interest rate to charge. Landlords, insurers, and some utility companies may also look at credit information. A higher score generally means easier approvals and lower borrowing costs, so understanding how scores work can save real money over a lifetime.

## Credit reports vs. credit scores

These two terms are related but different:

- A **credit report** is a detailed record of your borrowing history: your accounts, balances, payment history, and certain public records such as bankruptcies. In the U.S., three nationwide credit bureaus (Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion) each maintain a report on you.
- A **credit score** is calculated from the information in a report using a mathematical model.

Because each bureau may hold slightly different information, and because there are several scoring models (FICO and VantageScore are the best known, each with multiple versions), you don't have just one credit score. Common models use a range of roughly 300 to 850.

## What goes into a FICO score

FICO publishes the general weight of each category in its scores for the population as a whole. Your own mix can differ depending on your history.

- **Payment history (about 35%).** Whether you've paid bills on time. Late payments, collections, and bankruptcies hurt most.
- **Amounts owed (about 30%).** How much you owe, especially your **credit utilization ratio**: the share of your available revolving credit you're using. Using $1,500 of a $5,000 total limit is 30% utilization; lower is generally viewed more favorably.
- **Length of credit history (about 15%).** How long your accounts have been open, including the age of your oldest account and the average age of all accounts.
- **Credit mix (about 10%).** Experience with different types of credit, such as credit cards (revolving) and installment loans like car loans.
- **New credit (about 10%).** Opening several new accounts in a short time can signal higher risk. Applications typically create a "hard inquiry," which may lower scores slightly for a while.

Notice what isn't included: your income, savings, and investments don't directly affect credit scores.

## Habits that tend to help

- **Pay every bill on time.** Automatic minimum payments can prevent accidental late payments.
- **Keep balances low relative to limits.** Paying down card balances can improve utilization fairly quickly.
- **Be thoughtful about new applications.** Space them out when you can.
- **Let accounts age.** Closing an old card can shorten your history and reduce available credit, which may raise utilization.
- **Build credit if you have little history.** Options include a secured credit card (backed by a deposit) or a credit-builder loan from a bank or credit union.

**Hypothetical example:** Taylor has two cards with a combined $4,000 limit and carries a $2,000 balance, 50% utilization. Paying the balance down to $800 drops utilization to 20%, which, all else equal, is generally seen as lower risk.

## Checking your credit reports

Reviewing your reports helps catch mistakes and possible identity theft. You can get free reports from each of the three bureaus through **AnnualCreditReport.com**, the site authorized under federal law for free reports. The bureaus now let people check their reports there weekly at no charge. Watch out for look-alike websites that try to sell services.

If you find an error, such as an account you never opened, you can dispute it with the credit bureau and the company that reported it. Checking your own report or score does not hurt your score; only lender "hard" inquiries related to applications can.

Negative items don't last forever. Most, such as late payments, generally stay on a report for up to seven years, and their impact tends to fade as they age and positive history builds.

## Key takeaways
- A credit report is your borrowing history; a credit score is a number calculated from it to predict repayment risk.
- You have many scores, not one, because of different bureaus and scoring models.
- Payment history and amounts owed (including utilization) carry the most weight in FICO scores.
- Free credit reports are available through AnnualCreditReport.com, and checking your own credit doesn't lower your score.
