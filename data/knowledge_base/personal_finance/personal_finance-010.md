Investment fraud happens when someone uses lies, pressure, or tricks to take your money under the pretense of investing it. Scammers target people of every age, income, and experience level, and new investors can be especially appealing targets. The good news is that most schemes share recognizable warning signs, and a few simple checks can stop many of them.

## Common red flags

Regulators such as the SEC, FINRA, and the FTC highlight a consistent set of warning signs:

- **Promises of high returns with little or no risk.** Every real investment carries some risk. Anyone who claims their product is certain to pay off, or that you're sure to make money, is waving a major red flag.
- **Unusually steady returns.** An investment that goes up every single month, no matter what markets do, is suspicious. Real investments fluctuate.
- **Pressure to act now.** "This opportunity closes today" is designed to stop you from thinking or researching. Legitimate professionals give you time.
- **Unsolicited contact.** Cold calls, texts, emails, dating-app matches, or social media messages pitching investments deserve extra skepticism.
- **Secrecy or recruiting.** Being told to keep an investment quiet, or to bring in friends and family, are common features of fraud.
- **Vague or overly complex explanations.** If the seller can't clearly explain how the money is made, or credits a "secret" system, be wary.
- **Unregistered sellers or products.** Many frauds involve people who aren't licensed or investments with no official documents.
- **Unusual payment requests.** Being asked to pay with gift cards, wire transfers to individuals, or cryptocurrency, or to send money to someone personally rather than to a registered firm, is a warning sign.

## Common types of schemes

- **Ponzi schemes** pay earlier investors with money from newer investors rather than real profits. They collapse when new money dries up.
- **Pump-and-dump schemes** hype a thinly traded stock or crypto token to push the price up, then the promoters sell, leaving others with losses.
- **Affinity fraud** targets members of a group, such as a religious, ethnic, or professional community, by exploiting trust within it.
- **Relationship or romance investment scams** build a friendship or romance online over weeks or months before steering the victim to a fake investment platform.
- **Imposter scams** involve fraudsters posing as a real firm, a government regulator, or a well-known person.
- **"Investment training" and seminar scams** sell costly courses or coaching with promises of easy riches.

## Simple habits that protect you

- **Verify the seller.** You can look up brokers and investment advisers for free using FINRA BrokerCheck and the search tool on Investor.gov to see registration and any disciplinary history.
- **Verify the product.** Registered securities have official filings; the SEC's EDGAR database lets anyone search company filings.
- **Take your time.** Sleep on any decision, and talk it over with someone you trust who isn't involved in the deal.
- **Contact firms directly.** Use a phone number or website you find yourself, not one provided in a suspicious message.
- **Protect your information.** Don't share account passwords, one-time codes, or Social Security numbers with unsolicited callers.
- **Review statements.** Check for trades you didn't authorize or missing money.

## A hypothetical example

Dana receives a friendly message from a stranger on social media who, over several weeks, shares screenshots of big crypto gains and invites Dana to a trading app. Dana's first $500 deposit appears to grow quickly. When Dana tries to withdraw, the app demands a "tax" payment first. These are classic signs of a relationship investment scam: unsolicited contact, too-good results, an unfamiliar platform, and fees to access your own money.

## If you suspect fraud

Stop sending money, save all messages and records, and contact your bank or brokerage right away. Reports can be made to the SEC, FINRA, the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and your state securities regulator. Be cautious of "recovery" services that promise to get lost money back for an upfront fee; they are often a second scam.

## Key takeaways
- Promises of high returns with little risk, pressure to act fast, and unsolicited pitches are among the most common fraud warning signs.
- Ponzi, pump-and-dump, affinity, relationship, and imposter schemes are common forms of investment fraud.
- Checking a seller's registration on FINRA BrokerCheck or Investor.gov takes minutes and can prevent large losses.
- If you suspect fraud, stop payments, keep records, and report it to regulators; avoid paid "recovery" offers.
