Not every dollar belongs in the stock market. Money for emergencies or for goals a few years away usually needs to be safe and predictable more than it needs to grow fast. **High-yield savings accounts** and **certificates of deposit (CDs)** are two common places for that kind of money. Both are bank deposit products that pay interest, and both can be protected by federal deposit insurance, but they differ in flexibility.

## High-yield savings accounts

A high-yield savings account is a savings account that pays a higher interest rate than a typical traditional savings account. They are often offered by online banks, which have lower costs than banks with many branches, though some traditional banks and credit unions offer them too.

- **Access:** You can deposit and withdraw money when you need it, although some banks limit the number of certain withdrawals per month.
- **Variable rate:** The rate can change at any time, usually rising and falling with the general level of interest rates in the economy.
- **Watch for:** minimum balance requirements, monthly fees, and introductory rates that drop after a few months.

Rates are usually quoted as **APY (annual percentage yield)**, which includes the effect of compounding, meaning you earn interest on previously earned interest. APY makes it easy to compare accounts.

## Certificates of deposit (CDs)

A CD is a deposit you agree to leave at the bank for a fixed **term**, such as 6 months, 1 year, or 5 years. In exchange, the bank typically pays a **fixed rate** for the whole term.

- **Predictability:** You know in advance what you'll earn if you hold the CD to its **maturity date**.
- **Early withdrawal penalty:** Taking money out before maturity usually costs a penalty, often a set number of months of interest. In some cases, it can eat into your original deposit.
- **Rate trade-off:** If rates rise after you open a CD, you're locked into the lower rate. If rates fall, you keep the higher one.
- **Renewal:** Many CDs automatically roll into a new CD at maturity unless you act during a short grace window.

**Hypothetical example:** Lee deposits $10,000 in a 1-year CD paying 4% APY. At maturity, Lee would have about $10,400. If Lee withdraws after six months and the penalty is three months of interest, Lee gives up about $100 of the roughly $200 earned so far.

Some savers build a **CD ladder**: splitting money across CDs with staggered terms (for example, 1, 2, and 3 years) so a portion matures regularly. That provides periodic access to cash while capturing longer-term rates on part of the money.

## FDIC insurance: what's protected

The **Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC)** is a U.S. government agency that insures deposits at member banks. If an FDIC-insured bank fails, insured depositors are repaid up to the coverage limit. According to the FDIC, the standard coverage is **$250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each account ownership category** (such as single accounts, joint accounts, and certain retirement accounts). The FDIC also states that no depositor has lost a penny of insured funds since its founding in 1933. Credit union deposits receive similar protection through the **National Credit Union Administration (NCUA)**.

Deposit insurance covers **deposits**, such as checking, savings, money market deposit accounts, and CDs. It does **not** cover stocks, bonds, mutual funds, annuities, crypto assets, or Treasury securities, even when you buy them through a bank. It also doesn't protect against losing value to inflation.

## How they compare

| Feature | High-yield savings | CD |
|---|---|---|
| Access to money | Anytime (some limits) | Locked until maturity, or pay a penalty |
| Interest rate | Variable | Usually fixed for the term |
| Good fit for | Emergency funds, near-term flexible savings | Money with a known future date |

Both options generally carry very low risk of losing principal within insurance limits, but their returns may not keep up with inflation over long periods. That's why they're typically used for short-term needs and cash reserves rather than long-term growth.

## Key takeaways
- High-yield savings accounts offer flexible access and a variable rate; CDs lock money up for a set term, usually at a fixed rate.
- CDs typically charge an early withdrawal penalty, so matching the term to when you need the money matters.
- FDIC insurance covers deposits at insured banks, with standard coverage of $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category; NCUA provides similar coverage at credit unions.
- Deposit insurance does not cover investments like stocks, bonds, or mutual funds, and these accounts may not outpace inflation over the long run.
