Investing is about growing wealth; insurance is about protecting it. A single uninsured event, such as a serious illness, a car accident where you're at fault, or a disability that stops you from working, can wipe out years of savings or force you to sell investments at a bad time. For many people, having the right basic coverage is part of the foundation that makes long-term investing possible.

## How insurance works

Insurance transfers risk. You pay a regular **premium** to an insurer, and in return the insurer agrees to pay certain costs if a covered event happens. A few terms come up across most policies:

- **Deductible:** the amount you pay out of pocket before coverage kicks in. Higher deductibles usually mean lower premiums.
- **Coverage limit:** the maximum the policy will pay.
- **Exclusions:** situations the policy doesn't cover.
- **Beneficiary:** the person who receives a payout, mainly for life insurance.

The general principle is that insurance is most valuable for large, unlikely losses you couldn't afford to cover yourself. Small, predictable costs are often better handled with savings, such as an emergency fund.

## Common types of coverage

**Health insurance** helps pay for medical care, which can be extremely expensive in the U.S. Coverage may come through an employer, a government program, or the Health Insurance Marketplace. Under federal law, young adults can often stay on a parent's health plan until age 26.

**Auto insurance** is required in nearly every state if you drive. **Liability coverage** pays for damage or injuries you cause to others; collision and comprehensive coverage help with damage to your own car.

**Renters or homeowners insurance** covers your belongings against events such as fire or theft, and typically includes personal liability coverage if someone is hurt in your home. Renters insurance is often inexpensive relative to the value it protects. Mortgage lenders generally require homeowners insurance.

**Disability insurance** replaces part of your income if illness or injury keeps you from working. For many working adults, the ability to earn income is their largest financial asset, yet this coverage is often overlooked. Some employers offer short- or long-term disability coverage.

**Life insurance** pays a benefit when the insured person dies, to support people who depend on that person's income, such as a spouse or children. Someone with no dependents may have little need for it. There are two broad families:

- **Term life** covers a set period, such as 20 years, and pays only if death occurs during that term. It's typically the least expensive way to buy a given amount of coverage.
- **Permanent life** (whole, universal, and variable life) lasts for life and includes a cash value component. Premiums are much higher, and fees and features can be complex. Variable life and variable universal life are securities, so they come with a prospectus and investment risk.

**Umbrella insurance** adds extra liability coverage on top of auto and home policies. It's sometimes considered by people with significant assets that could be targeted in a lawsuit.

## A hypothetical example

Chris, 28, has $20,000 invested and no dependents. Without health insurance, a $40,000 hospital stay could erase those savings and more. Without renters insurance, a fire could mean replacing belongings out of pocket. Chris likely has little need for life insurance today, but that may change after marriage or children. The point isn't that Chris needs every policy, but that each type protects against a specific risk.

## Shopping for coverage

- **Compare quotes** from several insurers; prices for the same coverage can differ widely.
- **Read what's excluded,** not just what's covered.
- **Check employer benefits** first; group health, disability, and life coverage can be cost-effective.
- **Review coverage after life changes** such as moving, marriage, a new child, or buying a home.
- **Be cautious with insurance sold as an investment.** Products that mix insurance and investing can carry high costs and surrender charges. Understanding fees, and checking whether the seller is licensed, helps before signing.

## Key takeaways
- Insurance protects savings and investments from large losses that could otherwise derail financial progress.
- Key terms include premium, deductible, coverage limit, and exclusions; higher deductibles generally lower premiums.
- Health, auto, renters or homeowners, and disability coverage address common risks; life insurance matters most when others depend on your income.
- Term life is usually the simplest and cheapest life coverage, while permanent policies add cash value, complexity, and cost.
