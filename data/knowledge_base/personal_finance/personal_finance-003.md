When people have some extra money each month, a common question is whether to invest it or use it to pay down debt. There's no one answer for every situation, but for **high-interest debt**, such as most credit card balances, the math tends to point strongly in one direction. This article explains why, and where the trade-offs get less clear-cut.

## Paying off debt is like earning a return

Every dollar you use to pay off a debt saves you the interest that dollar would have cost. So paying off a balance charging 20% a year is similar to earning a 20% return on that money, with no market risk. The saving is certain as long as the debt would otherwise stay unpaid.

Investments work differently. Stocks have historically returned more than bonds or cash over long periods, but those returns are not promised and vary widely from year to year. Some years stocks fall sharply. It's very difficult for any investment to reliably beat the cost of a credit card rate in the high teens or above.

**Hypothetical example:** Sam has a $5,000 credit card balance at 22% APR and $5,000 in cash beyond an emergency fund.

- If Sam invests the $5,000 and the investment earns 7% in a year, that's about $350 before any taxes.
- Meanwhile, the card balance costs roughly $1,100 or more in interest over that year.
- Using the cash to pay off the card avoids that interest entirely, a clearly better outcome in this example.

The gap only widens if the investment has a bad year.

## What counts as "high interest"?

There's no official cutoff, but a useful way to think about it is to compare the debt's rate with what investments might reasonably earn over time, after accounting for risk.

- **Usually high:** credit cards, payday loans, many personal loans, and some pay-later installment plans once fees or promotional periods kick in. Rates on these often sit well into the double digits.
- **Often moderate:** some car loans and private student loans. The decision here is more of a judgment call.
- **Often low:** many mortgages and federal student loans, especially those taken out when rates were low. Many people invest while carrying these debts, because the expected long-term return on a diversified portfolio may exceed the rate.

Other factors matter too, such as whether interest is tax-deductible, whether the rate is fixed or variable, and how the debt affects your peace of mind.

## Important exceptions and ordering

Paying off high-interest debt first doesn't mean ignoring everything else. Some widely used priorities:

- **Keep a small emergency fund.** Without any cash cushion, the next surprise expense tends to go right back on the card. Many people build a starter fund of $500 to $1,000 before attacking debt aggressively.
- **Consider an employer retirement match.** If an employer matches 401(k) contributions, contributing enough to receive the full match is often compared to an immediate, large return on that money. Many people weigh this alongside debt payoff rather than skipping it.
- **Stay current on all minimum payments.** Missing payments can bring late fees, penalty rates, and credit score damage.

## After the debt is gone

Once high-interest balances are paid off, the money that was going toward them each month is freed up. Redirecting that same payment into savings and investments is a way to keep the habit going. Because of compound growth, starting to invest earlier generally gives money more time to grow, which is one more reason clearing expensive debt quickly can pay off.

## Key takeaways
- Paying off debt earns a certain "return" equal to its interest rate, with no market risk.
- For high-interest debt such as credit cards, that saved interest is usually larger than what investments can reliably earn.
- For low-rate debt, such as many mortgages, investing while paying on schedule is common; the decision depends on rates, taxes, and comfort with risk.
- A small emergency fund and any employer retirement match are often considered alongside paying down high-interest debt.
