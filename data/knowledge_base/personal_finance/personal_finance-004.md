If you have several debts, such as a couple of credit cards, a car loan, and a personal loan, it can be hard to know where to send any extra money. Two popular approaches, the **avalanche** method and the **snowball** method, give a clear order to follow. Both start the same way, and both work. The difference is what they prioritize.

## The common starting point

With either method:

1. List every debt with its **balance**, **interest rate (APR)**, and **minimum payment**.
2. Keep paying the minimum on every debt, every month. Missing minimums can mean late fees, penalty interest rates, and damage to your credit score.
3. Decide on a fixed extra amount you can put toward debt each month.
4. Send all of that extra money to one "target" debt until it's gone.
5. When a debt is paid off, add its old payment to the extra amount and target the next debt.

Step 5 is where the momentum comes from: the payment toward the current target keeps growing as each debt disappears.

## The avalanche method: highest rate first

The avalanche method targets the debt with the **highest interest rate** first, regardless of balance. Mathematically, this approach generally minimizes the total interest you pay and can get you debt-free slightly sooner, because the most expensive debt is eliminated first.

The downside is psychological. If the highest-rate debt also has a large balance, it may take many months before you pay anything off completely, and that can feel discouraging.

## The snowball method: smallest balance first

The snowball method targets the debt with the **smallest balance** first, regardless of interest rate. Small debts disappear quickly, which gives early wins and can build motivation to keep going. Research on behavior suggests that visible progress helps some people stick with a plan.

The downside is cost. If a larger debt carries a higher rate, it keeps growing interest while you work on smaller ones, so you'll usually pay more in total than with the avalanche.

## A hypothetical comparison

Imagine Alex has $300 a month beyond minimum payments and three debts:

| Debt | Balance | APR |
|---|---|---|
| Store card | $600 | 18% |
| Credit card | $4,000 | 24% |
| Car loan | $8,000 | 7% |

- **Avalanche order:** credit card (24%), then store card (18%), then car loan (7%).
- **Snowball order:** store card ($600), then credit card ($4,000), then car loan ($8,000).

With the snowball, the store card is gone in about two months, a quick win. With the avalanche, the first payoff takes longer, but the 24% card, the costliest debt, shrinks from the start, which usually lowers total interest. In this example the two orders differ only in the first two debts, so the cost difference is modest; it grows when a large balance carries the highest rate.

## Which one is better?

It depends on what keeps you going. If you're motivated by saving the most money and can stay patient, the avalanche lines up with the math. If you need quick wins to stay committed, the snowball may be easier to stick with, and a plan you follow beats a "better" plan you abandon. Some people use a hybrid: knock out one or two tiny balances for momentum, then switch to highest rate first.

## Other tools to know about

- **Balance transfer cards** may offer a low promotional rate for a limited time, usually with a transfer fee. The regular rate after the promotion can be high.
- **Debt consolidation loans** combine several debts into one payment, ideally at a lower rate. They help only if spending habits don't recreate the old balances.
- **Nonprofit credit counseling** agencies can help create a plan. Be cautious of companies that charge large upfront fees or promise to erase debt.

## Key takeaways
- Both methods pay minimums on everything and send extra money to one target debt, rolling payments forward as debts are paid off.
- The avalanche targets the highest interest rate first and generally costs the least in total interest.
- The snowball targets the smallest balance first and provides faster early wins that can help motivation.
- The best method is the one you'll consistently follow; a hybrid approach is also common.
