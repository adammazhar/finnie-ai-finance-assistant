When you leave a job, your 401(k) doesn't disappear. The money you contributed, plus any vested employer contributions, still belongs to you. But you'll need to decide what happens to it. A **rollover** means moving retirement money from one account to another while keeping its tax-advantaged status.

## Your main options

When you leave an employer, you typically have four choices for a 401(k) or similar plan:

1. **Leave it in the old plan.** Many plans let former employees keep their money where it is. You can no longer contribute, but the investments stay put.
2. **Roll it into your new employer's plan**, if the new plan accepts rollovers.
3. **Roll it into an IRA**, an individual retirement account you open yourself at a financial institution.
4. **Cash it out** by taking a lump-sum distribution.

Each option has trade-offs. Plans and IRAs can differ in investment choices, fees, and services, so it's worth comparing before deciding.

## Why cashing out is usually costly

Taking the money as cash ends its tax-advantaged status. For a traditional 401(k), the withdrawal is generally taxed as ordinary income. If you're under age 59½, you may also owe a 10% additional tax unless an exception applies. On top of that, money spent now no longer has decades to compound for retirement. Regulators such as FINRA describe cashing out as a potentially costly option for exactly these reasons.

## Direct vs. indirect rollovers

How you move the money matters a lot.

**Direct rollover:** The old plan sends the money straight to the new plan or IRA. You never touch the funds, and no taxes are withheld. This is generally the simplest route.

**Indirect (60-day) rollover:** The plan pays the money to you, and you have **60 days** to deposit it into another retirement plan or IRA. Here's the catch: when a workplace plan pays a distribution directly to you, it must withhold **20%** for federal income taxes. To roll over the full amount, you have to replace that withheld 20% from other money.

## A hypothetical example

Suppose you have $50,000 in your old 401(k) and choose an indirect rollover. These numbers are hypothetical.

- The plan withholds 20% ($10,000) and sends you a check for $40,000.
- To roll over the full $50,000, you'd deposit the $40,000 plus $10,000 from your own savings into an IRA within 60 days. The withheld $10,000 may come back to you as a credit when you file your taxes.
- If you deposit only the $40,000, the missing $10,000 is treated as a taxable distribution, and if you're under 59½, it may also face the 10% additional tax.

With a direct rollover of the same $50,000, the full amount moves with nothing withheld.

## Other rules to know

- **One-per-year limit for IRA-to-IRA rollovers.** You can do only one IRA-to-IRA 60-day rollover in any 12-month period. This limit doesn't apply to direct trustee-to-trustee transfers or to rollovers from a workplace plan to an IRA.
- **Not everything can be rolled over.** Required minimum distributions and hardship distributions, for example, aren't eligible.
- **Traditional vs. Roth money.** According to the IRS, rolling pre-tax money into a Roth IRA or designated Roth account is taxable, unlike a rollover between accounts of the same type. Keeping traditional money with traditional and Roth money with Roth avoids an unexpected tax bill.
- **Missed the 60 days?** The IRS may waive the deadline in certain situations beyond your control, but it's better not to rely on that.

## Questions people ask themselves

- How do the fees and investment options compare between the old plan, the new plan, and an IRA?
- Would having fewer accounts make it easier to keep track of everything?
- Is any of my employer money unvested, meaning I'd lose it by leaving?
- Does the new plan accept rollovers, and how long does the process take?

## Key takeaways
- When you change jobs, you can generally leave your 401(k), roll it to a new plan, roll it to an IRA, or cash it out.
- Cashing out usually means income tax and possibly a 10% additional tax if you're under 59½.
- Direct rollovers avoid the 20% mandatory withholding that applies when a plan pays you directly.
- Indirect rollovers must be completed within 60 days, and IRA-to-IRA rollovers are limited to one per 12 months.
