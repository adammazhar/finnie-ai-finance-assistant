Retirement accounts come with a trade-off: tax breaks in exchange for keeping the money in place until later in life. Take money out of a 401(k) or an IRA before **age 59½** and, on top of regular income tax, you generally owe a **10% additional tax** on the taxable part of the withdrawal. The IRS lists a number of exceptions to that extra tax. One of the best known is often called the **Rule of 55**, and it matters most to people who stop working in their mid-to-late 50s.

## What the rule says

If you **leave your employer during or after the calendar year you turn 55**, withdrawals from that employer's workplace plan, such as a 401(k) or 403(b), are not subject to the 10% additional tax. "Leaving" can mean retiring, quitting, or being laid off. The IRS calls it separation from service.

For some public safety employees of a state or local government, such as police officers and firefighters, the age is **50** for withdrawals from a governmental plan. The IRS also allows it after 25 years of service under the plan, if that comes first.

A few details matter:

- **The calendar year counts, not your birthday.** Someone who turns 55 in December and leaves their job in March of that same year can still qualify.
- **It's about the plan of the job you leave.** The exception covers the plan of the employer you separate from at or after that age. Money still sitting in a plan from a job you left at, say, 48 doesn't qualify.
- **Income tax still applies.** The rule removes only the 10% additional tax. Withdrawals of pre-tax money are still taxed as ordinary income in the year you take them.

## Why it doesn't work for IRAs

The Rule of 55 applies only to workplace plans. It does **not** apply to IRAs. That has a practical consequence: if you roll a 401(k) into an IRA after leaving your job, the money becomes IRA money, and the age-55 exception no longer covers it. Withdrawals before 59½ would then generally face the 10% additional tax unless another exception applies.

The reverse is also true for some exceptions. A first-time home purchase (up to $10,000) and qualified higher education expenses are exceptions for **IRAs only**; they don't apply to a 401(k). Because the lists differ, it helps to know which kind of account the money is in before planning an early withdrawal.

## A hypothetical example

Imagine Dana, who turns 56 this year and leaves a long-time job in June. Dana's former employer's 401(k) holds $300,000. In September, Dana withdraws $20,000 to cover living costs while starting a smaller business.

Because Dana separated from service in a year when Dana was at least 55, the 10% additional tax, $2,000 on this withdrawal, doesn't apply. The $20,000 is still added to Dana's taxable income for the year. If Dana had instead moved the whole balance to an IRA first, the same withdrawal would generally have triggered the extra $2,000.

## Other routes to early access

The Rule of 55 isn't the only path. The IRS's list includes exceptions for disability, certain medical expenses, a series of substantially equal periodic payments, and others. The equal-payments exception works for both IRAs and workplace plans, but it comes with strict rules about how long the payments must continue. Each exception has its own conditions, and plans set their own rules about how and when former employees can take withdrawals, so the plan's documents and the IRS pages are the places to confirm the details.

## Key takeaways
- Withdrawals before age 59½ generally face a 10% additional tax, but the IRS allows several exceptions.
- Under the Rule of 55, leaving your employer during or after the year you turn 55 (50 for some public safety workers) removes that extra tax on withdrawals from that employer's plan.
- The rule applies to workplace plans like 401(k)s, not to IRAs, so rolling the money into an IRA first gives up the exception.
- Regular income tax still applies to pre-tax money, and plan rules about withdrawals vary.
