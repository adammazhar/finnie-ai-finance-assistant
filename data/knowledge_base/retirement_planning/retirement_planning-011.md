Social Security is a federal program that pays monthly benefits to eligible retired workers, as well as to some disabled workers, spouses, and survivors. For many Americans, it's a foundation of retirement income. Understanding the basics, especially how your claiming age affects your check, can help you fit it into your broader retirement picture.

## How you qualify

While you work, you pay Social Security taxes through payroll deductions (or self-employment tax). In return, you earn **Social Security credits**. You can earn up to four credits per year, and the amount of earnings needed for each credit is adjusted over time.

To qualify for retirement benefits, you generally need **40 credits**, which works out to about 10 years of work. Earning more than 40 credits doesn't raise your benefit. The amount you receive depends on your earnings history, not on the number of credits.

## How your benefit is calculated

The Social Security Administration (SSA) looks at your earnings over your career, adjusts older years' earnings for changes in average wages, and uses up to **35 years** of those earnings, choosing the years with the highest adjusted earnings, to compute an average. A formula then converts that average into your **primary insurance amount**, the benefit you'd receive at full retirement age. Working more years at higher pay can replace lower-earning years in that calculation.

## Full retirement age and claiming choices

Your **full retirement age (FRA)** is the age at which you can receive your full, unreduced benefit. It depends on your birth year. For people born in **1960 or later, FRA is 67**.

You don't have to claim at FRA. You can choose to start anytime between **age 62** and **age 70**:

- **Claiming early (as early as 62):** Your monthly benefit is permanently reduced by a small percentage for each month before FRA. For someone with an FRA of 67, claiming at 62 reduces the benefit by about 30%.
- **Claiming at FRA:** You receive your full benefit.
- **Delaying past FRA:** You earn **delayed retirement credits**. For people born in 1943 or later, the benefit rises by 8% for each full year you wait beyond FRA. The increases stop at age 70, so there's no benefit-size reason to wait beyond that.

## A hypothetical example

Suppose someone born in 1965 has an estimated benefit of $2,000 a month at their FRA of 67. These numbers are hypothetical.

- **At 62:** about $1,400 a month (30% less).
- **At 67:** $2,000 a month.
- **At 70:** about $2,480 a month (three years of 8% credits, or 24% more).

The difference between claiming at 62 and 70 is large per month, but early claimers receive checks for more years. Which choice leads to more total income depends heavily on how long the person lives.

## Factors people weigh when deciding

There's no single best claiming age. Common considerations include:

- **Health and life expectancy.** Longer expected lifespans tend to reward waiting.
- **Other income and savings.** Some people need income sooner; others can draw from savings while delaying.
- **Marriage.** Spouses may be eligible for benefits based on a partner's record (up to 50% of the worker's full-retirement-age benefit), and claiming decisions can affect survivor benefits.
- **Continuing to work.** If you claim before FRA and keep working, benefits can be reduced when earnings exceed certain limits. SSA says the withheld amounts aren't lost: your benefit is increased at FRA to account for them.
- **Medicare timing.** Medicare eligibility generally begins at 65, and SSA reminds people to consider enrolling around then even if they delay Social Security.

## Checking your own record

You can create a free **my Social Security** account on SSA.gov to review your earnings history and see personalized benefit estimates at different claiming ages. Checking your record for errors every few years is a simple way to make sure your future benefit is based on accurate earnings.

## Key takeaways
- You generally need 40 credits (about 10 years of work) to qualify for Social Security retirement benefits.
- Benefits are based on up to 35 of your highest years of wage-adjusted earnings.
- Full retirement age is 67 for people born in 1960 or later; you can claim as early as 62 or as late as 70.
- Claiming at 62 cuts benefits by about 30% for those with an FRA of 67, while delaying past FRA adds 8% per year until 70.
- Your my Social Security account shows your earnings record and personalized estimates.
