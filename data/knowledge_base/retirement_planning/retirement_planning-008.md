Life doesn't always leave room to save for retirement early. Student loans, raising children, buying a home, or a stretch of lower income can all push retirement saving down the list. **Catch-up contributions** are a feature of the tax code that lets people age 50 and older put extra money into retirement accounts beyond the normal annual limits.

## Who qualifies

You're eligible for catch-up contributions in a given year if you'll be **age 50 or older by the end of that calendar year**. So someone who turns 50 in December can make catch-up contributions for that entire year.

You don't need to prove you're "behind" on saving. The name suggests catching up, but eligibility is based only on age. For workplace plans, the plan itself must allow catch-up contributions.

## The 2026 catch-up amounts

| Account | Regular 2026 limit | 2026 catch-up (50+) | Potential total |
|---|---|---|---|
| 401(k), 403(b), governmental 457 | $24,500 | $8,000 | $32,500 |
| Same plans, turning 60-63 during the year | $24,500 | $11,250 | $35,750 |
| Traditional and Roth IRAs (combined) | $7,500 | $1,100 | $8,600 |

These are 2026 figures. The IRS adjusts limits over time, so check IRS.gov for the current year.

## The higher catch-up for ages 60 to 63

A newer rule from the SECURE 2.0 Act created a larger catch-up window for people who turn 60, 61, 62, or 63 during the year. For 2026, eligible participants in 401(k) and similar plans can contribute up to $11,250 in catch-up contributions instead of $8,000, if their plan offers it. Once you pass that age window, the regular age-50 catch-up amount applies again.

This special window reflects the idea that the years just before retirement are often when people have the most income available to save, for example after children leave home or a mortgage is paid off.

## Roth catch-up rule for higher earners

Beginning in 2026, some workers must make their workplace catch-up contributions as **Roth** (after-tax) contributions rather than pre-tax. This applies to participants whose prior-year wages from the employer sponsoring the plan exceeded an IRS-set threshold. For these workers, catch-up contributions no longer reduce taxable income in the year they're made, though qualified withdrawals later can be tax-free. The IRS catch-up contributions page explains the details.

## A hypothetical example

Consider a 55-year-old who is able to contribute the maximum to a 401(k). Without catch-up contributions, the 2026 limit is $24,500. With the $8,000 catch-up, the total becomes $32,500. If that person also contributes to an IRA, they could add up to $8,600 there. These numbers are purely illustrative; most people contribute less than the maximum, and what's realistic depends on income and expenses.

If limits stayed the same for ten years (they usually rise), making the $8,000 catch-up each year would add $80,000 of contributions, before any investment growth.

## Things to keep in mind

- **Check your plan.** Your plan documents show whether catch-up contributions are allowed and how to elect them.
- **Multiple plans.** If you work for two unrelated employers, the IRS has specific rules on how catch-up contributions are counted across plans.
- **Budget reality.** Catch-up room is an option, not an obligation. Many people balance it with other goals such as paying down debt or maintaining an emergency fund.
- **Other accounts.** Health savings accounts have their own catch-up, $1,000 for 2026, starting at age 55 rather than 50.

## Key takeaways
- Catch-up contributions let people age 50 and older (by year-end) save beyond the normal limits.
- For 2026, the workplace-plan catch-up is $8,000, or $11,250 for those turning 60-63; the IRA catch-up is $1,100.
- Some higher earners must make workplace catch-up contributions on a Roth basis starting in 2026.
- Your plan must permit catch-up contributions, and limits change over time, so confirm details on IRS.gov.
