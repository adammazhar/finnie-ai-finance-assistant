An IRA, or **individual retirement arrangement** (often called an individual retirement account), is a retirement account you open on your own, usually at a bank, brokerage, or fund company. Unlike a 401(k), it isn't tied to your job. A **traditional IRA** is the original version, built around one core idea: pay taxes later instead of now.

## How a traditional IRA works

You put money into the account, choose investments inside it (such as funds, stocks, or bonds, depending on where you open it), and let it grow. The key tax feature is **tax deferral**: earnings inside the account aren't taxed each year. Instead, you generally pay income tax when you take money out.

Depending on your situation, your contributions may also be **tax-deductible**, meaning they reduce your taxable income for the year you contribute.

## Who can contribute

To contribute, you need **taxable compensation**, such as wages or self-employment income. For 2020 and later, there's no age limit on making regular contributions to a traditional IRA, so people can keep contributing while they have earned income.

There's one notable exception to the compensation rule: a **spousal IRA**. If you file a joint tax return, a spouse with little or no income may be able to contribute based on the other spouse's compensation.

## How much you can contribute

For 2026, the total you can put into all of your traditional and Roth IRAs combined is $7,500, or your taxable compensation for the year if that's less. People age 50 or older can add a $1,100 catch-up contribution. These limits typically change over time, so check IRS.gov each year.

## Will your contribution be deductible?

This is where traditional IRAs get a little more involved:

- If neither you nor your spouse is covered by a retirement plan at work, your contributions are generally fully deductible.
- If you or your spouse *is* covered by a workplace plan, the deduction may be reduced or eliminated depending on your income and filing status. The IRS publishes the income ranges each year.

If you can't deduct a contribution, you can still make a **nondeductible** contribution. The money still grows tax-deferred, but you'll need to report it and keep careful records (IRS.gov explains how) so you aren't taxed twice on the same dollars when you withdraw.

## A hypothetical example

Imagine someone in a 22% federal tax bracket who isn't covered by a workplace plan contributes $5,000 to a traditional IRA and deducts it. That deduction could lower their federal income tax bill for the year by roughly $1,100. Decades later, when they withdraw the money, both the original $5,000 and any growth are taxed as ordinary income at whatever rate applies then. These figures are hypothetical and simplified.

## Taking money out

- **Before age 59½:** Withdrawals are generally taxable and may also face a 10% additional tax, unless an exception applies (the IRS lists exceptions such as disability, certain higher education expenses, and a limited first-time homebuyer exception).
- **After 59½:** Withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income, with no additional tax.
- **Required minimum distributions:** You generally must start taking withdrawals from a traditional IRA at your RMD age: 73 if you were born 1951-1959, or 75 if you were born in 1960 or later (SECURE 2.0). This keeps the money from staying tax-deferred forever.

## When people consider a traditional IRA

A traditional IRA is often discussed as an option for people without a workplace plan, people who want to save beyond their workplace plan, or people who expect to be in a lower tax bracket in retirement than today. Whether it fits depends on your income, tax situation, and goals.

## Key takeaways
- A traditional IRA is a personal retirement account where investment growth is tax-deferred.
- You need taxable compensation to contribute; for 2026 the combined IRA limit is $7,500, plus $1,100 if you're 50 or older.
- Contributions may be deductible, but the deduction can be limited if you or your spouse has a workplace plan.
- Withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income, early withdrawals may face a 10% additional tax, and required distributions begin at age 73 or 75, depending on birth year.
