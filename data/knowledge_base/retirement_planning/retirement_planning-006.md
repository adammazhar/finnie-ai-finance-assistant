Many retirement accounts come in two tax flavors: **traditional** and **Roth**. You'll see this choice in 401(k) plans, 403(b) plans, and IRAs. Both offer tax advantages. The difference is *when* you get the tax break: now, or later.

## The two approaches in one sentence each

- **Traditional:** You get a tax break today (pre-tax or deductible contributions), and you pay income tax on withdrawals in retirement.
- **Roth:** You pay tax today (after-tax contributions), and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free.

In both cases, investments grow without being taxed year by year.

## The big idea: compare tax rates

The central question is whether your tax rate on this money will be higher now or when you withdraw it in retirement. A simple hypothetical example shows why.

Imagine you have $1,000 of pre-tax salary to save, and your investments quadruple over the years before retirement. Assume a flat 22% tax rate today. These numbers are hypothetical and simplified.

- **Traditional:** The full $1,000 goes in and grows to $4,000. If your tax rate in retirement is also 22%, you keep $3,120 after tax.
- **Roth:** You pay 22% tax first, so $780 goes in. It grows to $3,120, and qualified withdrawals are tax-free. You keep $3,120.

With the **same tax rate** now and later, the result is identical. The math only tips one way when the rates differ:

- If your retirement tax rate turns out to be **lower** (say 12%), the traditional account leaves you $3,520, more than the Roth.
- If your retirement tax rate turns out to be **higher** (say 32%), the traditional account leaves you $2,720, less than the Roth.

So the choice is largely a prediction about your future tax situation compared with today's.

## Factors people weigh

**Where you are in your career.** Early-career workers often earn less than they will later, so their current tax rate may be relatively low. That's one reason Roth contributions are often discussed for younger savers. People in their peak earning years may value the traditional deduction more.

**Expected retirement income.** Pensions, Social Security, required withdrawals from traditional accounts, and other income all affect your future tax bracket.

**Uncertainty about future tax law.** Nobody knows what tax rates will be decades from now. Tax rules can change.

**Tax diversification.** Some savers split contributions between traditional and Roth accounts. Having both types of money in retirement can offer flexibility in choosing which account to draw from each year to manage taxable income.

**Required minimum distributions.** Traditional accounts require withdrawals starting at a certain age. Roth IRAs have no required distributions during the owner's lifetime, and designated Roth accounts in 401(k) and 403(b) plans also aren't subject to lifetime distributions for the owner.

**Eligibility rules.** Roth IRA contributions phase out at higher incomes. Traditional IRA deductions can be limited if you or your spouse has a workplace plan. Roth 401(k) contributions, where a plan offers them, don't have an income limit.

**Cash flow today.** A traditional contribution lowers this year's tax bill, which can make it easier to save the same amount from your paycheck.

## You don't have to choose just one

Many 401(k) plans let you split contributions between traditional and Roth in any proportion. How employer matching contributions are treated depends on your plan's rules, so check your plan documents. Some people also pair a traditional 401(k) with a Roth IRA or the reverse.

## Key takeaways
- Traditional accounts give a tax break now and tax withdrawals later; Roth accounts tax contributions now and allow tax-free qualified withdrawals later.
- If your tax rate is the same now and in retirement, both approaches can produce the same after-tax result.
- A lower expected rate in retirement favors traditional; a higher expected rate favors Roth.
- Career stage, future income, tax-law uncertainty, and eligibility rules all factor in, and splitting between both is common.
