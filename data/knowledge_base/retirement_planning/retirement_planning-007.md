Tax-advantaged retirement accounts come with annual contribution limits set by the IRS. The limits exist because these accounts offer valuable tax breaks, and Congress caps how much of those breaks any one person can use each year. The IRS usually adjusts the figures for inflation, so they tend to rise over time. This article covers the **2026** figures, as announced by the IRS.

## The 2026 numbers at a glance

| Account or rule | 2026 limit |
|---|---|
| 401(k), 403(b), governmental 457 plans: employee contributions | $24,500 |
| Catch-up contribution, age 50 and older (401(k) and similar plans) | $8,000 |
| Higher catch-up, ages 60-63 (401(k) and similar plans) | $11,250 (instead of $8,000) |
| IRA contributions (traditional and Roth combined) | $7,500 |
| IRA catch-up, age 50 and older | $1,100 |
| Roth IRA income phase-out, single (MAGI) | $153,000 - $168,000 |
| Roth IRA income phase-out, married filing jointly (MAGI) | $242,000 - $252,000 |

These are 2026 figures only. Limits change from year to year, so always confirm the current numbers on IRS.gov.

## Workplace plans: 401(k), 403(b), and 457

In 2026, you can contribute up to **$24,500** of your pay to a 401(k) or similar plan. That limit applies to your traditional and Roth contributions *combined*, and it applies per person, not per plan. If you switch jobs mid-year and contribute to two 401(k)s, your total across both generally can't exceed the limit.

Employer contributions, such as a match, don't count toward your $24,500. They fall under a separate, higher overall limit on total contributions to your account.

## Catch-up contributions

If you're 50 or older by the end of the year, your plan may allow an extra **$8,000** catch-up contribution in 2026, for a potential total of $32,500 ($24,500 + $8,000).

People who turn 60, 61, 62, or 63 during the year may be eligible for a higher catch-up of **$11,250** instead of $8,000, if their plan allows it. That's a potential total of $35,750 ($24,500 + $11,250).

Starting in 2026, in plans that offer catch-up contributions, participants whose prior-year wages from that employer exceeded an IRS-set threshold must make their catch-up contributions on a Roth (after-tax) basis. IRS.gov has the details.

## IRAs

For 2026, you can contribute up to **$7,500** across all of your traditional and Roth IRAs combined. If you're 50 or older, you can add a **$1,100** catch-up, for $8,600 in total. You also can't contribute more than your taxable compensation for the year.

**Hypothetical example:** Someone age 40 with a traditional IRA and a Roth IRA could put $3,500 in one and $4,000 in the other for 2026, but not $7,500 in each.

## Roth IRA income limits

Roth IRAs limit contributions based on modified adjusted gross income (MAGI). Within the phase-out range, the allowed contribution shrinks gradually; above it, direct Roth IRA contributions aren't allowed. For 2026, the ranges are $153,000 to $168,000 for single filers and $242,000 to $252,000 for married couples filing jointly.

Traditional IRAs have no income limit for contributing, but the *deduction* can be reduced for people covered by a workplace plan above certain incomes. The IRS publishes those ranges each year.

## Why limits matter

Going over a limit creates an **excess contribution**, which can trigger penalties until it's corrected. For IRAs, the IRS applies a 6% tax each year an excess contribution remains in the account. Keeping track matters most if you have several accounts or change jobs during the year.

## Key takeaways
- For 2026, the 401(k)/403(b) employee limit is $24,500, and the IRA limit is $7,500 across all IRAs.
- Catch-up amounts for age 50 and older are $8,000 for workplace plans and $1,100 for IRAs, with a higher $11,250 workplace catch-up at ages 60-63.
- Roth IRA eligibility phases out between $153,000 and $168,000 (single) and $242,000 and $252,000 (married filing jointly).
- Limits usually change every year, so check IRS.gov before relying on any figure.
