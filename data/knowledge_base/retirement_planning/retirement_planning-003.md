Many employers that offer a 401(k) or similar workplace plan will add money to your account when you contribute. This is called an **employer match**, and it's one of the most talked-about perks in retirement saving. Understanding how your match works helps you see exactly what your employer is offering and what it takes to receive it.

## How a match works

A match is a formula. The employer promises to contribute a certain amount for every dollar you contribute, up to a cap that's usually expressed as a percentage of your pay. Two common styles:

- **Partial match:** For example, 50 cents for every dollar you contribute, on contributions up to 6% of your salary.
- **Dollar-for-dollar match:** For example, $1 for every $1 you contribute, on contributions up to 4% of your salary.

The formula varies widely from employer to employer, and some employers don't offer a match at all. Your plan documents or benefits team can tell you the exact rules.

## A hypothetical example

Suppose you earn $60,000 a year and your employer matches 50% of what you contribute, up to 6% of your pay. These numbers are hypothetical.

- If you contribute 6% ($3,600), your employer adds 50% of that: $1,800.
- If you contribute 3% ($1,800), your employer adds $900.
- If you contribute 10% ($6,000), your employer still adds only $1,800, because the match stops at 6% of pay.
- If you contribute nothing, the match is $0.

In the first case, you'd have $5,400 going into your account for the year, even though only $3,600 came out of your paycheck. That's why the match is often described as part of your total compensation: it's pay you receive only if you participate.

## Vesting: when the match becomes yours

Your own contributions, and the earnings on them, are always 100% yours. Employer contributions can be different. Many plans use a **vesting schedule**, which means you earn ownership of employer money gradually or after a waiting period.

According to the U.S. Department of Labor, employers generally choose between two schedules for matching contributions (or a faster one):

- **Cliff vesting:** You own 0% of the match until you complete three years of service, then 100%.
- **Graduated vesting:** Ownership rises in steps, reaching 100% after six years of service.

If you leave before you're fully vested, you may forfeit the unvested part. Some plans, such as safe harbor 401(k)s, vest required employer contributions immediately. Checking your vesting schedule can be useful when you're thinking about changing jobs.

## Other details worth knowing

**Timing.** Plans differ in when the match is deposited, such as every pay period or less often. Your plan documents explain the timing and any conditions.

**Tax treatment.** Matching contributions to a traditional 401(k) aren't taxed when they go in. Like pre-tax contributions, they're taxed when withdrawn.

**Limits.** Employer contributions don't count toward your own 2026 employee contribution limit of $24,500, but the IRS sets a separate, higher overall cap on the combined total of employee and employer contributions to your account. See IRS.gov for the details.

## Why people pay attention to the match

A match is an immediate addition to your savings that you don't get any other way. Many people consider contributing at least enough to receive the full match as a baseline, though everyone's budget and priorities differ. The key is knowing your plan's formula so you can make an informed choice.

## Key takeaways
- An employer match adds money to your retirement account based on how much you contribute, up to a cap.
- In a hypothetical 50%-up-to-6% match on a $60,000 salary, contributing $3,600 brings in $1,800 from the employer.
- Your own contributions are always yours; employer money may vest over time, often up to three years (cliff) or six years (graduated).
- Match formulas, timing, and vesting rules vary, so read your plan documents.
