Traditional retirement accounts let money grow without yearly taxes for decades. But that tax deferral doesn't last forever. At a certain age, the IRS requires you to start taking money out each year so that it eventually gets taxed. These mandatory withdrawals are called **required minimum distributions**, or **RMDs**.

## Which accounts have RMDs?

RMD rules generally apply to:

- Traditional IRAs, SEP IRAs, and SIMPLE IRAs
- Workplace plans such as 401(k), 403(b), and 457(b) plans

Two important exceptions for the original owner:

- **Roth IRAs** don't require withdrawals while the owner is alive.
- **Designated Roth accounts** in 401(k) and 403(b) plans also don't require withdrawals while the owner is alive.

People who *inherit* retirement accounts, including Roth accounts, have their own distribution rules, which are more complex and aren't covered here.

## When do RMDs start?

RMDs begin at your **RMD age**, which depends on when you were born. Under the SECURE 2.0 Act, as clarified in IRS proposed regulations:

- **Born 1951-1959:** your RMD age is **73**.
- **Born in 1960 or later:** your RMD age is **75**.

Then:

- **First RMD deadline:** For IRAs, your first RMD is due by **April 1 of the year after you reach your RMD age**.
- **Later RMDs:** Every year after that, the RMD is due by **December 31**.

There's a catch with that first deadline. If you wait until April 1 of the following year, you'll also owe that year's regular RMD by December 31, which means two taxable withdrawals in the same tax year. Taking the first one in the year you reach your RMD age spreads them across two tax years.

**Still working?** For a workplace plan, if the plan allows it and you're still employed there, you may be able to delay RMDs from that plan until April 1 after the year you retire. This exception doesn't apply to IRAs, and some plans require distributions starting at your RMD age anyway.

## How the amount is calculated

The basic formula is:

**RMD = account balance on December 31 of the prior year / distribution period from an IRS life expectancy table**

Most account owners use the IRS **Uniform Lifetime Table**. The distribution period gets smaller as you age, so the percentage you must withdraw gradually rises.

**Hypothetical example:** Suppose an IRA was worth $500,000 on December 31, and, to keep the math simple, the table factor for the owner's age were 25. The RMD would be $500,000 / 25 = $20,000. (The factor here is illustrative; look up the actual factor for your age in the IRS table.)

The RMD is a *minimum*. You can always withdraw more.

## Multiple accounts

- **IRAs:** You calculate the RMD separately for each IRA, but you can take the combined total from any one or more of your IRAs.
- **403(b)s:** Similar to IRAs, the total can be taken from one or more 403(b) contracts.
- **401(k) and 457(b) plans:** Each plan's RMD must be taken from that plan.

Your IRA custodian or plan administrator may calculate the amount, but the account owner is ultimately responsible for taking the correct RMD.

## Taxes and penalties

RMDs from traditional accounts are generally taxed as ordinary income, except for any portion that was already taxed (such as nondeductible contributions). RMDs also can't be rolled over into another retirement account.

If you miss an RMD or take too little, the IRS may impose a **25% excise tax** on the amount you should have withdrawn. That drops to **10%** if you correct the shortfall within two years. Because the penalty can be significant, some people arrange automatic annual withdrawals with their account provider.

## Why RMDs matter for planning

RMDs can push retirees into higher tax brackets, especially if traditional account balances are large. That's one reason some people consider Roth accounts, which have no lifetime RMDs for the owner, as part of a long-term plan. Planning ahead can help you anticipate your future taxable income.

## Key takeaways
- RMDs are mandatory yearly withdrawals from traditional IRAs and workplace plans, starting at your RMD age: 73 if you were born 1951-1959, 75 if born in 1960 or later.
- The first RMD from an IRA is due by April 1 of the year after you reach your RMD age; later ones are due by December 31 each year.
- The amount equals the prior year-end balance divided by a factor from an IRS life expectancy table.
- Roth IRAs and designated Roth 401(k)/403(b) accounts have no RMDs during the owner's lifetime.
- Missing an RMD can trigger a 25% excise tax, reduced to 10% if corrected within two years.
