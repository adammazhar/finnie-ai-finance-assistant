A **Roth IRA** is an individual retirement account that flips the usual tax arrangement. Instead of getting a tax break when you put money in, you contribute money you've already paid tax on. In exchange, if you follow the rules, the money you take out in retirement, including all the growth, can be completely tax-free.

## The core trade-off

With a traditional IRA, you may get a deduction now and pay tax later. With a Roth IRA, it's the reverse:

- **Going in:** Contributions are not tax-deductible.
- **Growing:** Investments inside the account grow without annual taxes on dividends, interest, or gains.
- **Coming out:** **Qualified distributions** are tax-free, both the money you contributed and the earnings.

**Hypothetical example:** Suppose you contribute $5,000 a year to a Roth IRA for 30 years, $150,000 in total, and your investments grow the account to $400,000. If your withdrawals in retirement are qualified, the full $400,000 can come out without federal income tax, including the $250,000 of growth. These numbers are hypothetical, and actual returns could be higher or lower.

## What makes a distribution "qualified"?

According to the IRS, a Roth IRA withdrawal of contributions and earnings is qualified, and therefore tax-free, when the account has been held for at least five years **and** the withdrawal is made:

- on or after reaching age 59½,
- because of disability,
- after the owner's death, or
- for a qualified first-time home purchase (subject to a limit).

Withdrawals that don't meet these tests may have earnings taxed and possibly face an additional tax. The detailed rules are in IRS Publication 590-B.

## Who can contribute

You need taxable compensation, such as wages or self-employment income, to contribute. There's no maximum age: you can contribute to a Roth IRA at any age as long as you have eligible income.

Roth IRAs also have **income limits**. As your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) rises through a "phase-out range," the amount you're allowed to contribute shrinks until it reaches zero. For 2026:

| Filing status | Phase-out range (MAGI) |
|---|---|
| Single | $153,000 - $168,000 |
| Married filing jointly | $242,000 - $252,000 |

## How much you can contribute

For 2026, the combined limit for all your traditional and Roth IRAs is $7,500, plus a $1,100 catch-up contribution if you're 50 or older. That's one shared limit, not $7,500 per account. Limits generally change over time, so check IRS.gov for the current year.

## No required withdrawals during your lifetime

Traditional IRAs require you to start taking minimum distributions at a certain age. Roth IRAs don't: the IRS says the owner can leave money in a Roth IRA for as long as they live. Beneficiaries who inherit a Roth IRA do have distribution rules to follow.

## Why people consider a Roth IRA

- They expect their tax rate in retirement to be the same or higher than it is today, as many early-career workers do.
- They like the idea of having some tax-free money in retirement to balance taxable accounts.
- They value the flexibility of not being forced to withdraw at a certain age.

On the other hand, someone who expects a lower tax rate in retirement may find the upfront deduction of a traditional account more appealing. Neither is automatically better; it depends on your situation.

## Key takeaways
- Roth IRA contributions are made with after-tax money and aren't deductible.
- Qualified distributions, generally after five years and age 59½, are tax-free, including earnings.
- Eligibility phases out at higher incomes: $153,000-$168,000 (single) and $242,000-$252,000 (married filing jointly) for 2026.
- The 2026 IRA limit is $7,500 across all IRAs, plus $1,100 at 50 or older.
- Owners aren't required to take distributions from a Roth IRA during their lifetime.
