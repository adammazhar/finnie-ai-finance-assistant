A 401(k) is a retirement savings plan offered by many employers in the United States. It gets its odd name from the section of the tax code that created it. For many workers, it's the main way they save for retirement, because it combines automatic saving, tax benefits, and sometimes free money from the employer.

## The basic idea

When you join a 401(k), you choose a portion of each paycheck to contribute. Your employer takes that money out of your pay and deposits it into your own individual account within the plan. This is called an **elective deferral**, because you are electing to defer part of your salary into the plan.

Inside the account, you choose how the money is invested from a menu the plan offers. Menus commonly include mutual funds, index funds, and **target date funds**, which automatically shift from more stocks to more bonds as a chosen retirement year approaches. Your balance rises or falls with the performance of those investments, minus any fees.

## Traditional vs. Roth 401(k)

Many plans offer two tax treatments, and some let you split contributions between them.

- **Traditional 401(k):** Contributions come out of your pay before income tax, which lowers your taxable income today. The money grows tax-deferred, and withdrawals in retirement, including earnings, are taxed as ordinary income.
- **Roth 401(k):** Contributions are made with after-tax money, so there's no tax break today. If you meet the rules for a qualified distribution, withdrawals in retirement, including earnings, are tax-free.

**Hypothetical example:** Suppose you earn $50,000 and contribute $5,000 to a traditional 401(k). Your taxable wages for income tax purposes drop to about $45,000 for the year. With a Roth 401(k), your taxable wages would stay at $50,000, but qualified withdrawals later would be tax-free.

## Employer contributions

Employers can add money to your account too. The most common form is a **match**, where the employer contributes a certain amount for each dollar you put in, up to a limit. Some employers also make contributions regardless of whether you contribute. Employer contributions may be subject to a **vesting schedule**, meaning you may need to work there a certain number of years before that money is fully yours. Your own contributions are always yours.

## Contribution limits

The IRS caps how much you can contribute each year. For 2026, the employee contribution limit is $24,500, with extra catch-up amounts allowed for people age 50 and older. These limits usually change from year to year, so check IRS.gov for the current figures.

## Getting money out

A 401(k) is designed for retirement, so the rules discourage early withdrawals. Taking money out before age 59½ generally means paying regular income tax on the pre-tax portion plus a 10% additional tax, unless an exception applies. The IRS lists exceptions such as disability and certain other situations. Some plans also allow loans or hardship withdrawals, but these have their own rules and trade-offs.

Later in life, traditional 401(k) balances become subject to required minimum distributions, rules that make you start withdrawing at a certain age.

## Things to look for in your plan

- **Automatic enrollment:** Some plans enroll new workers automatically at a default rate. You can usually change the rate or opt out.
- **Fees:** Investment and administrative fees reduce your returns over time. Look for them in your plan's documents and statements.
- **Investment menu:** Compare the options' costs and how diversified they are.
- **Match formula and vesting:** Your plan documents explain exactly how the employer contribution works.

## Key takeaways
- A 401(k) lets you save part of each paycheck into an investment account sponsored by your employer.
- Traditional contributions cut taxes now; Roth contributions can mean tax-free qualified withdrawals later.
- Many employers add matching contributions, which may vest over time.
- The 2026 employee contribution limit is $24,500, and limits change over time.
- Withdrawals before 59½ generally face income tax plus a 10% additional tax unless an exception applies.
