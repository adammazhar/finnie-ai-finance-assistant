Retirement can feel like a problem for a much older version of yourself. But when it comes to saving for it, the calendar is one of the most powerful tools you have. Starting early gives your money more years to grow, which can make a surprisingly large difference by the time you stop working.

## Compounding: growth on top of growth

The main reason early saving matters is **compounding**. When your savings earn a return, that return gets added to your balance. The next year, you earn a return on the original money *and* on the earlier gains. Over many years, this "growth on growth" can end up larger than the money you actually put in.

Compounding works slowly at first and then speeds up. In the early years, most of your balance is money you contributed. Decades later, most of it may be accumulated growth. That's why the years at the *start* of your saving life are so valuable: they have the longest time to compound.

## A hypothetical example: two savers

Imagine two people. These numbers are purely hypothetical and assume a steady 6% annual return, which real investments don't deliver in a straight line.

- **Saver A** puts away $2,000 a year from age 25 to 65: 40 years, $80,000 contributed in total.
- **Saver B** puts away $2,000 a year from age 35 to 65: 30 years, $60,000 contributed in total.

Under these assumptions, Saver A ends up with roughly $328,000, while Saver B ends up with roughly $168,000. Saver A contributed only $20,000 more but finished with about twice as much. The extra ten years of compounding did most of the work.

Real markets rise and fall, and actual results could be much higher or lower. The point of the example isn't the exact dollar figures. It's the shape of the result: time matters a lot.

## Other advantages of starting early

**Smaller amounts can go further.** Someone who starts in their twenties can often reach a goal with smaller monthly contributions than someone who starts in their forties and has to catch up.

**Habits form.** Saving automatically from each paycheck, sometimes called "paying yourself first", becomes routine. Many workplace retirement plans make this easy by taking contributions straight from your pay.

**Room for ups and downs.** A longer time horizon gives you more time to ride out market declines before you need the money.

**Tax-advantaged accounts.** Retirement accounts like 401(k)s and IRAs offer tax benefits, but each has an annual contribution limit. Years when you don't contribute generally can't be made up later, so starting early means more years of using that tax-advantaged space.

**Employer matches.** If your employer matches part of your contributions, starting sooner means collecting that extra money for more years.

## What if you're starting later?

Starting later isn't a reason to give up. Many people begin saving seriously in their thirties, forties, or beyond. Later starters may need to save a larger share of their income, work a bit longer, or adjust their retirement expectations. The tax code also allows extra "catch-up" contributions for people age 50 and older. The best time to start was years ago; the next best time is usually now.

## Small steps count

You don't need a large salary to begin. Many people start with a small percentage of their pay and increase it with each raise. Investor.gov offers a free compound interest calculator where you can try your own hypothetical numbers and see how time changes the outcome.

## Key takeaways
- Compounding means your returns can earn returns, and it needs time to work.
- In a hypothetical example, starting ten years earlier roughly doubled the ending balance with only modestly more money contributed.
- Starting early lets smaller contributions go further and builds a lasting saving habit.
- Late starters still have options, such as saving more, working longer, or making catch-up contributions after 50.
