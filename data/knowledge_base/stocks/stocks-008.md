Companies sometimes change the number of shares they have outstanding. Two common ways they do this are **stock splits** and **share buybacks**. They make headlines often, but they work very differently. A split changes how a company's value is sliced up without changing the value itself. A buyback uses company cash to reduce the number of shares, which changes each remaining shareholder's slice.

## Stock splits

In a **stock split**, a company divides each existing share into multiple new shares. The share price drops in proportion, so the total value of what you own stays the same.

A hypothetical example: you own 10 shares at $300 each, worth $3,000. The company announces a 3-for-1 split. Afterward you own 30 shares at $100 each, still worth $3,000. Your percentage ownership of the company is unchanged, and so is the company's market capitalization. If the company pays a dividend, the dividend per share is adjusted down by the same ratio, so your total dividend income is unchanged too.

Think of it like cutting a pizza into more slices. You have more pieces, but not more pizza.

### Why companies split

- **Lower share price.** A lower price per share can make the stock feel more accessible, especially to investors who buy whole shares.
- **Trading activity.** Some companies believe a lower price attracts more buyers and makes trading smoother.

With many brokers now offering fractional shares, a high share price is less of a barrier than it once was. A split does not change the company's earnings, assets, or prospects. Any price move after a split announcement reflects investor reaction, not new value created by the split.

### Reverse splits

A **reverse split** does the opposite: it combines shares. In a 1-for-10 reverse split, 100 shares at $1 become 10 shares at $10. Companies sometimes do this to raise a low share price, for example to meet an exchange's minimum price requirement to stay listed. A reverse split can be a sign that a company has been struggling, although that is not always the case.

## Share buybacks

In a **share buyback** (or repurchase), a company uses its own cash to buy its shares, usually on the open market. The repurchased shares are typically retired or held by the company, so fewer shares remain outstanding.

A hypothetical example: a company earns $100 million and has 100 million shares, so earnings per share (EPS) is $1. If it buys back 10 million shares and earnings stay the same, EPS becomes $100 million / 90 million, about $1.11. Each remaining share now represents a slightly larger piece of the company.

### Why companies buy back shares

- **Returning cash to shareholders.** Buybacks are an alternative to dividends. Instead of receiving cash, shareholders who do not sell end up owning a larger percentage of the company.
- **Signaling confidence.** Management may be signaling that it thinks the stock is undervalued.
- **Offsetting dilution.** Companies that give stock to employees often buy shares back to keep the share count from creeping up.

### Criticisms and cautions

Buybacks are not automatically good. If a company pays a high price for its own shares, it may destroy value rather than create it. Buybacks funded by borrowing add debt to the balance sheet. Because buybacks raise EPS even when total profits do not grow, a rising EPS number can look better than the underlying business really is. Some critics also argue the cash could be better used for investment, wages, or paying down debt.

## Comparing the two

A split changes the number of shares but leaves ownership percentages and company value alone. A buyback reduces the number of shares, spends company cash, and raises each remaining shareholder's ownership percentage. Neither one, by itself, makes the business more profitable.

## Key takeaways
- A stock split increases the number of shares and lowers the price proportionally; total value stays the same.
- A reverse split combines shares and raises the price per share, sometimes to meet exchange listing rules.
- A buyback uses company cash to reduce the number of shares outstanding.
- Buybacks raise earnings per share even if total profits do not change, so look at total profits too.
- Neither splits nor buybacks change how profitable the underlying business is.
