Market capitalization, usually shortened to **market cap**, is the total market value of all of a company's shares. It is one of the simplest ways to describe how big a company is in the eyes of the stock market, and it is used to sort stocks into groups such as large caps, mid caps, and small caps.

## How market cap is calculated

The formula is straightforward:

**Market cap = share price x number of shares outstanding**

"Shares outstanding" means all the shares currently held by investors, including company insiders.

A hypothetical example: Company A has 100 million shares and each trades at $40. Its market cap is $4 billion. Company B has only 10 million shares, but each trades at $200. Its market cap is $2 billion.

Notice that Company B has the higher share price but is the smaller company. A high share price on its own tells you very little about a company's size. Market cap is the better comparison because it accounts for how many shares exist.

Market cap moves every time the share price moves. If Company A's shares fall from $40 to $30, its market cap drops from $4 billion to $3 billion, even though nothing about the business may have changed that day.

## The size categories

Stocks are commonly grouped into size buckets. There are no fixed cutoff points, and different index providers and fund companies draw the lines in different places, but one common convention is:

- **Large cap:** about $10 billion or more. These are typically well-known, established companies.
- **Mid cap:** roughly $2 billion to $10 billion. Often companies that are still expanding.
- **Small cap:** under roughly $2 billion. Often younger or more specialized businesses.
- **Micro cap:** even smaller companies at the bottom of the small-cap range. These can be very small and lightly traded.

You may also hear "mega cap" for the very largest companies. Some sources set the cutoffs as much as twice as high. Because the boundaries are approximate and markets change, it is best to treat these labels as general descriptions rather than precise cutoffs.

## How the groups tend to behave

Each size group has typical tendencies, though individual companies vary widely.

**Large caps** tend to have steadier revenue, more access to financing, and more analysts and journalists following them. Their prices usually swing less than those of smaller companies, and many pay dividends. The trade-off is that a giant company may find it harder to keep growing quickly.

**Mid caps** sit in between. They have often proven their business model but still have room to grow.

**Small caps** can offer more room to grow, since a small company can double in size more easily than a huge one. They also tend to be more volatile, more sensitive to economic slowdowns, and less widely researched. Their shares may trade less often, which can make prices jump around more. The smallest companies carry extra risk, including less available information.

## Why market cap matters to investors

Market cap is used in a few important ways:

- **Diversification.** Many investors think about holding a mix of company sizes, since large and small companies do not always move together.
- **Fund labels.** Funds often describe themselves by size, such as a "large-cap fund" or a "small-cap index fund."
- **Index weighting.** Many stock market indexes are **market-cap weighted**, meaning bigger companies make up a bigger share of the index. In such an index, a handful of the largest companies can have an outsized effect on its returns.

Market cap measures what the stock market thinks a company's shares are worth. It is not the same as the company's revenue, its profits, or even the total value of the business including its debt.

## Key takeaways
- Market cap equals the share price times the number of shares outstanding.
- A high share price does not mean a company is large; market cap is the better size measure.
- One common convention is large cap ($10 billion+), mid cap ($2 to $10 billion), and small cap (under about $2 billion), but definitions vary.
- Smaller companies tend to be more volatile and less researched than larger ones.
- Many indexes weight companies by market cap, so the largest firms have the biggest influence.
