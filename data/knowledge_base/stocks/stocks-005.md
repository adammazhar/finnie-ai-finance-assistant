A **dividend** is a payment a company makes to its shareholders, usually out of its profits. For many investors, dividends are a way to receive regular income from stocks without selling any shares. Understanding how dividends work, and how to read the **dividend yield**, helps you compare income-paying stocks sensibly.

## How dividends work

When a company earns a profit, its board of directors decides what to do with the money. It can reinvest in the business, pay down debt, buy back shares, or pay some of it to shareholders as a dividend.

Most U.S. companies that pay dividends do so on a regular schedule, often quarterly. Occasionally a company pays a one-time **special dividend**, for example after an unusually profitable year. Dividends are usually paid in cash, though some companies pay them in additional shares.

Dividends are **not guaranteed**. The board can raise, cut, or eliminate a dividend at any time. Companies that run into trouble often reduce their dividends to save cash.

## Important dates

A few dates determine who receives a dividend:

- **Declaration date:** the company announces the dividend amount and schedule.
- **Ex-dividend date:** you must own the stock before this date to receive the upcoming dividend. Buy on or after it, and the payment goes to the seller.
- **Record date:** the company checks its records to see who the shareholders are.
- **Payment date:** the cash arrives in shareholders' accounts.

On the ex-dividend date, the share price may fall by about the amount of the dividend, because new buyers will not receive it. This is why buying a stock just to "capture" a dividend usually does not create free money.

## Dividend yield

The **dividend yield** shows how much a stock pays in dividends each year relative to its price:

**Dividend yield = annual dividends per share / share price**

A hypothetical example: a stock trades at $50 and pays $0.50 per quarter, or $2 per year. Its dividend yield is 2 / 50 = **4%**. If you invested $1,000 at that price, you would expect about $40 a year in dividends, as long as the company keeps paying the same amount.

Because the share price is in the denominator, the yield changes as the price changes. If that stock fell to $40 while the dividend stayed at $2, the yield would rise to 5%.

## Why a high yield is not always good

An unusually high yield can be a warning sign. Often it is high because the share price has dropped sharply, which may mean investors fear the business is struggling and the dividend could be cut. When a company cuts its dividend, the stock can fall further. Some investors look at the **payout ratio**, the share of earnings paid as dividends, to judge whether a dividend looks sustainable. A company paying out more than it earns may not be able to keep that up.

## Growth companies and dividends

Many fast-growing companies pay little or no dividend. They prefer to reinvest their profits to expand, hoping to raise the share price over time. More mature companies, such as established utilities or consumer brands, are more likely to pay steady dividends. Neither approach is automatically better; they reflect different stages and strategies.

## Reinvesting dividends

Many brokers and companies offer **dividend reinvestment plans (DRIPs)**, which use your dividends to buy more shares automatically. Over long periods, reinvested dividends can make up a meaningful part of a stock's total return, because each reinvested payment buys more shares that can pay dividends of their own.

## A note on taxes

In a regular taxable account, dividends are generally taxable in the year you receive them, even if you reinvest them. Some dividends, called qualified dividends, may be taxed at the lower long-term capital gains rates if certain holding requirements are met. Rules are detailed and change over time, so IRS Publication 550 is the place to confirm current treatment.

## Key takeaways
- A dividend is a payment from a company to its shareholders, usually from profits, and it can be cut at any time.
- You must own the stock before the ex-dividend date to receive the next dividend.
- Dividend yield equals annual dividends per share divided by the share price.
- A very high yield can signal a falling share price and a dividend at risk.
- Reinvesting dividends can add meaningfully to long-term total return.
