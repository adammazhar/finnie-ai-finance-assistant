The price-to-earnings ratio, or **P/E ratio**, is one of the most widely quoted numbers in investing. It answers a simple question: how many dollars are investors paying today for each dollar the company earns in profit? It gives a quick sense of whether a stock looks expensive or cheap compared with its own history or with similar companies.

## How the P/E ratio is calculated

First you need **earnings per share (EPS)**, which is the company's profit divided by the number of shares outstanding. Then:

**P/E ratio = share price / earnings per share**

A hypothetical example: a company earned $100 million over the past year and has 50 million shares. Its EPS is $2. If the stock trades at $30, its P/E ratio is 30 / 2 = **15**. Investors are paying $15 for every $1 of annual earnings.

Another way to think about it: if earnings stayed flat forever and all were paid to you, it would take about 15 years of earnings to "earn back" the price. That is a rough mental picture, not a real forecast, since earnings change over time.

## Trailing and forward P/E

- **Trailing P/E** uses actual earnings from the past 12 months. It is based on real reported numbers, but it looks backward.
- **Forward P/E** uses analysts' estimates of earnings for the next 12 months. It looks ahead, but it depends on predictions that can turn out wrong.

When you see a P/E quoted, it is worth checking which version it is.

## What a high or low P/E can mean

A **high P/E** often means investors expect earnings to grow quickly, so they are willing to pay more today. It can also mean the stock is simply popular and priced optimistically.

A **low P/E** can signal that a stock is inexpensive relative to its earnings. It can also mean investors expect earnings to shrink, or see problems ahead. A low P/E is not automatically a bargain; sometimes a stock is cheap for a good reason.

This is why the P/E ratio is most useful as a **comparison tool** rather than a verdict. Compare a company with:

- Its own P/E over past years.
- Competitors in the same industry.
- The broader market's average P/E.

Industries differ a lot. Fast-growing technology companies often carry higher P/E ratios than utilities or banks, so comparing a software company's P/E to a power company's P/E is not very meaningful.

## Limitations to keep in mind

**No earnings, no useful P/E.** If a company lost money, its EPS is negative and the P/E ratio is not meaningful. Many young companies fall into this group.

**Earnings can be distorted.** One-time events, such as selling a division or taking a large write-off, can make a single year's earnings unusually high or low, which skews the ratio. Accounting choices also affect reported earnings.

**It ignores growth rates and debt.** Two companies with the same P/E can have very different growth prospects or debt loads. Some investors use related measures, such as the PEG ratio (P/E divided by the expected earnings growth rate), to add growth into the picture.

**Estimates can be wrong.** A forward P/E is only as good as the earnings forecast behind it.

## The earnings yield

Flipping the ratio gives the **earnings yield**: earnings per share divided by price. In the example above, $2 / $30 is about 6.7%. Some investors use this to compare stocks with the yields available on bonds, though the two are not directly equivalent because stock earnings are not guaranteed.

## Key takeaways
- The P/E ratio is the share price divided by earnings per share.
- Trailing P/E uses past earnings; forward P/E uses estimates of future earnings.
- A high P/E often reflects growth expectations; a low P/E may signal value or trouble.
- P/E is most useful for comparing a company with its history, its peers, and the market.
- It is not meaningful for companies without profits and should be combined with other measures.
