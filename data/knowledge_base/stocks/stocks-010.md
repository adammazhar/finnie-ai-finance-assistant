Four times a year, most public companies in the U.S. report how their business performed over the previous quarter. These **earnings reports** are some of the most closely watched events in the stock market. A stock can rise or fall sharply in a single day after one. Understanding why helps make those moves less mysterious.

## What is in an earnings report

Companies usually release results in a press release, followed by a conference call where executives discuss the numbers and answer analysts' questions. The formal quarterly filing with the SEC is the **Form 10-Q** (the annual version is the 10-K). Key items include:

- **Revenue:** total sales for the quarter.
- **Earnings per share (EPS):** profit divided by the number of shares.
- **Margins:** how much of each dollar of sales becomes profit.
- **Guidance:** management's outlook for upcoming quarters or the full year, if the company provides one.
- **Business details:** figures that matter for that company, such as customer counts, store sales, or subscriptions.

The weeks when many companies report are known as **earnings season**.

## Expectations are what matter

Before a company reports, stock analysts publish estimates of what they think revenue and EPS will be. The average of these is often called the **consensus estimate**. The stock price before the report already reflects what investors broadly expect.

So the market reaction depends less on whether results are "good" and more on whether they are **better or worse than expected**.

- A company that **beats** expectations reports results above the consensus.
- A company that **misses** reports results below it.
- The gap is called an **earnings surprise**.

A hypothetical example: analysts expect EPS of $1.00. The company reports $1.10, a 10% beat. The stock might jump. But suppose a different company was expected to earn $1.00, reports $1.05, and the stock falls anyway. That can happen if revenue missed, if margins shrank, or if the company lowered its guidance for next year.

## Why guidance often moves stocks the most

A stock's price is largely a bet on the future, and last quarter is already in the past. When management says it expects stronger or weaker results ahead, investors revise their estimates of future profits, and the price adjusts. A company can beat this quarter's estimate yet fall sharply because its outlook disappointed.

## Why the moves can be so large

Earnings reports deliver a burst of new information all at once. Many investors react at the same moment, and the price can **gap** up or down, meaning it opens far from the previous close. Reports are often released outside regular trading hours, so the first reaction appears in after-hours or pre-market trading, where fewer shares change hands and prices can jump around more.

Other factors that can amplify a reaction:

- **High expectations.** Popular stocks with high valuations can fall hard on small disappointments.
- **Surprises in quality.** A beat driven by a one-time gain or a tax benefit may not impress investors.
- **Industry signals.** One company's results can move competitors' stocks too, because they hint at conditions across the industry.

## Keeping perspective

Single-quarter reactions can be dramatic, but one report is a small piece of a company's long-term story. Trying to predict how a stock will react to earnings is very difficult, even for professionals, because it requires guessing both the results and how the market will interpret them. Long-term investors often focus more on trends across many quarters, such as steady revenue growth or improving cash flow, than on any single beat or miss.

## Key takeaways
- Most U.S. public companies report earnings quarterly and file a Form 10-Q (or, for the full year, a Form 10-K) with the SEC.
- Stock reactions depend on results versus expectations, not on results alone.
- Guidance about future quarters often moves a stock more than last quarter's numbers.
- Prices can gap sharply because a lot of new information arrives at once, often outside regular hours.
- Trends across many quarters usually say more about a business than any single report.
