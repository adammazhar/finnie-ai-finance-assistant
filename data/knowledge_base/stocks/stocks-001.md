A stock is a small slice of ownership in a company. When you own a share, you own a fraction of that business: a tiny claim on its assets and on the profits it earns. Stocks are also called **equities**, because they represent equity, or ownership, rather than a loan.

## Why companies sell stock

Businesses need money to grow. They might want to build a new factory, launch a product, expand into a new region, or pay down debt. One way to raise that money is to sell ownership stakes to the public. The first time a company does this is called an **initial public offering (IPO)**. After the IPO, the shares trade between investors on a stock exchange, and the company itself is usually no longer part of each trade.

Selling stock is different from borrowing. A company that takes out a loan or issues a bond has to pay the money back with interest. A company that sells stock does not promise to repay shareholders anything. Instead, shareholders share in whatever success or failure the business has.

## How shareholders can make money

There are two main ways a stock can reward its owner:

- **Price appreciation (capital gains).** If the company becomes more valuable, other investors may be willing to pay more for its shares. Selling for more than you paid produces a gain.
- **Dividends.** Some companies pay part of their profits to shareholders in cash, often every quarter. Many younger, fast-growing companies pay no dividend and reinvest their profits instead.

A hypothetical example: suppose you buy 10 shares at $50 each, for $500 total. Over a few years the company grows, and the price rises to $70. Your shares are now worth $700. If the company also paid $1 per share in dividends each year, you would have collected $10 a year in cash along the way. Of course, the price could just as easily have fallen to $30, leaving your shares worth $300.

## Common and preferred stock

Most shares people buy are **common stock**. Common shareholders usually get to vote on certain company matters, such as electing the board of directors, and they receive dividends if the board declares them.

**Preferred stock** works a little more like a bond. Preferred shareholders typically receive a set dividend and get paid before common shareholders, but they usually have no voting rights and less potential for the share price to rise. Some companies also issue different classes of common stock with different voting power.

## The risks of owning stock

Owning a stock means accepting that its value can change a lot, and quickly. Prices can fall because of weak company results, a slowing economy, rising interest rates, problems in an industry, or simply shifting investor mood. If a company goes bankrupt, shareholders are last in line, behind lenders and bondholders, and they often receive nothing.

Because a single company can struggle or fail, many investors spread their money across many stocks, often through mutual funds or exchange-traded funds (ETFs). This is called **diversification**. It does not remove risk, but it reduces the damage any one company can do.

Over long periods, stocks as a group have historically returned more than bonds or cash. That higher long-run return has come with bigger ups and downs along the way, which is why stocks are generally considered better suited to money that will not be needed for many years.

## How people buy stocks

Most individuals buy and sell stocks through a brokerage account. You place an order, the broker sends it to the market, and the trade is matched with another investor. Some companies also offer direct stock purchase plans or dividend reinvestment plans that let shareholders buy small amounts directly.

## Key takeaways
- A stock is a share of ownership in a company, not a loan to it.
- Shareholders can earn money through rising share prices and through dividends, but neither is promised.
- Common stock usually carries voting rights; preferred stock usually pays a set dividend but has limited upside.
- Stock prices can fall sharply, and shareholders are paid last if a company fails.
- Holding many stocks, often through funds, spreads out the risk of any single company.
