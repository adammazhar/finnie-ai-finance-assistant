Public companies in the U.S. must regularly report their financial results. These reports tell you where a company's money comes from, where it goes, and what the company owns and owes. You do not need to be an accountant to get useful information from them. Knowing the three main statements, and what each one answers, is a strong foundation.

## Where to find them

Companies file an annual report called the **Form 10-K** and quarterly reports called **Form 10-Q** with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). You can read them free on the SEC's EDGAR database or on a company's investor relations website. The 10-K includes financial statements audited by an independent accounting firm; the 10-Q includes unaudited financial statements.

## The income statement: did the company make money?

The **income statement** (also called the profit and loss statement) covers a period of time, such as a quarter or a year. It works like a staircase, starting with sales and subtracting costs step by step:

- **Revenue:** money earned from selling products or services.
- **Cost of goods sold:** the direct cost of making those products.
- **Gross profit:** revenue minus cost of goods sold.
- **Operating expenses:** salaries, rent, marketing, research, and so on.
- **Operating income:** gross profit minus operating expenses.
- **Net income:** what is left after interest and taxes, the "bottom line."

A hypothetical example: revenue of $1,000,000, cost of goods of $600,000, operating expenses of $250,000, and interest and taxes of $50,000 leaves net income of $100,000. Dividing net income by shares outstanding gives **earnings per share**.

## The balance sheet: what does it own and owe?

The **balance sheet** is a snapshot on a single date. It follows one equation:

**Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' equity**

- **Assets** are what the company owns: cash, inventory, money owed by customers, buildings, equipment.
- **Liabilities** are what it owes: bills to suppliers, loans, bonds.
- **Shareholders' equity** is what would be left for owners if all assets were sold and all debts paid.

Assets and liabilities are usually split into **current** (expected to be used or paid within a year) and **long-term**. Comparing current assets with current liabilities gives a sense of whether the company can pay its near-term bills.

## The cash flow statement: where did the cash go?

Profit and cash are not the same thing. A company can report a profit while customers have not yet paid, or show a loss partly because of non-cash charges. The **cash flow statement** tracks actual cash in three buckets:

- **Operating activities:** cash from running the core business.
- **Investing activities:** buying or selling equipment, property, or other businesses.
- **Financing activities:** borrowing, repaying debt, issuing or buying back shares, and paying dividends.

A business that consistently produces positive cash from operations is generally in a stronger position than one that relies on borrowing to stay afloat.

## Don't skip the notes and the discussion

The **footnotes** explain accounting methods, debts, lawsuits, and other details that the tables cannot show. The section called **Management's Discussion and Analysis (MD&A)** is where management explains the results in plain words, including risks and trends. Both can reveal issues that the headline numbers hide.

## Simple questions to ask

When looking at statements, compare periods rather than reading one year in isolation:

- Is revenue growing, flat, or shrinking?
- Are profit margins rising or falling?
- Is debt growing faster than the business?
- Does operating cash flow roughly keep pace with net income?

Financial statements describe the past. They are an important input for understanding a company, but they cannot tell you what the stock will do next.

## Key takeaways
- Public companies file annual (10-K) and quarterly (10-Q) reports with the SEC, available free on EDGAR.
- The income statement shows revenue, expenses, and profit over a period.
- The balance sheet shows assets, liabilities, and shareholders' equity on one date.
- The cash flow statement shows actual cash from operating, investing, and financing activities.
- Footnotes, management's discussion, and trends over several periods add essential context.
