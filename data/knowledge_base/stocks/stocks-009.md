When you buy or sell a stock or ETF through a brokerage account, you choose an **order type**. The order type tells your broker how to handle the trade: whether to act immediately at whatever price is available, wait for a specific price, or spring into action only if the price moves past a certain level. Picking the right one is about balancing two things: certainty that the trade happens, and control over the price you get.

## Market orders

A **market order** asks to buy or sell right away at the best price currently available.

- **Pro:** It will almost always be filled quickly, as long as the stock is trading.
- **Con:** You do not control the exact price. A buy market order generally fills near the current **ask** (the lowest price sellers are asking), and a sell order near the current **bid** (the highest price buyers are offering).

The last price you saw on the screen is not necessarily the price you will get. In a fast-moving market, or for a stock that trades infrequently, the fill price can differ noticeably from what you expected. This difference is sometimes called **slippage**.

## Limit orders

A **limit order** sets the worst price you are willing to accept.

- A **buy limit** order will only execute at your limit price or lower.
- A **sell limit** order will only execute at your limit price or higher.

A hypothetical example: a stock trades around $52, and you are only willing to pay up to $50. You place a buy limit order at $50. If the price drops to $50 or below, the order can fill. If the price never gets there, nothing happens.

- **Pro:** You control the price.
- **Con:** There is no guarantee the order will fill, even if the stock briefly touches your price, because other orders may be ahead of yours.

Limit orders can be set to expire at the end of the day or remain open for a longer period, depending on your broker's options.

## Stop orders (stop-loss orders)

A **stop order** stays inactive until the stock reaches a trigger price, called the **stop price**. Once triggered, it becomes a **market order**.

- A **sell stop** is placed below the current price. Investors often use it to limit a loss or protect a gain on a stock they own.
- A **buy stop** is placed above the current price, often used by investors who have sold a stock short.

A hypothetical example: you own a stock bought at $40 that now trades at $50. You set a sell stop at $45. If the price falls to $45, your order becomes a market order and sells at the next available price.

The catch is that the next available price may be lower than your stop price. If a stock closes at $46 and opens the next morning at $38 after bad news, your stop triggers and sells near $38, not $45. A stop order does not guarantee your exit price.

## Stop-limit orders

A **stop-limit order** combines the two. When the stop price is reached, it becomes a **limit order** instead of a market order.

Using the example above, you might set a stop at $45 with a limit of $44. Once triggered, the order will only sell at $44 or higher. This protects you from selling at a much lower price, but if the stock gaps down past $44, the order may never fill, and you could keep holding as the price keeps falling.

## Choosing among them

| Order type | Price control | Likely to fill? |
|---|---|---|
| Market | None | Very likely |
| Limit | Full | Only if price reached |
| Stop | None once triggered | Likely once triggered |
| Stop-limit | Full once triggered | Not guaranteed |

Market orders prioritize speed; limit orders prioritize price. Some brokers offer additional order types and conditions. It is worth reading your broker's descriptions carefully, because details can differ.

## Key takeaways
- A market order executes quickly but does not guarantee the price.
- A limit order guarantees the price or better but may never execute.
- A stop order becomes a market order once the stop price is reached, and can fill well below the stop in a fast drop.
- A stop-limit order becomes a limit order when triggered, which protects price but risks no fill.
- The main trade-off is between certainty of execution and control over price.
