Nobody sets the price of a stock by decree. The price you see on a screen is simply the most recent price at which a buyer and a seller agreed to trade. Every second the market is open, millions of investors with different opinions are deciding what they are willing to pay or accept, and the price moves as those opinions change.

## Buyers, sellers, and the bid-ask spread

At any moment, a stock has two important prices:

- The **bid** is the highest price a buyer is currently willing to pay.
- The **ask** (or offer) is the lowest price a seller is currently willing to accept.

The gap between them is the **bid-ask spread**. A trade happens when someone is willing to meet the other side. For heavily traded stocks the spread tends to be narrow; for thinly traded stocks it can be much wider.

Here is a hypothetical example. Suppose the best bid for a stock is $49.98 and the best ask is $50.02. If an eager buyer agrees to pay $50.02, a trade occurs and the "last price" becomes $50.02. If instead a seller in a hurry accepts $49.98, the last price becomes $49.98. Neither price is the "true" value; each is just where a deal was struck.

## Supply and demand

Underneath all the trading is basic supply and demand. If more investors want to buy a stock than want to sell it at the current price, buyers have to offer more to find willing sellers, and the price rises. If more investors want to sell than buy, sellers have to accept less, and the price falls.

So the real question is: what makes people want to buy or sell?

## What drives investor demand

**Company fundamentals.** Over the long run, a stock's price tends to follow the company's ability to earn profits. A share is a claim on future earnings, so news that changes expectations about those earnings, such as a strong new product, a lost customer, or a quarterly earnings report, can move the price.

**Expectations, not just results.** Prices reflect what investors expect to happen, not only what has happened. A company can report record profits and still see its stock fall if investors had expected even more.

**Interest rates and the economy.** When interest rates rise, safer investments like bonds and savings accounts pay more, and future company profits are worth less in today's dollars. That can pull stock prices down broadly. A growing economy tends to support corporate profits, while a slowing one can weigh on them.

**Industry and competition.** Changes in regulation, technology, or competition can lift or hurt a whole group of companies at once.

**Investor sentiment.** Fear, excitement, and herd behavior can push prices well above or below what the underlying business seems to justify, at least for a while. Short-term price moves often have more to do with mood and news flow than with long-term value.

## Price versus value

It helps to separate a stock's **price** from the company's **value**. Price is what the market pays today. Value is an estimate of what the business is actually worth, based on things like its earnings, assets, and growth prospects. Investors use tools such as the price-to-earnings ratio to compare the two. Because estimates of value differ from person to person, the same stock can look cheap to one investor and expensive to another, and that disagreement is exactly what allows trades to happen.

## Why prices can swing so much

Stock prices react quickly to new information. When surprising news arrives, investors rush to adjust, and the price may jump or drop within minutes. Stocks with fewer shares trading can move even more sharply, because it takes fewer orders to shift the balance between buyers and sellers. These swings are a normal part of owning stocks, though they can be unsettling.

## Key takeaways
- A stock's price is the latest price at which a buyer and a seller agreed to trade.
- The bid is the best price buyers offer; the ask is the best price sellers accept; the gap is the spread.
- Prices rise when buying pressure exceeds selling pressure, and fall when the reverse is true.
- Earnings expectations, interest rates, the economy, and investor sentiment all influence demand.
- Market price and a company's underlying value are related but not the same thing.
