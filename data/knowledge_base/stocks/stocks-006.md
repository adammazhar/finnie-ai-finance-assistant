"Growth" and "value" are two of the most common labels used to describe stocks and the funds that hold them. They describe different reasons investors are drawn to a company. Neither style is right or wrong, and many investors own some of both. Understanding the difference helps you read fund names and understand why different parts of the market can behave differently.

## What is a growth stock?

A **growth stock** belongs to a company whose sales or earnings are expected to grow faster than the average company. Investors buy growth stocks mainly hoping the share price will rise as the business expands.

Common traits include:

- **High valuations.** Growth stocks often have high price-to-earnings (P/E) ratios, because investors are paying today for profits they expect in the future.
- **Little or no dividend.** Growth companies usually reinvest profits into research, hiring, and expansion rather than paying them out.
- **Big price swings.** Because so much of the price depends on future expectations, even small changes in those expectations can move the stock sharply.

A hypothetical example: a young software company earns $1 per share and trades at $60, a P/E of 60. Investors accept that high price because they expect earnings to grow quickly for years. If growth comes in slower than hoped, the stock could fall hard even if the company is still profitable.

## What is a value stock?

A **value stock** is one that appears inexpensive compared with measures of the company's worth, such as its earnings, assets, or cash flow. Value investors look for companies the market may be underrating, hoping the price will eventually move closer to what they believe the business is worth.

Common traits include:

- **Lower valuations.** Value stocks tend to have lower P/E ratios and lower price-to-book ratios.
- **Often more mature businesses.** Many are established companies in slower-growing industries.
- **More likely to pay dividends.** Steady, mature companies often return cash to shareholders.

A hypothetical example: an established manufacturer earns $5 per share and trades at $50, a P/E of 10. Investors may be pessimistic about its industry. If the company performs better than feared, the price could rise. If the pessimism was justified, the stock may stay cheap or fall further.

## The risks on each side

Each style carries its own main risk:

- **Growth risk: paying too much.** When expectations are very high, disappointments can cause steep drops. Some expected growth never arrives.
- **Value risk: the "value trap."** A stock can look cheap because the business really is in decline. A low price is not a bargain if the company keeps shrinking.

## Why the styles take turns

Historically, growth and value have taken turns leading the market, sometimes for many years at a time. Growth stocks have tended to do well in some periods, such as when investors are especially enthusiastic about new technologies, while value stocks have done better in others. Changes in interest rates, economic conditions, and investor sentiment all play a part. Because it is very hard to predict which style will lead next, many investors hold a blend rather than trying to time the switch.

## Where the labels come from

There is no single official definition. Index providers and fund companies each use their own formulas, typically combining measures such as P/E, price-to-book, earnings growth, and sales growth. A stock can be classed as growth by one provider and value by another, and a company can move from one category to the other over time. Some stocks fall in between and are called **blend** or core holdings.

Many funds are labeled by both size and style, such as "large-cap growth" or "small-cap value." Reading the fund's prospectus shows exactly how it defines those terms.

## Key takeaways
- Growth stocks are expected to grow faster than average and often have high P/E ratios and small or no dividends.
- Value stocks look inexpensive relative to earnings or assets and are often mature, dividend-paying companies.
- Growth investors risk overpaying; value investors risk buying a "value trap."
- Growth and value have historically taken turns outperforming, often for years at a time.
- Definitions vary by index provider, so check how a fund defines its style.
