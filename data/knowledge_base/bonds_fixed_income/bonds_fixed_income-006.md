The U.S. government borrows money by selling debt securities known as **Treasuries**. Because they are backed by the full faith and credit of the U.S. government, Treasuries are widely regarded as having very low default risk, and their yields serve as a benchmark for interest rates across the economy. The three core types, bills, notes, and bonds, differ mainly in how long they last and how they pay interest.

## Treasury bills: short-term

**Treasury bills** (T-bills) mature in one year or less. The Treasury sells them for terms ranging from 4 weeks to 52 weeks.

T-bills do not pay a separate interest check. Instead, they are usually sold at a **discount** to face value, and you receive the full face value at maturity. The difference is your interest.

Here is a hypothetical example. You buy a 26-week bill with a $1,000 face value for $980. About six months later, you receive $1,000. Your $20 gain is the interest.

Because they are short-term, T-bills are much less sensitive to interest rate changes than longer Treasuries. They are often used for cash that may be needed within a year.

## Treasury notes: medium-term

**Treasury notes** are issued with terms of 2, 3, 5, 7, or 10 years. They pay a fixed rate of interest every six months until they mature, then return the face value.

Hypothetical example: a $1,000, 5-year note with a 4% interest rate pays $20 every six months ($40 a year) for five years, then returns $1,000.

The 10-year Treasury note is closely watched. Its yield influences mortgage rates and is commonly quoted in the news as a gauge of long-term borrowing costs.

## Treasury bonds: long-term

**Treasury bonds** are issued with terms of 20 or 30 years. Like notes, they pay fixed interest every six months and return face value at maturity. Treasury bonds are different from U.S. savings bonds such as EE and I bonds, which are a separate program.

Because of their long lifespans, Treasury bonds are the most sensitive of the three to changes in interest rates. If rates rise after you buy, the market price of a 30-year bond can drop considerably, even though the government is still expected to make every payment.

## Other Treasury securities

The Treasury also issues:

- **TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities),** whose principal adjusts with inflation.
- **Floating Rate Notes (FRNs),** whose interest payments rise and fall with short-term rates.
- **STRIPS,** which separate a note or bond's individual interest and principal payments into zero-coupon securities, sold through brokers.

## How to buy and sell

Treasuries are first sold at **auctions** on a regular schedule, where the interest rate or yield is set. Individuals can buy at auction directly through the government's TreasuryDirect website or through a bank or brokerage. After issuance, Treasuries trade in a large, active secondary market, so they can usually be sold before maturity through a broker. You can also hold Treasuries indirectly through mutual funds and ETFs.

If you sell before maturity, you receive the current market price, which may be more or less than you paid. If you hold to maturity, you receive the face value.

## Tax treatment

Interest from Treasury bills, notes, and bonds is subject to federal income tax but is exempt from state and local income taxes. For people in states with high income taxes, that exemption can make Treasuries' after-tax yields more competitive with other taxable bonds. Tax situations vary, so IRS.gov and a tax professional are good places to confirm details.

## Key takeaways
- Treasuries are U.S. government debt, widely considered to have very low default risk.
- Bills mature in 4 to 52 weeks and are usually sold at a discount; notes last 2 to 10 years; bonds last 20 or 30 years.
- Notes and bonds pay fixed interest every six months; longer terms mean more interest rate sensitivity.
- Treasuries can be bought at auction through TreasuryDirect or a broker, and sold before maturity at market prices.
- Treasury interest is federally taxable but exempt from state and local income taxes.
