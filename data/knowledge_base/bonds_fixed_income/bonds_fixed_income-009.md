Companies need money to build factories, develop products, buy other businesses, or refinance old debt. One way they raise it is by issuing **corporate bonds**. When you buy a corporate bond, you are lending money to a company in exchange for regular interest payments and the return of your principal at maturity. Corporate bonds generally pay more than U.S. Treasuries, and the reason is simple: a company is more likely than the federal government to run into trouble repaying its debts.

## Investment grade vs. high yield

Corporate bonds are usually grouped by their credit ratings, which are opinions from rating agencies about how likely the company is to pay on time.

- **Investment-grade bonds** are rated BBB- or higher (Baa3 or higher by Moody's). They come from companies considered more likely to meet their obligations and typically offer moderately higher yields than Treasuries.
- **High-yield bonds**, also called non-investment grade, speculative, or "junk" bonds, are rated below that line. They offer higher interest rates because of a higher risk of default. Issuers may be companies carrying a lot of debt, companies in financial difficulty, or smaller or newer firms with limited track records.

A company's rating can change. A company that is downgraded from investment grade to high yield is sometimes called a "fallen angel."

## Why the extra yield exists

The additional yield a corporate bond offers over a comparable Treasury is called its **credit spread**. It compensates investors for default risk and often for lower liquidity.

Here is a hypothetical comparison of 10-year bonds:

- Treasury: 4% yield
- Investment-grade corporate: about 5% yield
- High-yield corporate: about 7.5% yield

On a $10,000 investment, the high-yield bond would pay about $750 a year instead of $400 from the Treasury, but only if the company keeps paying. If a high-yield issuer defaults, bondholders may receive only part of their money back, sometimes after a long bankruptcy process, and sometimes little or nothing.

## Features to look for

Corporate bonds can carry terms that change how they behave:

- **Seniority:** Senior bonds are repaid before subordinated (junior) bonds if a company fails. **Secured** bonds are backed by specific assets; **unsecured** bonds, often called debentures, are not.
- **Call provisions:** Many corporate bonds are callable, so the company can repay early, often when rates fall. This limits price gains and forces reinvestment at lower rates.
- **Covenants:** Some bonds include promises the company must keep, such as limits on taking on more debt.
- **Convertible bonds:** These can be exchanged for company stock under set conditions.

## How high-yield bonds behave

High-yield bonds sit between investment-grade bonds and stocks in their behavior. Because their fate depends heavily on the issuing company's health, their prices often move with the stock market and the economy. During recessions, defaults tend to rise and high-yield prices can fall sharply, sometimes at the same moment stocks are falling. That means they may not provide the same cushion in a downturn that high-quality bonds have historically provided.

They are also generally less sensitive to interest rate changes than long-term Treasuries, because their higher coupons return more cash sooner and because credit concerns tend to dominate their price movements.

## Ways investors access corporate bonds

Individual corporate bonds can be bought through brokers, but they often trade in large amounts and may be harder to sell quickly than Treasuries. Many investors instead use bond mutual funds or ETFs, which spread money across hundreds of issuers. Diversification is especially important with high-yield bonds, since a single default can have a large effect on a small portfolio.

## Key takeaways
- Corporate bonds are loans to companies and usually pay more than Treasuries to compensate for credit risk.
- Investment-grade bonds are rated BBB- (Baa3) or higher; high-yield bonds are rated below and carry more default risk.
- The credit spread is the extra yield over Treasuries that compensates for that risk.
- Seniority, security, call provisions, and covenants all affect a corporate bond's risk.
- High-yield bonds often move with stocks and the economy, so they may fall when stocks fall.
