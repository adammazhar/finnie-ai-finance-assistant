Inflation is a quiet threat to ordinary bonds. If a bond pays a fixed $40 a year and prices rise over time, each payment buys a little less. The U.S. Treasury offers two types of securities designed to address this problem: **Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS)** and **Series I savings bonds (I bonds)**. Both are linked to the Consumer Price Index, but they work quite differently.

## How TIPS work

TIPS are marketable Treasury securities issued with terms of 5, 10, or 30 years. Their key feature is that the **principal** (the amount the interest is based on) adjusts with inflation, as measured by the Consumer Price Index.

- When inflation rises, the principal goes up.
- When prices fall (deflation), the principal goes down.
- TIPS pay a fixed interest rate every six months, but that rate is applied to the adjusted principal, so the dollar amount of each payment changes too.
- At maturity, you receive the adjusted principal or the original principal, whichever is greater. That floor protects your original investment from deflation if you hold to maturity.

Here is a hypothetical example. You buy $1,000 of TIPS with a 1% fixed rate. If inflation over the first year is 3%, your principal grows to about $1,030. Your interest for that year is about 1% of the growing principal, roughly $10.30 instead of $10. Over many years, both the principal and the interest keep pace with inflation.

**Things to know about TIPS:**

- TIPS can be bought at auction through TreasuryDirect or a broker, and they trade in the secondary market, so they can be sold before maturity. Their market prices move with interest rates, like other bonds.
- The fixed rate on TIPS is usually lower than the rate on regular Treasuries of the same maturity, because part of your return comes from the inflation adjustment.
- The yearly increases in principal are generally subject to federal income tax in the year they occur, even though you do not receive that money until maturity. This is sometimes called "phantom income." For this reason, some investors hold TIPS in tax-advantaged accounts. Like other Treasuries, TIPS interest is exempt from state and local income taxes.

## How I bonds work

I bonds are **savings bonds**, not marketable securities. You buy them from the Treasury and cash them in with the Treasury; you cannot sell them to another investor.

Their interest rate, called the **combined rate**, has two parts:

- A **fixed rate** that is set when you buy the bond and never changes.
- An **inflation rate** that is reset every six months based on changes in the Consumer Price Index.

Interest is added to the bond's value rather than paid out, and it compounds. When inflation rises, the combined rate rises; when inflation falls, it falls. If deflation were severe enough to push the combined rate below zero, the Treasury sets it at zero, so the bond's value does not go down.

**Rules to know about I bonds:**

- I bonds earn interest for up to 30 years.
- You cannot cash them in during the first 12 months.
- If you cash them in before five years, you give up the last three months of interest.
- Interest is subject to federal income tax but exempt from state and local income taxes. You can generally choose to report the interest each year or wait until you cash in the bond.

The Treasury sets annual purchase limits and announces new rates twice a year. Because these details change, the TreasuryDirect website is the place to check current rates and rules.

## Comparing the two

- **Price swings:** TIPS prices move with interest rates if sold early; I bonds do not lose value.
- **Access:** TIPS can be sold anytime in the market; I bonds are locked for 12 months and carry a small penalty before five years.
- **Income:** TIPS pay interest every six months; I bonds accumulate interest until you cash them in.
- **Taxes:** TIPS inflation adjustments may be taxed annually; I bond interest can usually be deferred.

Neither offers protection against every risk. They are built to preserve purchasing power, not to produce high returns, and TIPS can still lose value in the short run when real interest rates rise.

## Key takeaways
- TIPS and I bonds are Treasury securities designed to keep pace with inflation measured by the CPI.
- TIPS adjust their principal for inflation, pay interest every six months, and trade in the market.
- At maturity, TIPS return the greater of the adjusted or original principal.
- I bonds combine a fixed rate with an inflation rate reset every six months and cannot lose value.
- I bonds have holding rules and purchase limits; check TreasuryDirect for current rates and limits.
