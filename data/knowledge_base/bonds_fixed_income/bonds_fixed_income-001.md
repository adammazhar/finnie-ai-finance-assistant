A bond is a loan that you make to a government, company, or other organization. In return for your money, the borrower (called the **issuer**) promises to pay you interest along the way and to give your original money back on a set date. Because the payments usually follow a known schedule, bonds are often called **fixed-income** investments.

Stocks make you a part-owner of a company. Bonds make you a lender. That difference shapes almost everything about how bonds behave.

## The basic parts of a bond

Every bond has a few key features that are set when it is issued:

- **Face value (par value):** the amount the issuer promises to repay at the end. Many bonds have a face value of $1,000.
- **Coupon rate:** the yearly interest rate the issuer pays, stated as a percentage of face value. The interest payments themselves are called **coupons**, a name left over from the days when paper bonds had coupons you clipped and turned in for payment.
- **Maturity date:** the day the loan ends and the issuer repays the face value. Maturities range from a few weeks to 30 years or more.
- **Issuer:** who is borrowing. This matters because it determines how likely you are to be repaid.

## A simple example

Here is a hypothetical example. Suppose a city issues a 10-year bond with a $1,000 face value and a 4% coupon. If you buy it when it is issued and hold it to the end:

- You receive $40 in interest each year (4% of $1,000), often paid as two $20 payments six months apart.
- After 10 years, you get your $1,000 back.
- Over the life of the bond, you collect $400 in interest plus the return of your $1,000.

That predictability is the main appeal. As long as the issuer keeps its promises, you know in advance what you will receive and when.

## Who issues bonds?

- **The U.S. federal government** issues Treasury bills, notes, and bonds. These are backed by the full faith and credit of the U.S. government.
- **States, cities, and other local governments** issue **municipal bonds** to pay for things like schools, roads, and water systems. Their interest is often exempt from federal income tax.
- **Corporations** issue **corporate bonds** to fund operations, expansion, or other needs.
- **Government agencies and other entities** also issue bonds, including mortgage-backed securities.

## Bonds are not risk-free

Bonds are usually less volatile than stocks, but they carry real risks:

- **Credit (default) risk:** the issuer may fail to make interest or principal payments on time.
- **Interest rate risk:** if market interest rates rise, the market price of an existing bond usually falls. This matters if you need to sell before maturity.
- **Inflation risk:** fixed payments buy less when prices rise over time.
- **Call risk:** some bonds let the issuer repay early, often when rates have dropped, leaving you to reinvest at lower rates.
- **Liquidity risk:** some bonds are hard to sell quickly at a fair price.

## Why people own bonds

Investors commonly use bonds to create a steady stream of income, to reduce the overall ups and downs of a portfolio that also holds stocks, and to set aside money for a known future date. Historically, stocks have returned more than bonds over long periods, but with larger swings along the way. Many diversified portfolios hold some of each, with the mix depending on goals, time horizon, and comfort with risk.

## Key takeaways
- A bond is a loan to an issuer, who pays interest (coupons) and repays the face value at maturity.
- The face value, coupon rate, maturity date, and issuer define a bond's basic terms.
- Governments, municipalities, and corporations all issue bonds, with different levels of risk.
- Bonds carry credit, interest rate, inflation, call, and liquidity risks.
- Bonds are often used for income and to steady a portfolio, while stocks have historically offered higher long-run growth.
