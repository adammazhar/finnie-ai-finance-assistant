There are two main ways to hold bonds: buy individual bonds directly, or buy shares of a **bond fund**, such as a mutual fund or exchange-traded fund (ETF) that owns many bonds at once. Both give you exposure to interest income and to the same basic risks, but they behave differently in some important ways. Understanding the trade-offs helps explain why investors choose one, the other, or a mix.

## How individual bonds work

When you buy an individual bond, you know its key terms up front: the coupon rate, the payment schedule, and the maturity date. If you hold it to maturity and the issuer does not default, you receive every scheduled interest payment and your face value back. The bond's market price will rise and fall with interest rates in the meantime, but those swings matter mostly if you sell early.

That predictability is useful for matching money to a known future expense. Some investors build a **bond ladder**, a set of bonds maturing in different years, so that a portion comes due regularly and can be spent or reinvested.

Drawbacks of individual bonds include:

- **Diversification takes money.** Spreading risk across many issuers requires buying many bonds, which can be hard with a small portfolio. This matters most for corporate and municipal bonds, where a single default can be costly.
- **Research is on you.** You need to evaluate each issuer's credit quality and the bond's terms, such as call provisions.
- **Trading costs and liquidity.** Many individual bonds, especially munis and smaller corporate issues, trade infrequently. The markup between buying and selling prices can be meaningful, and selling before maturity may mean accepting a lower price. U.S. Treasuries are a notable exception, with a very active market.

## How bond funds work

A bond fund pools money from many investors to buy a portfolio of bonds. A fund may focus on one type, such as government, municipal, corporate, or high-yield bonds, or mix several. Its managers buy and sell bonds over time, usually keeping the portfolio within a target range of maturities.

Advantages include:

- **Instant diversification** across dozens or hundreds of issuers with a small investment.
- **Professional management** of credit research and trading.
- **Convenience:** easy to buy, sell, and reinvest income automatically. Mutual fund shares are priced once per day; ETF shares trade throughout the day.

Drawbacks include:

- **No maturity date.** Most bond funds never mature, because managers keep replacing bonds as they come due. There is no date when you are promised your original investment back, so you can lose money in a bond fund, even one holding only government bonds.
- **Changing income.** Monthly distributions rise and fall as the fund's holdings change.
- **Ongoing fees.** Funds charge an expense ratio every year, which reduces returns. These costs vary widely.

## The interest rate question

A common belief is that individual bonds avoid losses when rates rise, while funds do not. The reality is more nuanced. When rates rise, the market value of both falls. An individual bond hides that drop if you hold to maturity, but you are still earning a below-market rate. A bond fund shows the drop immediately in its share price, but it also starts reinvesting at the new, higher rates.

Here is a hypothetical example. A fund has a duration of 5 years. Rates rise by 1 percentage point, and its share price falls roughly 5%. Over the following years, higher income from newly purchased bonds helps the fund gradually recover that loss. For an investor who holds for roughly the fund's duration or longer, the outcome can end up broadly similar to holding individual bonds of comparable maturity.

## Choosing between them

Neither approach is universally better. Individual bonds may appeal to those who want known cash flows for specific dates and have enough money to diversify. Bond funds may appeal to those who value simplicity, diversification, and small minimum investments. Target-maturity bond ETFs, which hold bonds that all mature in the same year and then wind down, blend some features of both.

## Key takeaways
- Individual bonds have a set maturity date and, absent default, return face value at the end.
- Bond funds offer diversification and convenience but have no maturity date and charge ongoing fees.
- Rising rates reduce the market value of both; funds show it immediately and reinvest at higher rates.
- Diversifying across individual corporate or municipal bonds usually requires a larger portfolio.
- The choice depends on goals, time horizon, portfolio size, and desire for simplicity.
