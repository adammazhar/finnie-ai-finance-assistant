One of the first surprises for new bond investors is that a bond's price can fall even when nothing is wrong with the issuer. The most common reason is a change in market interest rates. When rates go up, the prices of existing fixed-rate bonds go down. When rates go down, their prices go up. This seesaw relationship is called **interest rate risk**, and it affects every fixed-rate bond, including U.S. Treasuries.

## The core idea: competing with new bonds

A fixed-rate bond pays the same coupon for its whole life. New bonds, however, are issued at whatever rates the market offers today. An older bond has to compete with those new bonds for buyers.

Here is a hypothetical example. Suppose you own a bond with a $1,000 face value that pays a 3% coupon, or $30 a year.

- **Rates rise to 5%.** A new $1,000 bond now pays $50 a year. Nobody will pay the full $1,000 for your bond that pays only $30. To attract a buyer, its price must drop until its effective return is roughly competitive with the new 5% bonds.
- **Rates fall to 2%.** New $1,000 bonds pay only $20 a year. Your bond's $30 payment now looks attractive, so buyers are willing to pay more than $1,000 for it.

The coupon never changes. What changes is the price someone is willing to pay for that fixed stream of payments. A lower price paired with the same coupon means a higher yield for the new buyer, which is how the old bond "catches up" to current rates.

## A second way to see it: today's value of future cash

A bond is a set of future payments. To decide what those payments are worth today, investors **discount** them using current interest rates. A higher discount rate makes future dollars worth less today, so the bond's price falls. A lower discount rate makes them worth more, so the price rises. Both explanations describe the same math.

## Longer bonds move more

The longer a bond has until maturity, the more its price tends to react to a rate change. A bond maturing in six months will soon repay its face value, so a rate change affects only a few remaining payments. A 30-year bond locks in its coupon for decades, so being "stuck" with a below-market rate matters much more. Bonds with lower coupons also tend to be more sensitive. Professionals summarize this sensitivity with a measure called **duration**.

## Does this matter if you hold to maturity?

It depends on your plans. If you hold an individual bond until it matures and the issuer does not default, you receive all the scheduled interest and your face value back, regardless of what happened to its market price along the way. The price swings matter mainly if you need or choose to sell early.

There is still an opportunity cost, though. If rates rise and you hold a lower-paying bond, you are earning less than newly issued bonds would pay. And bond funds, which do not have a single maturity date, see their share prices move with rates on an ongoing basis.

## Why rates change

Market interest rates respond to many forces, including Federal Reserve policy, inflation expectations, economic growth, and supply and demand for credit. Rising inflation tends to push rates up because lenders want to be compensated for the loss of purchasing power. No one can reliably predict these moves, which is why understanding the relationship matters more than forecasting it.

## Key takeaways
- When market interest rates rise, prices of existing fixed-rate bonds fall, and when rates fall, prices rise.
- The coupon stays the same; the price adjusts so the bond's yield stays competitive with new bonds.
- Longer-maturity and lower-coupon bonds are generally more sensitive to rate changes.
- Holding an individual bond to maturity avoids locking in price swings, assuming no default, but carries opportunity cost.
- Interest rate risk applies even to high-quality bonds such as U.S. Treasuries.
