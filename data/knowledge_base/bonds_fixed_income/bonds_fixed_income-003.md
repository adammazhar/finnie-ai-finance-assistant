"Yield" is the word bond investors use to describe the return a bond provides. The trouble is that there are several different yields, and they can give quite different numbers for the same bond. Knowing which one you are looking at helps you compare bonds fairly. This article covers the three most common: the coupon rate, current yield, and yield to maturity.

## Coupon rate: what the bond promises to pay

The **coupon rate** is the fixed annual interest rate printed in the bond's terms, stated as a percentage of its face value. A $1,000 bond with a 5% coupon pays $50 a year, no matter what happens to its market price.

The coupon rate is useful for knowing the size of your interest checks. It is less useful for comparing investments, because it ignores the price you actually pay. Once a bond starts trading, its price moves above or below face value as interest rates change.

## Current yield: income relative to today's price

**Current yield** measures the annual interest compared with the bond's current market price:

Current yield = annual coupon payment / current price

Using a hypothetical 10-year bond with a $1,000 face value and a 5% coupon ($50 a year):

- If it trades at $1,000 (par), the current yield is 5%.
- If it trades at $900 (a **discount**), the current yield is $50 / $900, or about 5.6%.
- If it trades at $1,100 (a **premium**), the current yield is $50 / $1,100, or about 4.5%.

Current yield is quick to calculate and tells you the income you get per dollar invested today. Its blind spot is that it ignores what happens at maturity. If you buy at $900, you will get $1,000 back at the end, a $100 gain that current yield leaves out. If you buy at $1,100, you will get only $1,000 back, a $100 loss it also leaves out.

## Yield to maturity: the full picture

**Yield to maturity (YTM)** is the annual rate of return you would earn if you bought the bond at today's price, held it until it matures, and received every payment on time. It combines all the coupon payments with any gain or loss between your purchase price and the face value, and it accounts for the timing of those payments.

Continuing the hypothetical example with annual payments and 10 years left:

- Bought at $1,000: YTM is 5%, the same as the coupon.
- Bought at $900: YTM is roughly 6.4%, higher than the current yield because it counts the $100 gain at maturity.
- Bought at $1,100: YTM is roughly 3.8%, lower than the current yield because it counts the $100 loss at maturity.

YTM is found by solving for the single interest rate that makes the present value of all future payments equal to the bond's price. In practice, brokerage platforms and financial calculators do this for you.

## How the three yields relate

A simple pattern holds for fixed-rate bonds:

- **At par:** coupon rate = current yield = YTM.
- **At a discount:** coupon rate < current yield < YTM.
- **At a premium:** coupon rate > current yield > YTM.

## Assumptions and cautions

YTM rests on assumptions that may not hold. It assumes you hold to maturity, that the issuer makes every payment, and that you can reinvest each coupon at the same yield. If rates change, your actual return may differ. For bonds that the issuer can repay early, investors also look at **yield to call** and **yield to worst**, the lowest yield you could receive under the bond's call provisions.

A high yield is not free money. Bonds usually offer higher yields because they carry more risk, such as weaker credit or a longer maturity. Comparing yields makes the most sense between bonds with similar risks.

## Key takeaways
- The coupon rate is the fixed interest rate based on face value; it ignores the price you pay.
- Current yield divides annual interest by the current price but ignores gains or losses at maturity.
- Yield to maturity estimates the total annual return if you hold to maturity and all payments are made.
- Discount bonds have a YTM above their coupon; premium bonds have a YTM below it.
- Higher yields generally reflect higher risk, so compare yields across similar bonds.
