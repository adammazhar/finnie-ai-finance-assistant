When a state builds a highway, a city upgrades its water system, or a school district constructs a new building, the money often comes from **municipal bonds**, nicknamed "munis." These are debt securities issued by states, cities, counties, and other local government entities. Their best-known feature is favorable tax treatment, which makes them a common choice for some taxable investors.

## Two main types

- **General obligation (GO) bonds** are backed by the "full faith and credit" of the issuing government, which can use its power to tax residents to repay bondholders. They are not tied to a specific project's income.
- **Revenue bonds** are repaid from the income of a particular project or source, such as highway tolls, airport fees, or utility bills. If that revenue falls short, bondholders may be at greater risk, because the government's general taxing power is not pledged.

Munis come in a wide range of maturities, from a year or two to 30 years or longer, and many are **callable**, meaning the issuer can repay them early.

## How munis are taxed

- **Federal tax:** Interest on most municipal bonds is exempt from federal income tax.
- **State and local tax:** Interest may also be exempt from state and local income taxes if you live in the state where the bond was issued. Bonds from other states are often taxable at the state level. Bonds issued by U.S. territories may be exempt in all states.
- **Capital gains:** The exemption covers interest only. If you sell a muni for more than you paid, the gain is generally taxable.
- **Exceptions:** Some munis, such as certain "private activity" bonds that finance projects benefiting private businesses, can be subject to the federal alternative minimum tax. Some munis are fully taxable. Tax-exempt interest can also factor into other calculations, such as how much of a person's Social Security benefits is taxable.

Because tax rules are detailed, IRS.gov and a tax professional are the best places to confirm how a specific bond would be treated.

## Comparing munis with taxable bonds

Because of the tax break, munis usually pay lower stated interest rates than taxable bonds with similar maturity and credit quality. To compare fairly, investors often calculate a **tax-equivalent yield**, the yield a taxable bond would need to offer to match the muni after taxes:

Tax-equivalent yield = muni yield / (1 - your tax rate)

Here is a hypothetical example using round numbers and federal tax only:

- A muni yields 3%.
- An investor's federal marginal tax rate is 24%.
- Tax-equivalent yield = 3% / (1 - 0.24) = about 3.95%.

So for this investor, a taxable bond would need to yield about 3.95% to leave the same amount after federal taxes. For someone in a 12% bracket, the same muni's tax-equivalent yield would be only about 3.4%. In general, the higher your tax bracket, the more valuable the exemption. It also explains why the muni tax break adds little inside tax-advantaged accounts such as IRAs, where income is already sheltered from current taxes.

## Risks to keep in mind

- **Credit risk:** Most munis have historically had low default rates, but defaults do happen, particularly with some revenue bonds or when a local economy struggles. Credit ratings help, but they are opinions.
- **Interest rate risk:** Like other bonds, munis lose market value when rates rise.
- **Call risk:** An issuer may repay a callable bond early, often when rates fall.
- **Liquidity risk:** Many individual munis trade infrequently, so selling one before maturity can mean accepting a lower price.
- **Concentration risk:** Holding bonds from a single state or city ties your results to that area's finances.

Investors can buy individual munis through a broker or hold many at once through municipal bond funds and ETFs, including funds that focus on a single state for residents seeking state tax benefits.

## Key takeaways
- Municipal bonds are issued by states, cities, and other local governments to fund public projects.
- General obligation bonds rely on taxing power; revenue bonds rely on a specific project's income.
- Muni interest is usually exempt from federal income tax and may be exempt from state tax for in-state residents.
- Tax-equivalent yield helps compare munis with taxable bonds; the benefit grows with your tax bracket.
- Munis still carry credit, interest rate, call, and liquidity risks.
