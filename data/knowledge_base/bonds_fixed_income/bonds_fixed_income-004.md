Bond prices move in the opposite direction of interest rates, but not all bonds move by the same amount. Some barely budge when rates change; others swing sharply. **Duration** is the tool investors use to estimate how sensitive a bond, or a bond fund, is to changes in interest rates. It is one of the most useful numbers on a bond fund's fact sheet, and one of the most misunderstood.

## What duration measures

Duration is expressed in years, which makes it easy to confuse with maturity. They are related but different.

- **Maturity** is simply when the bond's final payment arrives.
- **Duration** reflects the weighted-average timing of *all* the bond's payments, both coupons and the final principal. Because coupon payments arrive before maturity, a coupon-paying bond's duration is shorter than its maturity. A **zero-coupon bond**, which pays nothing until the end, has a duration equal to its maturity.

The version most investors use day to day is often called **modified** or **effective duration**, which turns that timing measure into a price sensitivity estimate.

## The rule of thumb

For small changes in interest rates, a bond's price changes by roughly its duration multiplied by the rate change, in the opposite direction:

Approximate price change (%) = - duration x change in rates (percentage points)

Here are hypothetical examples:

- A bond fund with a duration of 6 years. If rates rise by 1 percentage point, its price would be expected to fall by about 6%. If rates fall by 1 point, its price would rise by about 6%.
- A short-term fund with a duration of 2 years. The same 1-point rise would mean a drop of only about 2%.
- A long-term fund with a duration of 15 years. A 1-point rise could mean a drop of roughly 15%.

This estimate works best for small rate moves. For larger moves, a property called **convexity** makes the actual price change somewhat different from the straight-line estimate, but the rule of thumb is still a helpful starting point.

## What makes duration longer or shorter

- **Time to maturity:** longer maturities generally mean longer duration.
- **Coupon rate:** lower coupons mean longer duration, because more of the value comes from the final payment far in the future.
- **Yield level:** when yields are lower, durations tend to be somewhat longer.
- **Call features:** bonds that can be repaid early may behave as if they have shorter duration when rates fall, since the issuer may call them.

## Duration and your time horizon

Duration also connects to how long you plan to hold. When rates rise, prices fall right away, but the bond or fund can then reinvest coupon payments at the new, higher rates. Over time, that higher income can help make up for the initial price drop. Some investors use a rough guideline: if the holding period is roughly as long as the bond's duration, those two effects may come close to offsetting each other, although this is an approximation rather than a guarantee. If you might need the money much sooner than the duration, a rise in rates could leave you with less than you expected.

That is why short-duration bonds are often associated with money needed soon, and longer-duration bonds with longer horizons. Longer-duration bonds usually, but not always, pay higher yields in exchange for the extra sensitivity.

## Limits of duration

Duration measures sensitivity to interest rates only. It does not capture credit risk (the chance of default), inflation risk, or liquidity risk. Two funds with the same duration can behave very differently if one holds Treasuries and the other holds high-yield corporate bonds. Duration also assumes all rates shift by the same amount, which does not always happen in real markets.

## Key takeaways
- Duration, measured in years, estimates how much a bond's price will change when interest rates change.
- Rule of thumb: price change is roughly minus duration times the change in rates.
- Longer maturities and lower coupons generally mean longer duration and more rate sensitivity.
- Matching duration to your time horizon can help balance price risk against reinvestment at new rates.
- Duration ignores credit, inflation, and liquidity risk, so it is only one part of a bond's risk picture.
