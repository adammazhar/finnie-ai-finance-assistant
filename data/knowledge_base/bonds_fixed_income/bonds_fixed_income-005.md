When you buy a bond, you are lending money and counting on the borrower to pay you back. **Default risk**, also called **credit risk**, is the chance that the issuer fails to make interest or principal payments in full and on time. Credit ratings are one of the main tools investors use to gauge that risk.

## What a credit rating is

A credit rating is a letter grade from an independent company, called a **credit rating agency**, that expresses its opinion of how likely an issuer is to meet its debt obligations. Ratings can apply to a whole company or government, or to a specific bond. Firms that register with the SEC and meet its standards are known as **Nationally Recognized Statistical Rating Organizations (NRSROs)**. The best known are S&P Global Ratings, Moody's, and Fitch Ratings.

Agencies look at factors such as how much debt the issuer carries, the stability of its income or tax base, its cash flow, its industry, and the specific terms of the bond. They review ratings over time and can **upgrade** or **downgrade** them as conditions change.

## Reading the letter grades

Each agency uses its own scale, but they look similar. S&P and Fitch use grades like AAA, AA, A, BBB, BB, B, CCC, and lower, sometimes with a plus or minus. Moody's uses Aaa, Aa, A, Baa, Ba, B, Caa, and lower, with numbers 1 to 3 for finer steps.

The most important dividing line is between two broad groups:

- **Investment grade:** BBB- or higher (Baa3 or higher at Moody's). These issuers are considered more likely to pay on time.
- **Non-investment grade:** below BBB- (below Baa3). These are also called **high-yield** or **speculative** bonds, and informally "junk" bonds.

Within each group, higher grades indicate lower expected default risk. A AAA rating represents the agency's highest level of confidence.

## How ratings connect to yield

Investors expect to be paid for taking on more risk. As a result, lower-rated bonds generally have to offer higher yields to attract buyers. The extra yield a bond offers compared with a similar-maturity U.S. Treasury is called its **credit spread**.

Here is a hypothetical example. Suppose a 10-year Treasury yields 4%. A highly rated corporate bond with the same maturity might yield around 5%, while a lower-rated, high-yield bond might yield 7% or more. The bigger spread compensates for a greater chance that some payments will not arrive.

Ratings changes also affect prices. If a bond is downgraded, its price usually falls, because buyers now demand a higher yield. A downgrade from investment grade to high yield can be especially significant, because the bond moves into the riskier, speculative category.

## What a credit rating is not

Ratings are useful, but they have limits:

- **They are opinions, not guarantees.** Even a AAA rating does not mean you will always be paid, and agencies can revise ratings as conditions change.
- **They are not a recommendation.** A rating does not tell you whether a bond's price is attractive or whether it fits your needs.
- **They address credit risk only.** A AAA bond can still lose market value when interest rates rise.
- **There can be conflicts of interest.** Under the common "issuer-pays" model, the issuer pays the agency for its rating.

For these reasons, many investors treat ratings as a starting point rather than the final word.

## Managing default risk

Common ways investors limit the impact of any single default include holding many different bonds, often through a bond fund; favoring higher-quality issuers for money that must be safe; and understanding where a bond ranks in the issuer's capital structure, since some debt is repaid before other debt if a company fails. U.S. Treasury securities are generally regarded as having very low default risk because they are backed by the full faith and credit of the U.S. government.

## Key takeaways
- Default (credit) risk is the chance an issuer fails to pay interest or principal in full and on time.
- Credit rating agencies assign letter grades; BBB- (Baa3) and above is investment grade, below that is high yield.
- Lower ratings generally mean higher yields to compensate investors for extra risk.
- Ratings are opinions that can change and do not measure interest rate risk or price value.
- Diversification and attention to issuer quality help manage default risk.
