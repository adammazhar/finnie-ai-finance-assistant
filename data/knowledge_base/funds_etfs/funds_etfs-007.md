Most beginner-friendly funds try to capture a broad slice of the market. **Sector funds** and **thematic funds** do the opposite: they deliberately narrow their focus to one part of the economy or one idea. That focus can make them exciting, and it can also make them much riskier than a broad fund.

## What is a sector fund?

A **sector fund** invests mainly in companies from a single sector or industry. Common sectors include technology, health care, financials, energy, utilities, consumer staples, industrials, and real estate. Sector funds come as both mutual funds and ETFs, and they may be index-based (tracking a sector index) or actively managed.

Investors use sector funds for several reasons: to add more weight to an industry they expect to do well, to fill a gap in their holdings, or to study how different parts of the economy behave.

## What is a thematic fund?

A **thematic fund** is built around a trend or idea rather than a traditional industry label. Examples of themes include clean energy, robotics, cybersecurity, aging populations, or artificial intelligence. A thematic fund often cuts across sectors, owning, say, a chip maker, a software company, and an industrial firm that all connect to the same theme.

Themes are appealing because they tell a clear story about the future. But deciding which companies truly fit a theme can be subjective, and two funds with similar names may own very different companies.

## Concentration risk

The main risk of both types is **concentration**. A broad stock fund spreads money across many industries, so trouble in one area is cushioned by the others. A sector or thematic fund puts much of its money into companies that tend to rise and fall together, because they share customers, costs, regulations, or investor enthusiasm.

A hypothetical example: suppose a broad fund holds 10% of its money in energy companies, and energy stocks fall 40% in a bad year. The energy portion drags the broad fund down by about 4%. A dedicated energy fund in the same situation could fall close to 40%.

Sector funds can also be heavily concentrated in a few large companies. In some sectors, a handful of firms make up a big share of the index, so the fund's results may depend heavily on just those companies.

## Other things to watch

**Timing and hype.** A theme can become popular, and prices can climb, before many investors notice it. Buying after a surge of enthusiasm raises the risk of buying near a peak, and a fund whose theme falls out of favor may shrink or even close.

**Costs.** Specialized funds can charge higher expense ratios than broad index funds, so compare costs before buying.

**Overlap.** A broad market fund already owns most of the companies in any given sector. Adding a sector fund on top can increase your exposure to companies you already hold, sometimes more than you realize.

**Cycles.** Some sectors, such as energy and materials, are **cyclical**, rising and falling with the economy. Others, like utilities and consumer staples, have historically been steadier but can still decline.

## How they fit in a portfolio

Some investors use sector or thematic funds as small "satellite" holdings around a diversified core, while others avoid them. Because returns can differ widely from the broader market, both for better and for worse, it is important to understand what the fund owns, how concentrated it is, and how a large drop would affect your overall plan. The fund's prospectus and holdings list are the best places to check.

## Key takeaways
- Sector funds focus on one industry; thematic funds focus on a trend that may span several industries.
- Both carry concentration risk because their holdings tend to move together.
- Popular themes can attract money near price peaks, and a fund whose theme fades may shrink or close.
- Specialized funds can cost more and may overlap with broad funds you already own.
- Check a fund's holdings and prospectus to see what it actually owns.
