Mutual funds and exchange-traded funds (ETFs) have a lot in common. Both pool money from many investors, both are registered with the SEC, and both can hold hundreds of stocks or bonds in a single investment. The differences are mostly about **how you buy and sell them**, **how they are priced**, and **how they handle taxes and costs**. Knowing these differences helps you understand what you are choosing between; neither structure is better for everyone.

## What they share

- **Pooled, professionally managed portfolios.** Each share represents a proportional stake in the fund's holdings.
- **SEC registration and a prospectus** that describes goals, risks, and fees.
- **Many strategies available.** Both come in index and actively managed versions, covering stocks, bonds, and more.
- **Ongoing costs** expressed as an expense ratio.
- **Market risk.** Neither is FDIC-insured, and both can lose value.

You cannot always tell which structure a fund uses from its name. The prospectus will say.

## Trading and pricing

**Mutual funds** are bought from and sold back to the fund company, directly or through a broker. Orders are filled once per day at the **next net asset value (NAV)** calculated after the market closes. Everyone who places an order that day gets the same price.

**ETFs** trade on a stock exchange throughout the day. Their price moves minute to minute and can sit slightly above or below NAV. You can use limit orders and other stock-style order types, and you pay the bid-ask spread when you trade.

A hypothetical example: on a volatile day, the market drops 2% in the morning and recovers by the close. An ETF investor who sold at 10 a.m. locked in the morning price. A mutual fund investor who placed a sell order at 10 a.m. received the closing NAV.

## Minimums and how you invest

Some mutual funds require an initial minimum investment, and many let you invest exact dollar amounts, including automatic monthly contributions. ETFs trade by the share, so the minimum is typically one share. Many brokers now offer fractional ETF shares, which narrows this difference.

## Costs

Both charge an **expense ratio**. Index versions of either structure tend to be cheaper than actively managed ones. Some mutual funds also have **sales loads** or **12b-1 fees**; ETFs generally do not charge loads, but you may pay a trading commission (many brokers charge none) and the bid-ask spread. Comparing the total cost of owning a specific fund matters more than the label.

## Taxes

In a taxable account, this is one of the biggest differences. When a mutual fund sells holdings at a profit, often to meet redemptions from other investors, it may pass capital gains distributions to all shareholders, who owe tax even if they sold nothing.

Many ETFs avoid much of this because shares are created and redeemed **in kind**, meaning securities rather than cash change hands with large institutions. This lets the ETF move out low-cost holdings without selling them. As a result, ETFs have typically made fewer capital gains distributions. Inside tax-advantaged accounts such as IRAs and 401(k)s, this difference matters much less.

## A quick comparison

| Feature | Mutual fund | ETF |
|---|---|---|
| Where traded | With the fund company | On an exchange |
| Pricing | Once daily at NAV | Continuously at market price |
| Order types | Dollar amount at next NAV | Market, limit, stop, etc. |
| Minimum | Varies by fund | Usually one share or less |
| Capital gains distributions | More common | Typically fewer |

## Key takeaways
- Mutual funds and ETFs are both pooled, SEC-registered funds with similar investment options.
- Mutual funds trade once a day at NAV; ETFs trade all day at market prices.
- ETFs often distribute fewer capital gains, which matters most in taxable accounts.
- Costs depend on the specific fund, including expense ratios, loads, commissions, and spreads.
- The right structure depends on how and where a person invests, not on a universal rule.
