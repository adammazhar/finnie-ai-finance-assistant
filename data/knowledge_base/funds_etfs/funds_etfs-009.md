A **fund fact sheet** is a short summary, usually one or two pages, that a fund company publishes for each of its mutual funds and ETFs. It pulls the most important information into one place: what the fund invests in, what it costs, how it has performed, and what it holds. Fact sheets are usually updated monthly or quarterly. Knowing where to look on one makes comparing funds much faster.

A fact sheet is a marketing summary, not the legal document. The official source is the fund's **prospectus** (or the shorter **summary prospectus**), which the SEC requires to cover the fund's objectives, strategies, risks, fees, and past performance. When the two differ, the prospectus is the one to trust.

## 1. Objective and strategy

Near the top, the fact sheet states what the fund is trying to do, such as "seeks to track the performance of a broad U.S. stock index" or "seeks current income." It also names the **benchmark**, the index the fund compares itself to. This tells you what kind of investment you are looking at and what its performance should be judged against.

## 2. Fees

Look for the **expense ratio**, the annual cost as a percentage of your investment. A fact sheet may show a **gross** ratio and a lower **net** ratio; the net figure reflects a fee waiver that may be temporary. Also check for **sales loads** or other charges. Fees come out of returns every year, so they are one of the most important numbers on the page.

## 3. Performance

Performance is usually shown for several periods, such as 1, 3, 5, and 10 years and "since inception," often next to the benchmark's returns. A few tips:

- Returns over one year are usually **annualized**, meaning shown as an average yearly rate.
- Check whether returns are shown **before or after** sales charges.
- Compare the fund with its benchmark and with similar funds, not with unrelated investments.
- Remember the standard warning: past performance does not guarantee future results.

A hypothetical example: a fund shows a 5-year annualized return of 8.0% while its benchmark shows 8.3%. That 0.3% gap is roughly what you might expect from fees and trading costs in an index fund. A much larger gap would be worth investigating.

## 4. Holdings and allocation

Most fact sheets list:

- **Top 10 holdings** and what percentage of the fund they make up. A high percentage in the top 10 means the fund is more concentrated.
- **Number of holdings**, a quick measure of diversification.
- **Sector, country, or asset-class breakdowns.**
- For bond funds, **credit quality** and **duration**, a measure of sensitivity to interest-rate changes.

## 5. Risk measures and other statistics

You may see measures like **standard deviation** (how much returns have varied), **beta** (how much the fund has moved relative to the market), or, for index funds, **tracking error**. Other common items include **turnover** (how often holdings are traded, which can affect costs and taxes), total fund assets, the inception date, and the NAV. For ETFs, you might also see the ticker, exchange, and premium or discount to NAV.

## Putting it together

When comparing two funds with a similar objective, a fact sheet lets you line up the key points quickly: cost, benchmark, performance relative to that benchmark, concentration, and risk. It is a starting point for questions, and the prospectus fills in the details.

## Key takeaways
- A fund fact sheet is a brief summary of a fund's objective, costs, performance, and holdings.
- The prospectus, not the fact sheet, is the official disclosure document.
- Check the expense ratio, including whether a lower net figure depends on a temporary waiver.
- Compare performance with the fund's benchmark and similar funds over several periods.
- Top holdings, number of holdings, and turnover reveal how concentrated and active a fund is.
