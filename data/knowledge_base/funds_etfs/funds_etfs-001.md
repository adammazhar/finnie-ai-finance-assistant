A **mutual fund** pools money from many investors and uses it to buy a collection of investments, such as stocks, bonds, or short-term debt. Each investor owns shares of the fund, and each share represents a proportional slice of everything the fund holds. Mutual funds are one of the most common ways people invest, especially inside workplace retirement plans like 401(k)s.

## How a mutual fund works

Imagine 1,000 people each contributing $1,000. Together they have $1 million, enough to buy small pieces of hundreds of companies. A professional **investment adviser** manages the combined pot according to the fund's stated goal, such as "track the overall U.S. stock market" or "earn income from high-quality bonds."

Every investor shares in the results. If the fund's holdings rise in value, every share is worth more. If they fall, every share is worth less. The fund's goals, strategies, risks, and fees are described in a legal document called the **prospectus**, which funds must provide to investors.

## Net asset value (NAV)

A mutual fund's share price is called its **net asset value**, or NAV. It is calculated once per business day, after the markets close:

**NAV = (total value of the fund's assets - its liabilities) / number of shares outstanding**

A hypothetical example: a fund holds $10.5 million in investments, owes $0.5 million in expenses and other liabilities, and has 1 million shares. Its NAV is $10 per share.

This leads to an important feature: mutual fund orders are filled at the **next NAV calculated** after your order is received. If you place an order at 11 a.m., you will get that day's closing NAV, not the price from the moment you clicked. You buy shares directly from the fund (or through a broker acting for it) and sell them back to the fund, rather than trading with other investors on an exchange.

## Why people use mutual funds

- **Diversification.** One fund can hold dozens or thousands of securities, reducing the impact any single company has on your results.
- **Professional management.** Someone else researches, buys, and sells the investments.
- **Convenience.** Many funds allow automatic investments and dividend reinvestment, and you can usually invest a set dollar amount, including fractional shares.
- **Liquidity.** Mutual funds must generally let you sell (redeem) your shares on any business day.

## Active and index mutual funds

**Actively managed funds** have managers who choose investments trying to beat a benchmark, such as a stock market index. **Index funds** simply try to match a benchmark by holding the same securities. Index funds tend to have lower costs, which matters because fees are paid whether or not a fund beats its benchmark.

## How mutual funds make money for investors

- **Dividends and interest** earned on the fund's holdings, which are passed along to shareholders.
- **Capital gains distributions** when the fund sells investments at a profit. In a taxable account, these distributions can create a tax bill even if you did not sell any shares yourself.
- **A higher NAV**, which you realize when you sell your shares for more than you paid.

## Costs and risks

Mutual funds charge fees. The most important ongoing cost is the **expense ratio**, an annual percentage of your investment used to run the fund. Some funds also charge **sales loads** (commissions when you buy or sell) or **12b-1 fees** for marketing and distribution. Fees reduce your returns year after year, so they are worth comparing.

Mutual funds are **not** insured by the FDIC. Their value goes up and down with their holdings, and you can lose money. A fund's past performance does not guarantee future results, and diversification within a fund does not protect against broad market declines.

## Key takeaways
- A mutual fund pools many investors' money into a professionally managed portfolio.
- Shares are priced once a day at net asset value (NAV), and orders fill at the next calculated NAV.
- Funds offer diversification, professional management, and daily liquidity.
- Expense ratios, sales loads, and other fees reduce returns and vary widely between funds.
- Mutual funds can lose value and are not FDIC-insured.
