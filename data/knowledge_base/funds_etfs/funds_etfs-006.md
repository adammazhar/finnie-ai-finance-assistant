A **target-date fund** is an all-in-one fund built around a single year: the year you expect to start using the money, most often your planned retirement year. It holds a mix of stocks, bonds, and other investments, and it gradually shifts that mix to become more conservative as the target year approaches. Target-date funds are common in workplace retirement plans, and some 401(k) plans use them as the default choice for employees who don't pick investments themselves.

## How the name works

The year in the fund's name is the target date. A fund with "2060" in its name is designed for people planning to retire around 2060. Someone expecting to retire around 2035 would look at a 2035 fund. Fund families usually offer a series of funds with different target years.

## The glide path

The heart of a target-date fund is its **glide path**, the planned schedule for changing its mix of investments over time.

A hypothetical example of how one glide path might look:

| Years until target date | Stocks | Bonds and cash |
|---|---|---|
| 40 | 90% | 10% |
| 20 | 75% | 25% |
| 0 (target year) | 50% | 50% |
| 10 years after | 35% | 65% |

Early on, the fund holds mostly stocks, which have historically offered higher long-term growth but larger swings. As the date gets closer, it shifts toward bonds and cash, which generally fluctuate less. The idea is that a younger investor has time to recover from market drops, while someone near retirement has less time to wait.

The fund handles the shifting and **rebalancing** automatically, so the investor doesn't have to.

## "To" versus "through" glide paths

Glide paths differ in an important way:

- A **"to"** glide path reaches its most conservative mix at the target date and then stays there.
- A **"through"** glide path keeps adjusting for years after the target date, assuming the investor will keep money invested well into retirement.

Two funds with the same year in their names can therefore hold quite different mixes, especially near and after the target date.

## Why people use them

- **Simplicity.** One fund provides a diversified portfolio across many asset types.
- **Automatic adjustments.** The mix changes with age without any action from the investor.
- **Discipline.** Automatic rebalancing avoids the temptation to chase whatever has done well recently.

## Things to understand before relying on one

**No guarantees.** A target-date fund can lose money, including near or after its target date. Reaching the target year does not mean the fund guarantees enough income for retirement.

**Different funds, different risk.** Fund companies design their glide paths differently. At the same target date, one fund might hold far more stocks than another. The prospectus explains the glide path, usually with a chart.

**Costs vary.** Target-date funds often invest in other funds, and fees range from quite low (for index-based versions) to relatively high. Check the total expense ratio.

**One-size-fits-many.** The fund assumes your date is the only thing that matters. It doesn't know your other savings, your spouse's accounts, your pension, or your comfort with risk. Holding a target-date fund alongside several other funds can also undo its careful balance, leaving a portfolio with a different mix than intended.

## Key takeaways
- A target-date fund is an all-in-one portfolio designed around a target year, usually a retirement year.
- Its glide path shifts the mix from mostly stocks toward more bonds and cash over time.
- "To" glide paths stop adjusting at the target date; "through" glide paths keep adjusting afterward.
- Funds with the same target year can have very different risk levels and costs.
- Target-date funds can lose money, including near and after the target date.
