A **money market fund** is a type of mutual fund that invests in very short-term, high-quality debt, such as U.S. Treasury bills and other short-term IOUs from governments, banks, and corporations. Money market funds are designed to be low risk and easy to access. Many investors use them as a place to hold cash, and brokerage accounts often use one as the default spot for uninvested money.

## How they work

Money market funds lend money for short periods, often days or weeks, to borrowers considered very creditworthy. The interest they earn is passed to shareholders as dividends, usually daily or monthly. Because the loans are so short, the fund's yield tends to follow short-term interest rates: it rises when rates rise and falls when rates fall.

A hypothetical example: if a money market fund yields 3% a year, $10,000 invested would earn about $300 in a year, assuming the yield stayed the same. In practice, the yield changes as interest rates change.

## The stable $1.00 share price

Most money market funds sold to individual investors, and all government money market funds, aim to keep their share price, or **net asset value (NAV)**, fixed at **$1.00**. That means $500 buys 500 shares, and each share should be worth $1.00 when you sell. Your earnings show up as additional shares or cash payouts, not a rising share price.

Some money market funds used by institutions have a **floating NAV** that moves slightly with the value of their holdings.

## Types of money market funds

- **Government** money market funds invest almost entirely in cash, U.S. government securities, and deals backed by them. They are generally considered the lowest-risk type.
- **Prime** money market funds also buy short-term corporate debt, which can offer somewhat higher yields with somewhat more risk.
- **Tax-exempt (municipal)** money market funds buy short-term debt from state and local governments. Their income is often free from federal income tax, which may appeal to investors in higher tax brackets.

## Money market fund versus money market account

The names are confusingly similar:

- A **money market account** is a bank or credit union deposit account. Deposits at insured banks are covered by FDIC insurance (NCUA insurance at credit unions), up to the legal limits.
- A **money market fund** is an investment product. It is **not FDIC-insured** and is not guaranteed by any government agency.

If a money market fund is held at a brokerage, SIPC coverage may protect you if the brokerage firm itself fails, but SIPC does not protect against losses in the value of investments.

## Risks

Money market funds are among the lowest-risk investments, but they are not risk-free:

- **Breaking the buck.** In rare cases, losses on a fund's holdings can push a stable-NAV fund's value below $1.00. This has happened very rarely, but it has happened: the Reserve Primary Fund "broke the buck" in September 2008, during the financial crisis.
- **Inflation risk.** Yields can be low, and over long periods they may fail to keep up with inflation, shrinking purchasing power.
- **Low rates and fees.** When interest rates are very low, fund fees can eat up most of the income.
- **Opportunity cost.** Over long periods, money market funds have historically earned less than stocks and bonds.

## Common uses

People often use money market funds for emergency savings held at a brokerage, money set aside for a near-term purchase, or cash waiting to be invested. Their strength is stability and access, not long-term growth.

## Key takeaways
- Money market funds are mutual funds that invest in very short-term, high-quality debt.
- Most retail and government money market funds aim to keep a stable $1.00 share price.
- Yields follow short-term interest rates and change over time.
- A money market fund is not a bank money market account and is not FDIC-insured.
- They are low risk but can lose value in rare cases and may not keep pace with inflation.
