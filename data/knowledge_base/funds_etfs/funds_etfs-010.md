An index fund promises to follow an index, but it never matches it perfectly. The small gaps between a fund's returns and its benchmark's returns reveal how well the fund does its job. Two related ideas describe these gaps: **tracking difference** and **tracking error**. Understanding them helps you judge index funds and ETFs beyond just their expense ratios.

## Tracking difference vs. tracking error

**Tracking difference** is the simple gap between the fund's return and the index's return over a period.

A hypothetical example: over a year, an index returns 10.00% and a fund tracking it returns 9.90%. The tracking difference is -0.10%. For most index funds, tracking difference is slightly negative, because the fund has costs and the index does not.

**Tracking error** measures how **consistent** that gap is over time. Technically, it is the standard deviation of the differences between the fund's returns and the index's returns, usually measured over many days or months. A low tracking error means the fund follows the index closely and predictably. A high tracking error means the fund sometimes runs ahead, sometimes lags, and is harder to predict.

These are different things. A fund could lag its index by a steady 0.20% every year: a noticeable tracking difference, but a very low tracking error. Another fund might average close to the index but swing well above and below it: small average difference, high tracking error.

In everyday use, people often say "tracking error" to mean either idea, so it helps to check which one is being discussed.

## Why funds don't match their index exactly

- **Fees.** The expense ratio is the biggest and most predictable drag. An index has no costs; a real fund does.
- **Trading costs.** Buying and selling securities, especially when the index changes, costs money.
- **Cash drag.** Funds keep a little cash for redemptions and incoming money, and that cash does not earn the index's return.
- **Sampling.** Funds that hold a representative sample instead of every security will naturally drift a bit from the index.
- **Timing of dividends.** Indexes may assume dividends are reinvested immediately, while funds receive and reinvest them on a slight delay.
- **Securities lending.** Some funds lend out holdings for a fee, which can partly offset costs and nudge returns up.

## Why it matters

For an index fund, the goal is to deliver the index's return at the lowest possible cost. A fund with a low expense ratio but sloppy tracking may not actually serve investors better than one with a slightly higher fee and tight tracking. Looking at tracking difference over several years shows the **real** cost of owning the fund, since it captures fees plus everything else.

For ETFs, it is also worth remembering that the market price you pay can differ slightly from the fund's net asset value. That premium or discount is separate from tracking error but also affects your result.

## Tracking error in active funds

Tracking error is used for actively managed funds too, where a higher number is expected. An active manager deliberately makes different choices from the benchmark, trying to beat it. A very low tracking error in an active fund may suggest it holds something close to the index, which is worth noticing if it charges active-management fees (a pattern sometimes called "closet indexing").

## Where to find the numbers

Some fund companies report tracking difference or tracking error on fact sheets or websites. You can also estimate tracking difference yourself by comparing the fund's annual returns with its benchmark's returns, both usually shown in the prospectus and fact sheet.

## Key takeaways
- Tracking difference is the gap between a fund's return and its index's return over a period.
- Tracking error measures how consistent that gap is over time.
- Fees, trading costs, cash holdings, sampling, and dividend timing cause funds to drift from their index.
- Tracking difference over several years shows an index fund's true all-in cost.
- In active funds, a very low tracking error may signal index-like holdings at active-fund prices.
