An **exchange-traded fund**, or ETF, is a fund that holds a basket of investments, such as stocks or bonds, and trades on a stock exchange like an individual stock. Buying one share of an ETF gives you a small slice of everything the fund owns. ETFs combine the diversification of a fund with the ability to buy and sell throughout the trading day.

## How an ETF works

Like a mutual fund, an ETF pools money from many investors into a portfolio managed by an investment adviser. Most ETFs track an index, such as a broad stock market index or a bond index, though a growing number are actively managed.

The key difference is how you buy and sell. ETF shares are listed on an exchange. You trade them with other investors through a brokerage account, at whatever the market price is at that moment, just like a stock. That means:

- The price changes throughout the day.
- You can use the same order types as with stocks, such as market and limit orders.
- You pay the market price, which may include a small **bid-ask spread** (the gap between what buyers offer and sellers accept).

## Market price versus NAV

Every ETF has a **net asset value (NAV)**, the per-share value of its holdings. Because ETF shares trade on the market, their price can drift slightly above (a **premium**) or below (a **discount**) the NAV.

A process involving large financial firms, called **authorized participants**, helps keep the two close. When an ETF trades above its NAV, these firms can deliver the underlying securities to the fund in exchange for new ETF shares and sell them, which pushes the price down. When it trades below NAV, they can do the reverse. For large, heavily traded ETFs holding easily traded securities, the gap is usually tiny. For ETFs that hold harder-to-trade assets, or during market stress, it can be larger.

A hypothetical example: an ETF's holdings are worth $50.00 per share. During the day it might trade at $50.02 or $49.98. Those small differences are normal.

## Why investors use ETFs

- **Diversification.** A single ETF can hold hundreds or thousands of securities.
- **Low minimums.** You can often start with the price of one share, or less if your broker offers fractional shares.
- **Trading flexibility.** You can buy or sell anytime the market is open.
- **Transparency.** Many ETFs publish their holdings daily.
- **Tax efficiency.** Because of how shares are created and redeemed "in kind," many ETFs distribute fewer capital gains than comparable mutual funds, which can mean lower taxes in a taxable account.
- **Often low costs.** Many index ETFs have low expense ratios, though costs vary widely.

## Risks and things to watch

An ETF is only as diversified, and as risky, as what it holds. A broad stock market ETF behaves very differently from one focused on a single industry, a single country, or even a single stock.

Some exchange-traded products are complex. **Leveraged** and **inverse** ETFs aim to deliver multiples or opposites of an index's return, usually over a single day. Over longer periods their results can differ greatly from what investors expect, and regulators have warned that they are generally not designed for buy-and-hold investors. Some products that trade on exchanges, such as exchange-traded notes, are not funds at all and carry different risks, including the credit risk of the issuer.

The ease of trading can also be a drawback: frequent buying and selling can increase costs and encourage reacting to short-term market moves.

Like mutual funds, ETFs are not FDIC-insured and can lose value.

## Key takeaways
- An ETF is a fund that holds a basket of investments and trades on an exchange like a stock.
- ETF prices change during the day and can differ slightly from the fund's net asset value.
- ETFs typically offer diversification, low minimums, trading flexibility, and tax efficiency.
- Risk depends on what the ETF holds; narrow, leveraged, and inverse products can be much riskier.
- ETFs are not FDIC-insured and can lose money.
