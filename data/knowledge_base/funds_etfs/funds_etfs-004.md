An **index fund** is a mutual fund or ETF that tries to match the performance of a market index rather than beat it. Instead of hiring managers to pick winning stocks, the fund simply holds the securities in its chosen index. This straightforward approach has made index funds one of the most popular ways for everyday investors to build a diversified portfolio at low cost.

## What is a market index?

A **market index** is a list of securities that represents a slice of the market, along with rules for how much weight each one gets. Some indexes cover large U.S. companies, others the entire U.S. stock market, small companies, international stocks, or bonds. Well-known examples include the S&P 500 and the Russell 2000 (named here as examples, not recommendations).

An index is just a measurement. You cannot invest in one directly. An index fund gives you a practical way to own what the index measures.

## How index funds hold their investments

Index funds use one of two broad approaches:

- **Full replication:** buy every security in the index in the same proportions.
- **Sampling:** buy a representative subset when the index has thousands of securities, some of which are hard to trade. Bond index funds commonly do this.

Most stock indexes are **market-cap weighted**, meaning larger companies make up a larger portion. A few are **price-weighted** or **equal-weighted**. The weighting method shapes how the fund behaves; in a cap-weighted fund, the biggest companies drive much of the return.

When the index changes, for example when a company is added or removed, the fund adjusts its holdings to match.

## Why index funds tend to be low cost

Because the fund follows a published list, it does not need a large research team, and it trades relatively infrequently. That usually means:

- **Lower expense ratios** than actively managed funds.
- **Lower trading costs** inside the fund.
- **Fewer taxable capital gains**, because the fund sells less often.

Costs matter because they come straight out of returns. A hypothetical example: if two funds hold nearly the same stocks and earn the same 7% before costs, the one charging 0.05% a year keeps more of that return than one charging 1.00%. Over decades, that gap compounds into a large difference.

## Passive versus active

An **actively managed** fund tries to outperform a benchmark by choosing which securities to buy and sell. Some succeed in a given year, but studies over long periods have consistently found that most active funds trail their benchmark index after fees, and it is hard to identify in advance which ones will outperform.

Index investing accepts the market's return, minus a small cost, rather than trying to beat it. Supporters see this as a sensible way to avoid paying for performance that often does not materialize.

## What index funds don't do

- **They don't avoid losses.** An index fund falls when its index falls. A stock index fund can drop sharply in a bear market.
- **They don't pick quality.** The fund holds whatever the index holds, including companies that later struggle.
- **They aren't all alike.** Funds tracking the same index can differ in cost and in how closely they follow it. Some newer "index" products follow custom or narrow indexes, which can behave very differently from broad market indexes.
- **They aren't always perfectly matched.** Small gaps between a fund and its index are called **tracking error**.

## Key takeaways
- An index fund aims to match a market index, not beat it.
- Index funds may hold every security in the index or a representative sample.
- Their passive approach typically means lower costs and fewer taxable distributions.
- Over long periods, most actively managed funds have trailed their index after fees.
- Index funds still rise and fall with their markets and can lose money.
