**Concentration risk** is the danger that comes from having a large share of your money in a single investment, company, industry, or region. When one holding dominates a portfolio, its fortunes largely become your fortunes. If it does well, the results can be impressive. If it struggles, there may be little else in the portfolio to soften the blow.

## How concentration builds up

Concentration does not always come from a deliberate decision. Common ways it develops include:

- **Employer stock.** Employees may receive company shares through stock awards, options, purchase plans, or retirement plan matches. Over years, these can quietly become a large part of their savings.
- **A big winner.** A stock that has risen dramatically can grow to dominate a portfolio simply because its value has outpaced everything else.
- **Inheritance or gifts.** Receiving a large block of a single stock can create a concentrated position overnight.
- **Familiarity.** People tend to invest in what they know, such as a local company, a favorite brand, or their own industry.
- **Hidden overlap.** Several funds may all hold large positions in the same few companies, so a portfolio can be more concentrated than it looks.

## Why it matters

Individual companies are much more unpredictable than a broad market. A single business can be hurt by competition, lawsuits, accounting problems, regulatory changes, or simply a string of bad decisions. Even well-known, respected companies have lost most of their value, and some have failed entirely.

There is no official cutoff, but some financial firms point to a single stock making up more than about 10% of a portfolio as a level where concentration becomes a concern.

## A hypothetical example

Consider an employee with $200,000 in savings, $120,000 of which is in her employer's stock. If the company runs into trouble and its stock falls 60%, she loses $72,000, more than a third of her total savings. Worse, a struggling company may also cut jobs. Her paycheck and her investments are tied to the same source, so a single event could hurt both at once. This double exposure is a key reason employer stock deserves extra attention.

Had that same $120,000 been spread across a broad index fund, a single company's collapse would have had only a small effect on the total.

## Industry and geographic concentration

Concentration is not only about single stocks. A portfolio made up entirely of technology companies, or entirely of companies from one country, is exposed to risks that affect that whole group. Research on investor behavior has identified **inadequate diversification** and **familiarity bias**, the tendency to favor investments close to home or already familiar, as common patterns that can hurt results.

## Ways investors manage concentration

People reduce concentration in a variety of ways, and the right approach depends on individual circumstances:

- **Gradual selling** over time, which can spread out the tax impact of selling appreciated shares in a taxable account.
- **Directing new money elsewhere** so the concentrated position becomes a smaller share over time.
- **Charitable giving** of appreciated shares, which can have tax advantages.
- **Reviewing fund holdings** to spot overlap across funds.

Taxes, company stock plan rules, and trading restrictions for employees can all complicate these decisions, which is why many people with large concentrated positions seek help from a tax or financial professional.

## Key takeaways
- Concentration risk arises when one investment, industry, or region makes up a large share of a portfolio.
- It often builds up gradually through employer stock, big winners, inheritances, or overlapping funds.
- Employer stock can create double exposure, since both your job and your savings depend on the same company.
- Gradual selling, redirecting new savings, and reviewing overlap are common ways to reduce concentration.
