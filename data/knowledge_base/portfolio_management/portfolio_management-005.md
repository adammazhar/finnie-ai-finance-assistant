**Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT)** is a framework for building portfolios that was introduced by economist Harry Markowitz in 1952. His work later contributed to a Nobel Prize in economics in 1990, shared with William Sharpe and Merton Miller. The core insight is surprisingly simple: an investment is best understood not only on its own merits, but by how it changes the risk and return of your whole portfolio.

## The big idea: look at the whole portfolio

Before MPT, investors often picked investments one at a time, looking for the ones with the best prospects. Markowitz showed that what matters is how investments behave **together**.

An investment that looks risky on its own might actually reduce a portfolio's overall risk if it tends to rise when the other holdings fall. This is the idea of **correlation**, the degree to which investments move together. Combining investments that do not move in lockstep can lower a portfolio's ups and downs without necessarily giving up much expected return. This is why diversification is sometimes called "the only free lunch in investing."

## Risk and return as a trade-off

MPT measures two things for any portfolio:

- **Expected return:** the average return you might anticipate over time.
- **Risk:** usually measured as **volatility**, or how much returns bounce around their average. The common statistic for this is **standard deviation**.

The theory assumes investors prefer more return and less risk. Given a choice between two portfolios with the same expected return, a rational investor would pick the one with lower volatility.

## The efficient frontier

If you plotted every possible combination of a set of investments on a chart, with risk on one axis and expected return on the other, some combinations would clearly be better than others. The portfolios that offer the **highest expected return for each level of risk** form a curve called the **efficient frontier**.

A portfolio below the curve is "inefficient": you could get more return for the same risk, or the same return with less risk, by changing the mix. Where an investor chooses to sit along the frontier depends on how much risk they are willing to accept.

## A hypothetical illustration

Imagine two investments. Stock fund A has an expected return of 8% with high volatility. Bond fund B has an expected return of 4% with low volatility. If they do not move closely together, a mix such as 70% A and 30% B might have volatility noticeably lower than A alone while still offering an expected return well above B. These numbers are purely illustrative, but they show how mixing can improve the balance between risk and reward.

## Limits and criticisms

MPT is influential, but it has well-known weaknesses:

- **It depends on estimates.** Expected returns, volatilities, and correlations are based on history or forecasts, and small changes in these inputs can produce very different "optimal" portfolios.
- **Volatility is not the only risk.** Many people care most about permanent losses or running out of money, not about short-term price swings.
- **Market returns are not perfectly "normal."** Extreme events happen more often than simple models assume.
- **Correlations change.** Assets that usually move differently can fall together in a crisis.

Despite these limits, the central lessons of MPT, that diversification matters and that risk and return should be considered together, remain foundational to how many professionals and index-based investors think about portfolios.

## Key takeaways
- Modern Portfolio Theory, introduced by Harry Markowitz in 1952, evaluates investments by how they affect the whole portfolio.
- Combining investments that do not move in lockstep can reduce risk without necessarily sacrificing much return.
- The efficient frontier shows the best expected return available for each level of risk.
- MPT relies on estimates and simplifying assumptions, so its outputs are approximations rather than precise answers.
