**Rebalancing** is the process of bringing your portfolio back to the mix of investments you originally chose. Over time, some investments grow faster than others, and your portfolio quietly drifts away from its target. Rebalancing is how many investors keep their level of risk from changing without them noticing.

## How drift happens

Suppose you decide on a target of 60% stocks and 40% bonds, and you start with a hypothetical $10,000: $6,000 in stocks and $4,000 in bonds.

Over the next few years, stocks rise 50% while bonds rise 5%. Your stocks are now worth $9,000 and your bonds $4,200, for a total of $13,200. Stocks now make up about 68% of the portfolio instead of 60%.

That may feel like good news, and in a sense it is. But your portfolio is now riskier than the one you chose. If stocks fell sharply at this point, the loss would be larger than you originally signed up for.

## How rebalancing works

To return to 60/40 in the example above, the target amounts would be about $7,920 in stocks and $5,280 in bonds. There are a few ways to get there:

- **Sell and buy.** Sell about $1,080 of stocks and use the proceeds to buy bonds.
- **Direct new money.** Put new contributions into the asset class that is below its target until the mix is back in line. This avoids selling.
- **Redirect income.** Use dividends and interest from the overweight asset class to buy the underweight one.

Notice that rebalancing naturally means trimming what has done well and adding to what has lagged. That can feel uncomfortable, but it is a disciplined way to avoid letting recent performance dictate your risk level.

## When to rebalance

There is no single correct schedule. Common approaches include:

- **Calendar-based.** Checking once or twice a year, or on a set date such as a birthday.
- **Threshold-based.** Rebalancing only when an asset class drifts more than a chosen amount, such as 5 percentage points, from its target.
- **A combination.** Checking on a schedule but acting only if drift exceeds a threshold.

Rebalancing too often can mean extra trading costs and effort with little benefit. Rebalancing rarely can let risk drift far from your plan. Many investors settle somewhere in between.

## Costs and taxes to keep in mind

- **Taxes.** Selling investments that have gained value in a regular taxable brokerage account can trigger capital gains taxes. Rebalancing inside tax-advantaged accounts, such as a 401(k) or IRA, generally does not create a tax bill at the time of the trade. Using new contributions to rebalance can also reduce the need to sell.
- **Transaction costs.** Some accounts or funds charge fees for trades. Many brokers no longer charge commissions on stock and ETF trades, but it is worth checking.
- **Target date and balanced funds.** Some funds rebalance automatically inside the fund, which is one reason people choose them.

## Rebalancing versus changing your plan

Rebalancing restores a target you already chose. That is different from changing the target itself. It can make sense to revisit your target allocation when your goals, time horizon, or life circumstances change, such as approaching retirement. Rebalancing is about maintaining a plan, not reacting to headlines.

## Key takeaways
- Market movements cause a portfolio to drift away from its target mix, often making it riskier.
- Rebalancing restores the target by selling overweight assets, buying underweight ones, or directing new money.
- Common schedules are calendar-based, threshold-based, or a mix of both.
- Consider taxes and costs; rebalancing in tax-advantaged accounts or with new contributions can reduce them.
