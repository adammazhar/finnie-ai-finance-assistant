A **benchmark** is a standard you use to judge how an investment has performed. Saying a fund "returned 8% last year" means little on its own. Was that good? It depends on what similar investments did over the same period. A benchmark provides that point of comparison.

## What benchmarks usually are

Most benchmarks are **market indexes**. An index tracks the performance of a defined group, or "basket," of securities meant to represent a particular part of the market. Common examples include:

- **The S&P 500**, which tracks about 500 large U.S. companies.
- **Total U.S. stock market indexes**, which include companies of all sizes.
- **International stock indexes**, which track companies in developed and emerging markets outside the United States.
- **Bond indexes**, such as broad measures of the U.S. investment-grade bond market.

Index providers publish the rules for what goes into each index, so everyone can see what it measures.

## Why benchmarks matter

**They give context.** Suppose a hypothetical large U.S. stock fund returned 8% in a year when the S&P 500 returned 12%. The fund made money, but it lagged a relevant alternative by 4 percentage points. In a year when the index fell 15% and the fund fell 10%, the fund lost money but outperformed its benchmark.

**They help evaluate active management.** Actively managed funds charge fees in exchange for trying to beat the market. Comparing their results to a suitable benchmark shows whether that effort has paid off after costs.

**They support index investing.** An **index fund** aims to match a benchmark rather than beat it. For index funds, the key question is how closely the fund tracks its index. The gap between the two is called **tracking difference**.

## Choosing the right benchmark

A benchmark is only useful if it matches what the investment actually holds. Comparing apples to apples is essential:

- A small-company stock fund compared to the S&P 500 (a large-company index) may look unusually good or bad simply because small and large companies had different years.
- A bond fund compared to a stock index tells you very little.
- A portfolio that is 60% stocks and 40% bonds is better compared to a blended benchmark with a similar 60/40 mix than to a pure stock index.

Be alert when a performance presentation uses a benchmark that does not match the investment's strategy. A mismatched benchmark can make results look better than they really are.

## Fees and fair comparisons

Index returns typically do not reflect fees, since you cannot invest directly in an index. Real funds have expenses, so even an index fund will usually trail its benchmark slightly. When comparing, look at returns **after** fees, and use the same time periods for both the investment and the benchmark.

Short periods can be misleading. One strong or weak year may reflect luck or unusual conditions. Many investors look at several time periods, such as one, five, and ten years, and at how a fund did in both rising and falling markets.

## Your own personal benchmark

For individual investors, the most meaningful benchmark may be progress toward your own goals. Beating an index is less important than whether your savings are on track for retirement, a home, or education, with a level of risk you can live with.

## Key takeaways
- A benchmark is a standard, usually a market index, used to judge an investment's performance.
- The right benchmark matches the investment's holdings and strategy.
- Compare after-fee returns over the same time periods, and look at several periods rather than one.
- Beyond market indexes, progress toward personal goals is an important measure of success.
