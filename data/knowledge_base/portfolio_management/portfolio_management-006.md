The **three-fund portfolio** is a simple approach to investing that uses just three broad, low-cost index funds to cover a large part of the world's stock and bond markets. It has become popular with do-it-yourself investors, especially in the community inspired by Vanguard founder John Bogle, because it offers wide diversification with very little complexity.

## The three building blocks

A classic three-fund portfolio holds:

1. **A U.S. total stock market index fund.** This owns thousands of U.S. companies of all sizes, from the largest household names to small businesses.
2. **An international total stock market index fund.** This owns thousands of companies based outside the United States, often including both developed and emerging markets.
3. **A U.S. total bond market index fund.** This owns a broad mix of investment-grade bonds, such as U.S. Treasury, government agency, and corporate bonds.

An **index fund** is a mutual fund or exchange-traded fund (ETF) that aims to track the performance of a market index rather than trying to beat it. Because the fund is not paying analysts to pick investments, index funds often have low costs.

Several major fund companies offer funds in each of these categories, so the approach is not tied to any one provider.

## Why people like it

- **Broad diversification.** Three funds together can hold many thousands of securities across different countries, industries, and company sizes.
- **Low cost.** Broad index funds commonly have low expense ratios, and fewer funds can mean fewer decisions and transactions.
- **Easy to understand and maintain.** With only three pieces, it is simple to see your allocation and to rebalance.
- **Less temptation to tinker.** A simple structure can make it easier to stay the course rather than chase whatever is popular.

## Choosing the proportions

The three-fund portfolio is a structure, not a single fixed recipe. The investor decides how much goes into each fund, and that choice reflects time horizon and risk tolerance. Here are two purely hypothetical examples:

- **Growth-oriented:** 60% U.S. stocks, 30% international stocks, 10% bonds.
- **More conservative:** 30% U.S. stocks, 20% international stocks, 50% bonds.

With a hypothetical $10,000, the first example would mean $6,000, $3,000, and $1,000 in the three funds. More stocks generally means more growth potential and larger swings; more bonds generally means a steadier ride with lower expected long-term returns.

The split between U.S. and international stocks is also a choice. Some investors weight close to each region's share of global stock market value, while others hold more at home. There is no universally correct answer.

## Maintaining the portfolio

Over time, the three funds will grow at different rates and the mix will drift. Many three-fund investors **rebalance** periodically, such as once a year or when one fund drifts more than a set amount from its target, by redirecting new contributions or shifting money between funds.

## Trade-offs and variations

The approach is not perfect for everyone. It does not include some asset types, such as international bonds, real estate funds, or inflation-protected bonds, and some investors add a fourth fund for one of these. Others prefer an all-in-one fund, such as a target date fund, that holds a similar mix and rebalances automatically. A three-fund portfolio still carries market risk, and all three funds can fall at the same time.

## Key takeaways
- A three-fund portfolio combines U.S. stocks, international stocks, and U.S. bonds using broad index funds.
- Its appeal comes from wide diversification, low costs, and simplicity.
- The percentages in each fund are a personal choice based on time horizon and risk tolerance.
- The portfolio still needs periodic rebalancing and can still lose value in market downturns.
