**Asset allocation** is how you divide your investment money among broad categories called **asset classes**, most commonly stocks, bonds, and cash. It sounds simple, but it is one of the most important decisions in investing, because the mix largely determines how much your portfolio might grow and how bumpy the ride is likely to be.

## The main asset classes

- **Stocks** represent ownership in companies. Historically they have offered the highest long-term growth of the three, but their prices can swing sharply, and they can lose a large share of their value in a bad year.
- **Bonds** are loans to governments or companies that pay interest. They have generally been less volatile than stocks and have historically returned less over long periods. They can still lose value, for example when interest rates rise.
- **Cash and cash equivalents** include savings accounts, money market funds, and Treasury bills. They are the most stable, but their returns are usually the lowest and may not keep up with inflation over time.

Some investors also include other categories, such as real estate or commodities, but stocks, bonds, and cash are the core building blocks.

## Why the mix matters

Because the three asset classes behave differently, combining them changes the character of a portfolio. Consider two hypothetical portfolios of $10,000:

- **Portfolio 1:** 90% stocks, 10% bonds.
- **Portfolio 2:** 40% stocks, 60% bonds.

Suppose stocks fall 30% in a bad year while bonds hold steady. Portfolio 1 drops by about $2,700 (27%). Portfolio 2 drops by about $1,200 (12%). In a strong year for stocks, the pattern reverses, and Portfolio 1 would likely pull ahead. Neither mix is "right"; each reflects a different trade-off between growth potential and stability.

## Factors that shape an allocation

Two factors come up again and again:

**Time horizon.** This is how long you expect to keep the money invested before you need it. Someone saving for a goal 30 years away has time to recover from downturns and may be able to accept more stock-market ups and downs. Someone who needs the money in two years has far less time to recover from a drop.

**Risk tolerance.** This is your ability and willingness to see your investments lose value in pursuit of higher potential returns. Two people with the same time horizon can have very different comfort levels. An allocation you cannot stick with during a downturn may lead to selling at a bad time.

Other considerations include your financial situation, emergency savings, income stability, and the specific goals you are funding. Many people have different allocations for different goals, such as a conservative mix for a house down payment and a growth-oriented mix for retirement.

## Asset allocation and diversification work together

Asset allocation is about the split between asset classes. **Diversification** is about spreading money within each asset class, such as owning many stocks instead of a few. Both help manage risk, and they complement each other.

## Allocations drift over time

Once you choose a mix, market movements will gradually change it. If stocks rise faster than bonds, stocks will become a larger share of the portfolio than you originally planned. Periodically bringing the mix back to its target is called **rebalancing**. Many investors also revisit their allocation after major life changes or as a goal gets closer.

## Key takeaways
- Asset allocation is the split of your money among asset classes such as stocks, bonds, and cash.
- Stocks have historically offered more growth with more volatility; bonds and cash have offered more stability with lower returns.
- Time horizon and risk tolerance are the two biggest factors in choosing a mix.
- An allocation drifts as markets move, which is why investors periodically review and rebalance.
