Owning many investments only reduces risk if those investments do not all move the same way at the same time. The measure that captures how closely two investments move together is called **correlation**. Understanding it helps explain why some combinations of investments smooth out a portfolio and others barely help at all.

## What correlation measures

Correlation is expressed as a number between -1 and +1, called the **correlation coefficient**.

- **+1 (perfect positive correlation):** The two investments always move in the same direction, in lockstep. When one rises, so does the other.
- **0 (no correlation):** The movements are unrelated. Knowing what one did tells you nothing about the other.
- **-1 (perfect negative correlation):** The two always move in opposite directions.

Real investments almost never sit exactly at these extremes. Two large U.S. companies in the same industry might have a correlation around +0.7 or +0.8, for example, while stocks and some high-quality bonds have at times shown correlations near zero or even negative.

Correlation describes **direction**, not size. Two investments can be highly correlated even if one tends to move much more than the other.

## Why low correlation helps

Diversification works best when you combine investments with low or negative correlation. When one zigs, the other may zag, and the combined portfolio moves less than either piece alone.

Here is a simplified hypothetical example. Investment A and Investment B each have ups and downs:

| Year | A | B | 50/50 mix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | +20% | -5% | +7.5% |
| 2 | -10% | +10% | 0% |
| 3 | +15% | 0% | +7.5% |

Each investment alone had a rough year. Because they tended to move differently, the 50/50 mix never lost money in this example and had a much smoother path. If A and B had been highly correlated, both would have fallen in the same years, and mixing them would have offered little protection.

## Stocks and bonds: a changing relationship

For many years, U.S. stock and bond prices often moved in opposite directions, which made bonds a useful cushion during stock market declines. But correlations are not fixed. In some periods, particularly when inflation and interest rates rise quickly, stocks and bonds have fallen at the same time. This is a reminder that the relationships investors rely on can shift.

## Correlations can rise in a crisis

Another important pattern: during sharp market sell-offs, many types of investments tend to fall together. Correlations that looked low in calm times can jump when investors rush to sell. This is one reason diversification reduces risk but does not eliminate it.

## Practical ways correlation shows up

You do not need to calculate correlation coefficients to use the idea:

- **Owning more of the same thing adds little.** Ten technology stocks are likely to be highly correlated with each other. Adding an eleventh does less for diversification than adding something different.
- **Different asset classes behave differently.** Stocks, bonds, and cash respond to different economic forces, which is why asset allocation is a core diversification tool.
- **Overlapping funds can hide high correlation.** Two funds with different names may hold many of the same companies.

## Key takeaways
- Correlation measures how closely two investments move together, on a scale from -1 to +1.
- Combining investments with low or negative correlation can make a portfolio's overall path smoother.
- Correlations change over time, and stocks and bonds have sometimes fallen together.
- During market crises, correlations often rise, so diversification reduces but never removes risk.
