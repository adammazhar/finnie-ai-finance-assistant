A common pattern in long-term investing is that portfolios tend to hold more stocks when people are young and gradually shift toward more bonds and cash as they approach and enter retirement. This article explains the reasoning behind that pattern and some popular rules of thumb. These rules are simplified starting points for understanding the idea, not recommendations for any particular person.

## Why age and allocation are linked

The connection comes mainly from **time horizon**, the length of time before you need to use the money.

- **Longer horizons** give more time to recover from market declines. A 25-year-old saving for retirement may not touch that money for 40 years, so a bad year for stocks has decades to be made up.
- **Shorter horizons** leave less time to recover. Someone planning to start withdrawals in three years could be hurt badly if stocks fell sharply right before or just after retirement.

There is also the idea of **human capital**: a young worker's future earnings are a large "asset" that can keep adding to savings, which may make it easier to handle investment swings. As working years shrink, that cushion shrinks too.

## Popular rules of thumb

Several shortcuts have circulated for decades. They are best viewed as conversation starters.

- **"Age in bonds."** Hold a bond percentage roughly equal to your age. At 30, that means about 30% bonds and 70% stocks; at 60, about 60% bonds.
- **"110 minus age" or "120 minus age."** Subtract your age from 110 or 120 to get a stock percentage. At 30, the 120 version suggests about 90% stocks. These variations emerged partly because people are living longer and may need growth for a longer retirement.

Here is how the rules compare for a hypothetical 40-year-old:

| Rule | Stocks | Bonds |
|---|---|---|
| Age in bonds | 60% | 40% |
| 110 minus age | 70% | 30% |
| 120 minus age | 80% | 20% |

The spread between these results shows how much judgment is involved. None of them accounts for an individual's savings, income, other assets, pensions, health, or comfort with risk.

## Target date funds and glide paths

**Target date funds** package this age-based idea into a single fund. The fund's name includes a year, usually the approximate year the investor expects to retire, such as a "2055" fund. The fund automatically shifts from mostly stocks toward more bonds and cash as that year approaches.

The schedule for that shift is called the **glide path**. Glide paths differ among fund companies:

- A **"to"** glide path reaches its most conservative mix at the target date.
- A **"through"** glide path keeps adjusting for years after the target date.

Two funds with the same target year can hold quite different mixes, so reading how a specific fund's glide path works is important. Target date funds can still lose money, including near and after the target date.

## Why rules of thumb are only a start

Age is one input among many. Two people of the same age can reasonably hold very different allocations because of:

- **Risk tolerance:** how comfortable they are seeing their balance drop.
- **Other income:** a pension or annuity can act somewhat like a bond holding.
- **Goals:** money needed for a home purchase soon behaves differently from money meant for age 85.
- **Retirement length:** retirements can last 30 years or more, so some growth may still be needed after retiring.

## Key takeaways
- Allocations often become more conservative with age because time horizons shorten.
- Rules like "age in bonds" and "110 or 120 minus age" are simplified rules of thumb, not advice.
- Target date funds automate this shift through a glide path, which varies from fund to fund.
- Risk tolerance, other income, and personal goals matter as much as age.
