"Risk" can mean many things in investing, but when professionals try to put a number on it, three measures come up again and again: **volatility**, **drawdown**, and **beta**. Each captures a different aspect of how an investment behaves. None tells the whole story alone, but together they give a much clearer picture.

## Volatility and standard deviation

**Volatility** describes how much an investment's returns bounce around. An investment whose returns are +2%, +3%, +1%, +2% is low-volatility. One whose returns are +30%, -20%, +25%, -15% is high-volatility, even if both average out to similar numbers over time.

The most common way to measure volatility is **standard deviation**, a statistic that shows how far returns typically stray from their average. A higher standard deviation means a wider range of likely outcomes.

A rough rule of thumb: in a typical year, returns often fall within one standard deviation of the average. Suppose a hypothetical fund has an average annual return of 8% and a standard deviation of 15%. In many years its return might land somewhere between -7% and +23%, and some years would fall outside even that wide range.

Volatility treats upside and downside surprises the same, which is one of its limitations. Most investors worry far more about the downside.

## Drawdown

**Drawdown** measures the decline from a peak value to a subsequent low point. **Maximum drawdown** is the largest such peak-to-trough decline over a given period.

For example, if a hypothetical portfolio grows to $10,000, then falls to $6,500 before recovering, its drawdown was 35%. This measure speaks directly to the question many investors care about: "How bad could it get?"

Drawdowns are a normal part of stock investing. Studies of the U.S. stock market have found that meaningful declines within a single year are common, and larger declines of 20% or more have happened in a sizable minority of years. Knowing this in advance can make downturns feel less like a surprise.

One important point: recovering from a drawdown requires a larger percentage gain than the loss. A 50% drop from $10,000 to $5,000 needs a 100% gain to get back to $10,000.

## Beta

**Beta** measures how sensitive an investment is to movements in the overall market, usually represented by a benchmark index such as the S&P 500.

- A **beta of 1** means the investment has historically tended to move roughly in line with the market.
- A **beta above 1** means it has tended to move more than the market. A stock with a beta of 1.5 has historically moved about 15% when the market moved 10%.
- A **beta below 1** means it has tended to move less than the market.
- A **negative beta**, which is rare, means it has tended to move opposite the market.

Beta is based on past data and describes sensitivity to broad market swings. It does not capture risks specific to one company, such as a failed product.

## Using the measures together

Each measure answers a different question:

| Measure | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| Standard deviation | How much do returns usually vary? |
| Maximum drawdown | What was the worst decline from a peak? |
| Beta | How much does it move relative to the market? |

All three are **backward-looking**. They describe what happened, which can help set expectations, but the future may look different.

## Key takeaways
- Volatility, usually measured by standard deviation, shows how widely returns vary around their average.
- Drawdown measures peak-to-trough declines; maximum drawdown shows the worst historical decline.
- Beta shows how sensitive an investment has been to overall market movements.
- These measures are based on past data and work best when used together, not in isolation.
