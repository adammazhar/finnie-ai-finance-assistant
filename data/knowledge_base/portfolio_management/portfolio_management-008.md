Investors in nearly every country tend to hold far more of their own country's stocks than that country's share of the global market would suggest. This tendency is called **home country bias** or **home bias**. It is closely related to **familiarity bias**, the preference for investments that feel familiar, which research on investor behavior has identified as a common pattern that can reduce diversification.

## What home bias looks like

The world's stock markets are spread across many countries. The United States makes up a large share of global stock market value, but a significant portion of the world's publicly traded companies are based elsewhere, in Europe, Japan, the rest of Asia, Canada, and emerging markets.

A U.S. investor who owns only U.S. stocks is making a large, often unintentional, bet on a single economy. The same is true for a Canadian, British, or Australian investor holding only domestic stocks, and for smaller markets the concentration can be even more extreme.

## Why investors favor home

- **Familiarity.** People know local brands and feel they understand them better.
- **Convenience.** Domestic stocks and funds have historically been easier to find and buy.
- **Currency.** Domestic investments avoid exchange-rate swings, and expenses in retirement are usually in the home currency.
- **Recent performance.** When the home market has done well for many years, it can feel unnecessary to look elsewhere.

Some of these reasons have merit. Currency matching, for example, is a legitimate consideration. The concern arises when a portfolio ends up highly concentrated in one country without a deliberate decision.

## The case for international diversification

The main reasons investors hold international stocks are **diversification** and access to **growth** in other economies.

Different countries go through different economic cycles, policies, and industry mixes. Over history, leadership has shifted: there have been long stretches when U.S. stocks outperformed international stocks, and other long stretches when the reverse was true. No one reliably knows which will lead next. Owning both means you are less dependent on any single country's results.

Consider a hypothetical investor with $10,000 who holds 70% U.S. and 30% international stocks. If one region has a poor decade while the other does well, the portfolio's results will fall somewhere in between rather than depending entirely on one outcome.

## Risks specific to international investing

International investing adds its own risks worth understanding:

- **Currency risk.** Returns can rise or fall when exchange rates change, independent of how the companies perform.
- **Political and economic risk.** Some countries have less stable governments or economies.
- **Different rules and disclosure.** Accounting standards, investor protections, and legal remedies may differ from those at home.
- **Liquidity and costs.** Some foreign markets trade less actively and can be more expensive to access.

Many individual investors get international exposure through U.S.-registered mutual funds or ETFs, which can provide broad diversification across many countries and are subject to U.S. regulations.

## How much is "enough"?

There is no single correct international allocation. Some investors aim to match global market weights; others hold a smaller international portion, such as 20% to 40% of their stocks, because of currency, cost, or comfort. The key is making a deliberate choice rather than defaulting to home bias without realizing it.

## Key takeaways
- Home bias is the tendency to overweight your own country's investments relative to its share of global markets.
- It stems from familiarity, convenience, currency, and recent performance.
- International diversification reduces dependence on a single economy, since regional leadership has shifted over time.
- Foreign investing adds currency, political, and regulatory risks, which broad funds can help manage.
