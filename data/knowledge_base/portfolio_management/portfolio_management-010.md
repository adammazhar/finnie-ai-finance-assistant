Comparing investments by return alone can be misleading. An investment that earned 12% while swinging wildly is not necessarily better than one that earned 10% with a smooth ride. **Risk-adjusted return** measures try to account for how much risk was taken to earn a return. The best known of these is the **Sharpe ratio**, developed by economist William Sharpe.

## The formula

The Sharpe ratio asks: how much extra return did an investment earn for each unit of risk it took?

**Sharpe ratio = (Investment return − Risk-free rate) ÷ Standard deviation of returns**

Each piece has a role:

- **Investment return** is the average return over the period being measured.
- **Risk-free rate** is the return you could have earned with essentially no risk. It is commonly represented by the yield on short-term U.S. Treasury bills, which are backed by the U.S. government and mature in a year or less.
- **Excess return** is the investment return minus the risk-free rate. It shows the reward for taking risk rather than parking money in T-bills.
- **Standard deviation** measures volatility, or how much returns bounced around their average.

## A hypothetical example

Suppose the risk-free rate is 3%, and you are comparing two funds over the same period:

| | Fund A | Fund B |
|---|---|---|
| Average return | 12% | 9% |
| Standard deviation | 18% | 8% |
| Excess return | 9% | 6% |
| Sharpe ratio | 9 ÷ 18 = 0.50 | 6 ÷ 8 = 0.75 |

Fund A had the higher raw return, but Fund B earned more return per unit of volatility. By the Sharpe ratio, Fund B delivered better risk-adjusted performance during this period. All numbers here are hypothetical.

## How to interpret it

- **Higher is better,** all else equal. It means more reward for each unit of risk.
- **A negative Sharpe ratio** means the investment did worse than the risk-free rate, so the risk taken was not rewarded during that period.
- **Comparisons are most meaningful** between similar investments measured over the same time period using the same risk-free rate.

There is no universal threshold for a "good" Sharpe ratio, because results depend heavily on the time period and market conditions.

## Limitations

The Sharpe ratio is useful, but it has blind spots:

- **It is backward-looking.** A strong past Sharpe ratio does not mean the future will look the same.
- **It treats all volatility as bad.** Big upside surprises raise standard deviation just like big losses do, which can penalize investments with occasional large gains.
- **It assumes returns are fairly "normal."** Investments with rare but severe losses can look deceptively good until a bad event occurs.
- **The time period matters a lot.** A calm stretch can make almost anything look good.

Because of these issues, analysts sometimes use related measures. The **Sortino ratio**, for example, only counts downside volatility in the denominator.

## Why it matters for beginners

Even if you never calculate a Sharpe ratio, the idea behind it is valuable: returns always come with risk, and a fair comparison considers both. When you see an investment advertised for its high returns, a natural follow-up question is, "How much risk was taken to get there?"

## Key takeaways
- The Sharpe ratio measures excess return per unit of volatility: (return − risk-free rate) ÷ standard deviation.
- The risk-free rate is commonly the yield on short-term U.S. Treasury bills.
- Higher Sharpe ratios indicate better historical risk-adjusted performance, but comparisons work best between similar investments over the same period.
- It is backward-looking and treats upside and downside volatility alike, so it is one tool among many.
