Our brains rely on mental shortcuts to make decisions quickly. Most of the time they serve us well, but in investing they can quietly lead us astray. Two of the most common are **anchoring**, which gives too much weight to a particular number, and **recency bias**, which gives too much weight to what just happened.

## Anchoring: stuck on a number

**Anchoring** happens when a person fixes on an initial piece of information, the "anchor," and lets it shape later judgments even when it is no longer relevant.

In investing, common anchors include:

- **Your purchase price.** Many people judge an investment by whether it is above or below what they paid. But the market does not know or care what you paid. The more relevant question is what the investment is likely to do from here.
- **A past high.** A stock that once traded at $100 and now trades at $60 can look like a bargain simply because of the old high. The business may have changed in ways that make $60 a fair or even generous price.
- **Round numbers and forecasts.** A headline predicting that an index will hit a certain level can become a mental target that colors expectations.

### A hypothetical example

An investor buys shares at $80. The price falls to $50 after the company loses a major customer. He tells himself he will sell "once it gets back to $80." That $80 figure has become an anchor. It says nothing about the company's prospects today, yet it drives his decision. A more useful question is: "If I had cash instead of these shares, would I buy them at $50?"

## Recency bias: the recent past feels like the future

**Recency bias** is the tendency to assume that recent events will continue. After a few strong years, it feels natural to expect more of the same. After a sharp decline, it can feel like the losses will never stop.

Recency bias can show up as:

- **Chasing performance.** Buying the fund or sector that did best last year, often after much of its gain has already happened.
- **Abandoning a plan after a downturn.** Selling stocks after a decline because the recent pain feels permanent.
- **Underestimating rare events.** After a long calm stretch, investors may forget that sharp declines, or periods of high inflation, can and do happen.

Research on U.S. investor behavior has also noted that many people focus heavily on past returns while paying too little attention to costs. Past performance, especially over a short period, is a weak guide to future results.

## How the two biases work together

Anchoring and recency bias often reinforce each other. A recent price can become the new anchor. For example, a stock's price from last month might feel like its "real" value, making any move away from it seem like a mistake that must correct.

## Ways to reduce their influence

- **Zoom out.** Looking at long-term history, across many market cycles, puts recent events in perspective.
- **Reframe decisions.** Ask whether you would make the same choice today with fresh cash, ignoring your purchase price.
- **Use rules set in advance.** A written asset allocation and rebalancing schedule reduce the pull of recent headlines.
- **Look beyond one period.** When reviewing funds, compare several time frames and consider fees, not just last year's return.
- **Seek disconfirming information.** Deliberately look for reasons your view could be wrong.

## Key takeaways
- Anchoring means fixating on a reference number, such as a purchase price or past high, that may no longer matter.
- Recency bias means expecting recent trends, good or bad, to continue.
- Both can lead to holding losers, chasing past performance, or abandoning a sound plan.
- Reframing decisions, zooming out, and following pre-set rules can reduce their pull.
