Markets rise and fall, sometimes sharply. When your account balance drops by thousands of dollars in a few weeks, it is natural to feel anxious and want to do something. Understanding why volatility happens, and preparing for it ahead of time, can make these periods easier to live through and less likely to trigger costly decisions.

## Volatility is normal

**Volatility** is the degree to which prices move up and down. For stocks, meaningful swings are part of the package, not a sign that something is broken.

Studies of the U.S. stock market over several decades have found that a decline of more than 10% from a peak at some point during a calendar year is common, and that drops of 20% or more have occurred in a meaningful share of years. Historically, the U.S. market has eventually recovered from every major decline and gone on to new highs, though some recoveries took years, and past patterns do not guarantee future ones.

Knowing that declines are a regular feature of investing can make them feel less like an emergency.

## Why reacting can be costly

The instinct during a sharp drop is often to sell and wait for calmer times. The problem is that calm usually returns only after prices have already recovered. Investors who sell during a decline turn a temporary paper loss into a permanent one, and then face the hard decision of when to get back in.

Consider a hypothetical $50,000 portfolio that falls 20% to $40,000. If the investor holds on and the market later recovers, the balance can return to where it was. If the investor sells at $40,000 and waits until the market "feels safe," they may buy back in at higher prices, locking in a loss that holding would have avoided.

## Preparing before the storm

The best time to plan for volatility is when markets are calm.

- **Build an emergency fund.** Having several months of expenses in cash means a job loss or surprise bill will not force you to sell investments at a low point.
- **Match your mix to your timeline.** Money needed within a few years is generally less suited to stocks. Longer-term money has more time to recover.
- **Know your risk tolerance.** An asset allocation you can stick with in a downturn is more valuable than one that looks optimal on paper.
- **Write down your plan.** A short statement of your goals, allocation, and rebalancing rules gives you something to return to when emotions run high.

## Tactics during a downturn

When volatility hits, some practical habits can help:

- **Check less often.** Watching daily moves can magnify anxiety without providing useful information.
- **Zoom out.** Looking at a long-term chart puts a recent drop into perspective.
- **Revisit your plan, not the headlines.** Ask whether your goals or time horizon have actually changed. If not, the plan may still fit.
- **Keep contributing if you can.** Regular investments made during declines buy more shares at lower prices.
- **Rebalance.** A drop in stocks may bring your allocation below target, and rebalancing restores the intended mix.
- **Watch for scams.** Fraudsters often exploit fear during turbulent markets with promises of safety or quick recovery.

## If retirement is near

Downturns are harder when you need the money soon. People close to or in retirement often keep a portion of their savings in bonds and cash to cover near-term spending, so they are less likely to need to sell stocks during a decline. Reviewing that cushion before a downturn is easier than building it during one.

## Key takeaways
- Volatility, including declines of 10% to 20% or more, is a normal part of stock investing.
- Selling during a decline can turn a temporary loss into a permanent one.
- An emergency fund, a suitable asset allocation, and a written plan are the best preparation.
- During turbulence, checking less often, zooming out, and following your plan can prevent rash decisions.
