**Risk tolerance** is your ability and willingness to see the value of your investments fall, possibly by a lot, in exchange for the chance of higher returns. It is one of the most personal factors in investing. Two people with the same income and age can have very different risk tolerances, and both can be perfectly reasonable.

## Willingness versus ability

Risk tolerance has two sides that do not always agree.

**Willingness to take risk** is emotional. It is about how you feel when your account balance drops. Some people can watch a 30% decline and shrug; others lose sleep over a 5% dip.

**Ability to take risk**, sometimes called **risk capacity**, is financial. It depends on your situation:

- **Time horizon:** how long until you need the money. Money needed next year has little time to recover from a fall.
- **Income stability:** a steady paycheck can make it easier to ride out market downturns without selling.
- **Emergency savings:** a cash cushion reduces the chance you will be forced to sell investments at a bad moment.
- **Other resources:** a pension, other savings, or a partner's income can add flexibility.

Someone might be very willing to take risk but have little capacity, such as a person who loves the thrill of volatile stocks but needs the money for a down payment in 18 months. Another person might have plenty of capacity but little willingness. Thinking about both helps paint a realistic picture.

## Why it matters

An investment mix that does not fit your risk tolerance can be hard to stick with. The biggest danger is not the market decline itself but how people react to it. An investor who takes on more risk than they can stomach may panic and sell near the bottom, locking in losses and potentially missing the recovery.

On the other side, taking too little risk has a cost too. Holding mostly cash for a goal decades away may feel safe, but low returns might not keep up with inflation or grow enough to reach the goal.

## Thinking in dollars, not just percentages

Percentages can feel abstract. Translating them into dollars often makes the trade-off clearer. Suppose you have a hypothetical $20,000 invested:

| Decline | Dollar loss | Remaining |
|---|---|---|
| 10% | $2,000 | $18,000 |
| 25% | $5,000 | $15,000 |
| 40% | $8,000 | $12,000 |

Declines of 25% or more have happened in stock markets several times in modern history. Asking "How would I feel, and what would I do, if my $20,000 became $12,000 for a year or two?" can be more revealing than any abstract label.

## Ways to gauge your risk tolerance

- **Questionnaires.** Many brokerages and financial websites offer short quizzes that ask about goals, time horizon, and reactions to hypothetical losses. They are a helpful starting point, though not a precise measurement.
- **Past behavior.** How did you react during previous market drops? Actions often say more than intentions.
- **Goal by goal.** Risk tolerance can differ by goal. You might accept more risk for retirement money decades away than for a car you plan to buy soon.

## Risk tolerance can change

Your risk tolerance is not fixed. It can shift with age, experience, wealth, family responsibilities, and even recent market events. People often feel braver after markets have risen and more cautious after a crash, which is a reason to set a plan during calm times and revisit it periodically rather than in the middle of turmoil.

## Key takeaways
- Risk tolerance combines your emotional willingness and your financial ability to handle losses.
- Time horizon, income stability, and emergency savings strongly affect your ability to take risk.
- A mix that exceeds your tolerance can lead to panic selling; a mix far below it may not reach your goals.
- Thinking in dollar terms and reviewing past behavior can help you understand your own tolerance.
