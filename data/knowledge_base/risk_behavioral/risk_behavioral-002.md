Imagine finding $100 on the street, then later losing a $100 bill from your pocket. Financially, you end up exactly where you started. Emotionally, most people would not feel neutral: the sting of the loss tends to outweigh the pleasure of the gain. This tendency is called **loss aversion**, and it is one of the best-documented patterns in behavioral finance.

## Where the idea comes from

Psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky studied how people make decisions under uncertainty. Their research, known as **prospect theory**, found that people evaluate outcomes as gains or losses relative to a reference point, and that losses carry more emotional weight than equal-sized gains. Studies have often estimated that a loss feels roughly twice as painful as a comparable gain feels good, though the exact amount varies from person to person and situation to situation.

Kahneman later received a Nobel Prize in economics, in part for this work, which helped launch the field of behavioral economics.

## How loss aversion shows up in investing

**Holding losers too long.** Selling an investment at a loss makes the loss feel real and final. Many investors hang on, hoping to "get back to even," even when their original reasons for owning it no longer apply.

**Selling winners too soon.** Locking in a gain feels good, so investors may sell rising investments early. The combination of holding losers and selling winners is called the **disposition effect**, and research on U.S. investors has identified it as a common behavior that can hurt performance.

**Panic selling in downturns.** Watching a balance fall can be so uncomfortable that some investors sell simply to make the pain stop, often near market lows.

**Being too conservative.** Fear of any loss can lead people to keep long-term money in cash for decades, where it may lose purchasing power to inflation. Avoiding visible losses can mean accepting a less visible one.

## A hypothetical example

An investor buys shares at $50. The price falls to $35. She decides to wait until it returns to $50 before selling, not because she has new information, but because selling at $35 would mean admitting a loss. Meanwhile, her $50 purchase price has become an emotional anchor that has no bearing on the company's future. The more useful question is whether she would buy the investment today at $35, given what she knows now.

## Ways investors counter loss aversion

No one can switch off loss aversion, but some habits can reduce its influence:

- **Write down a plan in advance.** Deciding on an asset allocation and rebalancing rules during calm periods gives you something to follow when emotions run high.
- **Check less often.** Frequent checking exposes you to more small losses, and each one registers emotionally. Longer-term investors often find less frequent reviews easier.
- **Focus on the whole portfolio.** A single losing holding feels different when viewed as part of a diversified mix.
- **Reframe the decision.** Ask whether you would buy an investment today, rather than how it has done since you bought it.
- **Know the tax angle.** In taxable accounts, realized losses can offset capital gains and, for 2026, up to $3,000 per year of ordinary income, with the rest carried forward. Tax rules and limits change, so current IRS guidance is the authority.

## Key takeaways
- Loss aversion is the tendency to feel losses more strongly than equal gains.
- It can lead investors to hold losers too long, sell winners too soon, and panic in downturns.
- Excessive fear of losses can also mean taking too little risk for long-term goals.
- A written plan, less frequent checking, and a whole-portfolio view can reduce its influence.
