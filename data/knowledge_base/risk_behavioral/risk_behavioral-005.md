Most drivers believe they are better than average, which is statistically impossible. Investors show the same pattern. **Overconfidence** is the tendency to overestimate our own knowledge, skill, and ability to predict outcomes. In investing, one of its most common and costly consequences is **overtrading**: buying and selling far more often than is helpful.

## Forms of overconfidence

Overconfidence shows up in several ways:

- **Overestimating skill.** Believing you can reliably pick winning stocks or predict market moves better than others.
- **Overestimating knowledge.** Feeling well-informed after reading a few articles or watching videos, even when professionals with far more resources disagree.
- **The illusion of control.** Believing that frequent monitoring and action give you more control over outcomes than they actually do.
- **Self-attribution.** Crediting wins to skill and blaming losses on bad luck, which prevents learning from mistakes.

Easy access to online information can strengthen these feelings. Having more data can make people feel more certain without actually making their predictions more accurate.

## Why overtrading hurts

Research on individual investors has consistently found that those who trade most frequently tend to earn lower returns, on average, than those who trade less. An SEC-commissioned study of U.S. investor behavior listed **active trading** first among the behaviors that can undermine performance. Several forces explain why:

**Costs add up.** Even without commissions, trades can involve bid-ask spreads and other costs. Each one is small, but frequent trading multiplies them.

**Taxes.** In a taxable account, selling investments held one year or less produces short-term capital gains, which are taxed at ordinary income rates. Investments sold after being held more than one year may qualify for lower long-term capital gains rates. Frequent trading tends to generate more short-term gains.

**Competing against professionals.** Every trade has someone on the other side, often a professional firm with sophisticated tools and information. Consistently outsmarting them is very hard.

**More chances for emotional decisions.** Each trade is another opportunity to act on fear, excitement, or a hunch.

## A hypothetical comparison

Two investors each start with $10,000. Investor A buys a broad index fund and holds it. Investor B trades actively, making dozens of trades a year. Suppose the market returns 8% in a year. Investor A earns close to that, minus a small fund expense. Investor B also has market exposure but gives up, say, 2% to trading costs and taxes and makes a few poorly timed trades. Investor B could easily end the year with noticeably less, despite much more effort. These numbers are illustrative, not predictions.

## Day trading: overconfidence at full speed

**Day trading** means buying and selling securities within the same day, trying to profit from small price moves. Regulators warn that it is highly risky and that many day traders lose money, particularly when using borrowed money (**margin**), which can magnify losses beyond the original investment. Day trading can also involve special account rules and requirements.

## Habits that counter overconfidence

- **Keep a decision journal.** Write down why you are making each trade and review the results honestly later.
- **Compare to a benchmark.** Measure your results against a simple index fund over the same period, after costs.
- **Create friction.** A waiting period before trading, or checking accounts less often, can reduce impulsive moves.
- **Separate "play money."** Some investors keep speculation to a small, fixed portion, leaving the core of their savings in a long-term plan.

## Key takeaways
- Overconfidence is the tendency to overestimate one's knowledge and ability to predict markets.
- Frequent traders have, on average, tended to earn lower returns than less active investors.
- Costs, short-term capital gains taxes, and emotional decisions all erode the results of overtrading.
- Decision journals, benchmark comparisons, and built-in waiting periods can help keep overconfidence in check.
